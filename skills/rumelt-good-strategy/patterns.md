# Patterns — Good Strategy / Bad Strategy

## The Kernel
**When to use**: Building or evaluating any strategy, at any scale.
**How**: State (1) a diagnosis of the challenge, (2) a guiding policy for addressing it, (3) coherent actions that carry out the policy. Missing or misshapen on any one element means it isn't a real strategy yet.
**Trade-offs**: Forces hard prioritization work up front; resists the temptation to skip straight to action or to stop at a vague policy statement. (Ch 5)

## Detecting Bad Strategy
**When to use**: Reviewing any existing "strategic plan" before trying to fix it.
**How**: Scan for the four hallmarks — fluff, failure to name the challenge, goals mistaken for strategy, bad (dog's-dinner or blue-sky) objectives. Any one present is disqualifying.
**Trade-offs**: Faster and more reliable than trying to build good strategy from scratch; doesn't by itself generate a fix. (Ch 3)

## Strategy Identification (Reverse-Engineering a Rival)
**When to use**: Understanding why a competitor outperforms its industry when the stated explanation doesn't add up.
**How**: List each policy that differs from the industry norm; for each, name the specific customer/situation it targets; find what target unifies the distinctive policies.
**Trade-offs**: Labor-intensive, requires real data; the official narrative (company's own or analysts') often misses the real mechanism. (Ch 10)

## Leverage: Anticipation + Pivot Point + Concentration
**When to use**: Deciding where to apply scarce resources for maximum effect.
**How**: Identify what's reliably predictable about rivals' downstream behavior; find the imbalance where a small push unbalances further; commit resources there instead of spreading them.
**Trade-offs**: Requires genuine domain expertise to distinguish real anticipation from wishful forecasting. (Ch 6)

## Absorbing Ambiguity into a Proximate Objective
**When to use**: A team is paralyzed by an ill-structured problem with no clear specification.
**How**: Issue a simplified, workable specification (even if not literally "the truth") that the team can actually design against, rather than waiting for certainty.
**Trade-offs**: Risk of committing to a wrong simplification; mitigated by choosing one that fails gracefully if wrong. (Ch 7)

## Unsticking a Chain-Linked System
**When to use**: A multi-part organization isn't improving despite reasonable local management.
**How**: Identify bottlenecks in logical/temporal order; run sequential (not simultaneous) fully-committed campaigns on each; suspend local profit/blame measurement during each campaign.
**Trade-offs**: Requires a leader willing to absorb short-term losses and resist decentralized pressure for quick local wins. (Ch 8)

## Resources vs. Integration Trade-off
**When to use**: Deciding how much design/coordination effort a competitive situation warrants.
**How**: Given fixed resources, greater challenge needs tighter integration; given fixed challenge, better resources reduce the need for tight integration.
**Trade-offs**: Tight integration is fragile and inflexible — reserve it for situations that genuinely justify the cost. (Ch 9)

## The Four Fronts of Increasing Advantage
**When to use**: An advantage exists but its value is static (the "silver machine" problem).
**How**: Check all four — deepen (cost/value gap), broaden (extend the underlying skill to new markets), create demand (grow buyers for the scarce resource), strengthen isolation (patents, moving-target imitation resistance).
**Trade-offs**: Broadening via brand extension risks dilution; creating demand only pays off if you already hold the advantaged resource. (Ch 12)

## Reading a Wave of Change
**When to use**: An industry feels unusually turbulent.
**How**: Apply the five guideposts — rising fixed costs, deregulation, predictable forecasting biases, incumbent response structure, attractor states (plus accelerants/impediments).
**Trade-offs**: No guaranteed forecast; the goal is being 10% clearer than rivals, not certain. (Ch 13)

## Breaking Organizational Inertia
**When to use**: An org resists a needed strategic pivot.
**How**: For routine inertia — change perceptions/people/routines directly. For cultural inertia — simplify, fragment, triage, replace the alpha, then reintroduce coordination. For inertia by proxy — recognize it's rational customer stickiness, not organizational sloth, and expect a sudden response once protecting the old profit stream stops being worth it.
**Trade-offs**: Cultural change is slow and requires replacing people invested in obsolete methods. (Ch 14)

## Strategy as Hypothesis
**When to use**: A team demands a guaranteed-to-work strategy.
**How**: Reframe as an educated conjecture; test against known principles first, then design the cheapest real-world experiment to reveal whether it holds; treat results as new data for the next iteration.
**Trade-offs**: Requires comfort with genuine uncertainty and iterative revision rather than one-shot correctness. (Ch 16)

## Create-Destroy via a Panel of Experts
**When to use**: Checking whether your first strategic insight is actually sound.
**How**: Hold a small set of real, well-understood people in mind; mentally ask each "what's wrong with this? What would you do?" rather than applying an abstract framework.
**Trade-offs**: Only as good as how well you actually know the panel members' real thinking, not a caricature of them. (Ch 17)

## Outside View vs. Inside View
**When to use**: Assessing risk in a decision that feels exceptional or unprecedented.
**How**: Deliberately look up the relevant historical base rate before trusting the feeling that "this case is different."
**Trade-offs**: Base rates can be imperfectly analogous; the discipline is checking them explicitly, not blindly deferring to them. (Ch 18)
