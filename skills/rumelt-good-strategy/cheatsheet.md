# Cheatsheet — Good Strategy / Bad Strategy

## Decision rules

**Is this actually a strategy?** Check for all three kernel elements: diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action. Missing any one → not yet a strategy, keep working. (Ch 5)

**Is this bad strategy?** If it contains fluff, no named challenge, goals-as-strategy, or dog's-dinner/blue-sky objectives → yes, regardless of polish or length. (Ch 3)

**Should we grow?** Only if growth is the *expected consequence* of a specific advantage you hold — never pursue growth as the goal itself. If the honest answer to "why will this be profitable" is "the market is growing," it's a trap, not a strategy. (Ch 11)

**Should we acquire this company?** Only if you can buy below true value, or you can add more value to the target than any other possible owner. A vague rationale ("scale," "platform," "economies of mass") = don't. (Ch 11)

**Is this objective proximate or blue-sky?** Ask: do we already know *how*, at least in outline? If not, it's blue sky — narrow the objective until the "how" becomes visible. (Ch 7)

**Is this advantage worth pursuing further, or is it a "silver machine"?** Ask: is there a specific action *I* can take to increase its value? If no lever exists, the advantage is real but not strategically interesting. (Ch 12)

**Should we tightly integrate/coordinate, or stay loose?** Tight integration only pays when the competitive challenge is high relative to your resources. Default to less coordination — it preserves specialization gains. (Ch 9)

**Is a rival's non-response real inertia, or inertia by proxy?** If they're protecting a genuinely profitable line propped up by *their customers'* stickiness (not their own dysfunction), expect a sudden, hard response once defending share matters more than protecting the old profit stream. (Ch 14)

**Trust the market/crowd, or trust the analysis?** If a completed structural analysis (e.g., Five Forces) contradicts market sentiment, check the specific facts the market's view depends on — don't treat the price itself as evidence. (Ch 18)

## Trade-off / decision tables

| Situation | Lean toward | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Resource-rich, low competitive challenge | Loose coordination | Tight integration is costly and unneeded (Ch 9) |
| Resource-poor, high competitive challenge | Tight, clever design | Resources and integration are substitutes (Ch 9) |
| Uncertain, dynamic environment | Proximate objective + option-creating moves | Foresight *worsens* with uncertainty (Ch 7) |
| Stable environment, good recent results | "Keep doing what works" | Deduction is fine when nothing new needs discovering (Ch 16) |
| Chain-linked system, underperforming | Sequential full-commitment campaigns | Simultaneous partial effort yields zero payoff (Ch 8) |
| Fixed budget, threshold effect present | Concentrate on fewer targets | Below-threshold spend anywhere is wasted (Ch 6) |

## Thresholds & defaults

- **Four hallmarks of bad strategy**: fluff, unnamed challenge, goals-as-strategy, bad objectives — any *one* disqualifies. (Ch 3)
- **Acquisition premium**: expect ~25% over market value plus fees — you need a specific reason you can recoup more than that. (Ch 11)
- **Fast-cycle advantage**: a rival on half your release cadence has the better product ~83% of the time (Nvidia's 6-month vs. 18-month cycle logic). (Ch 15)
- **Five causes of large-scale judgment disaster**: engineering overreach, smooth-sailing fallacy, risk-seeking incentives, social herding, inside view — check all five in any catastrophic-downside decision. (Ch 18)
- **Five guideposts for reading a wave of change**: rising fixed costs, deregulation, forecasting biases, incumbent response, attractor states. (Ch 13)

## Tells & smells (fast heuristics)

- Universal buy-in on a strategic statement → the hard trade-offs were dodged, not resolved. (Ch 4)
- A "vision" or "mission" that could describe almost any competitor → fluff, not strategy. (Ch 3)
- A rationale that shifts under questioning ("economies of scale" → "economies of mass" → "you can do a bigger deal") → no real rationale exists. (Ch 11)
- Nine SKUs from one company clustered at one price point → entropy has eaten a once-coherent product design. (Ch 14)
- A plan with a page of vision/mission/values before any action → template-style bad strategy. (Ch 4)
- A group converges unusually fast on one course of action for a complex problem → quick closure on the first idea, not real comparison. (Ch 17)
- "This case is different" in the face of clear historical base rates → inside view, flag and check the outside view explicitly. (Ch 18)
