## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The Kernel (Ch 5) — the book's central idea.** Every good strategy has three parts: (1) **Diagnosis** — simplifies the challenge down to its critical aspects ("what's going on here?"); (2) **Guiding policy** — an overall approach for overcoming the diagnosed obstacles, like highway guardrails that direct without dictating every move; (3) **Coherent action** — mutually reinforcing steps that actually carry out the policy. Missing any one element means it isn't a real strategy. Test any document calling itself a "strategy" against these three.

**The Four Hallmarks of Bad Strategy (Ch 3).** Detecting bad strategy is faster and more reliable than building good strategy from scratch. Scan for: **fluff** (inflated "Sunday words" masquerading as insight), **failure to face the challenge** (no named obstacle, so nothing can be evaluated), **mistaking goals for strategy** (a statement of desire like "20% growth" standing in for a plan), and **bad strategic objectives** — either a **dog's-dinner** (unprioritized laundry list) or **blue-sky** (as hard to achieve as the original challenge).

**Why bad strategy is so common (Ch 4).** Not miscalculation — active avoidance of the hard work of choice. Three pathways: unwillingness to choose (forced "consensus" among conflicting views produces empty compromise, not synthesis — universal buy-in usually signals the trade-offs were dodged), template-style vision/mission/values planning, and "New Thought" positive-thinking doctrine creeping into strategy via "shared vision" rhetoric.

**Sources of power (Ch 6–14) — nine ways a guiding policy creates advantage:**
- **Leverage** = anticipation (of others' predictable behavior) + pivot points (imbalances where small pushes unleash large effects) + concentration (threshold effects mean spreading resources thin often produces zero payoff).
- **Proximate objectives** — a target close enough at hand to be genuinely feasible; the more uncertain the situation, the *more* proximate (not more ambitious) the objective should be.
- **Chain-link systems** — performance capped by the weakest link; strengthening other links adds nothing. Unstick with sequential, fully-committed campaigns, not simultaneous partial effort.
- **Design** — strategy as an adroit, mutually-adjusted configuration (like engineering), not a choice from a pre-set menu. Resources and tight integration are partial substitutes: strong resources reduce the need for clever coordination, and vice versa.
- **Focus** — coordinating policies around one target that creates more value there than rivals can match; often *raises* unit cost while raising margin even more via pricing power.
- **Growth** is a symptom of good strategy, never a goal — engineered growth (acquisition, chasing a growing commodity market) typically destroys value.
- **Advantage** exists only in asymmetries between rivals; an advantage is only "interesting" if there are specific moves available to increase its value (the silver-machine problem: owning an advantage doesn't automatically create wealth).
- **Dynamics** — waves of change are largely exogenous; ride them via five guideposts: rising fixed costs, deregulation, predictable forecasting biases, incumbent response, attractor states.
- **Inertia & entropy** — three inertia types (routine, culture, by-proxy) each need different fixes; entropy (organizational decay) requires constant active maintenance even with zero external threat.

**Thinking like a strategist (Ch 16–18).** A strategy is a **hypothesis**, not a guarantee — test it against known principles, then the cheapest real experiment, then revise. Fight your own **quick closure** (grabbing the first plausible idea and defending it rather than questioning it) via **create-destroy** and a mental panel of real critics. Watch for **social herding** and the **inside view** ("this case is different") overriding completed structural analysis — a market consensus is not evidence.
