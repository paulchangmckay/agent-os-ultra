# Chapter 6: Using Leverage

## Core Idea
Strategic leverage comes from a mixture of three things: anticipating others' predictable behavior, finding a pivot point where a small push unleashes large pent-up forces, and concentrating limited resources on that pivot rather than spreading them evenly.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Strategic leverage = anticipation + pivot point + concentration**: the general recipe for turning limited resources into outsized effect.
  - When to use: whenever you're deciding where to apply scarce effort for maximum impact.
  - How: identify what you can reliably anticipate about others' behavior, find the imbalance in the situation that a small push would unbalance further, then commit resources at that single point rather than distributing them.
- **Anticipation**: insight into the predictable "downstream" results of events already in motion — trends, routines, and constraints that make others' behavior foreseeable, without requiring prediction of random events.
  - When to use: any time a competitor's or rival's future move follows from their own incentives, routines, or public commitments.
  - How: ask what happens next given what has *already* happened (a storm already fell in the Himalayas — the Ganges flood is now predictable), not what might happen from scratch.
- **Pivot points**: a natural or created imbalance in a situation where a relatively small adjustment unleashes much larger pent-up forces — an imbalance between position and underlying capability, or between pretension and reality.
  - When to use: when a rival's public stance or fixed position doesn't match their actual underlying strength.
  - How: find where the gap between claim and reality is widest and largest to exploit, and time your action to when that gap is most visible (Reagan's "tear down this wall" worked in 1987 under Gorbachev's liberalization claims; it would have landed flat on Andropov in 1983).
- **Concentration / threshold effects**: many systems have a critical level of effort below which action produces no result — meaning it's often better to fully clear that threshold in a narrow area than to spread the same total effort thin across many areas.
  - When to use: allocating a fixed budget/attention pool across multiple possible targets.
  - How: identify where a threshold exists (advertising, political messaging, school-turnaround programs), and concentrate enough resource in one place to actually clear it, rather than under-funding many.

## Key Concepts
- **Downstream anticipation** — Pierre Wack's Shell scenario-planning insight: "certain aspects of future events are predetermined... if there's a storm in the Himalayas, there will be flooding in the Ganges plain." Applied to the 1970s: growing populations + high reserves + development ambitions in Iran/Iraq/Venezuela predictably meant they'd push for oil price increases.
- **The "sucker bet" of median-case forecasting** — Wack's warning that standard high/medium/low scenario graphs make people feel they've accounted for uncertainty, then they plan on "medium" anyway — missing that the real risk is a price spike that lures a major investment, followed by a crash that strands it.
- **Salience/momentum effects** — people disproportionately notice and credit visible wins; fully turning around 2 schools can shift public opinion more than a diffuse 2% improvement across 200 schools, even if the latter has more total impact.
- **Anticipating others' anticipation** — a second-order failure mode: US 2003 Iraq war planners failed not just to anticipate the insurgency, but failed to anticipate that Iraqi officers (who had studied *Black Hawk Down*) were themselves anticipating that US casualties would erode American public support, as it had in Somalia and Vietnam.

## Mental Models
- Before committing resources, ask "what do I already know is going to happen downstream from events that have already occurred?" rather than trying to forecast novel uncertainty.
- When looking for a pivot point, look for the gap between a rival's stated position and their actual capability or resolve — the bigger and more exposed that gap, the more leverage a small, well-timed action has.
- When resources are limited, resist the instinct to spread them evenly across every deserving cause; ask where a threshold effect exists and fully clear it in one place instead.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a median forecast as risk management** — a three-line high/medium/low chart creates false confidence; the real danger is often the path (spike then crash) that no single line represents.
- **Spreading effort below the threshold everywhere** — diffusing a fixed budget across many targets that each individually fail to clear the response threshold wastes the entire budget, not just part of it.

## Worked Example
7-Eleven Japan (Seven & i Holdings), as explained to Rumelt by COO Noritoshi Murata in 2008. Diagnosis/pivot point: Japanese consumers are unusually variety-hungry and quickly bored (200+ soft drink brands, 70% annual turnover of the 50 varieties on a typical shelf). Leverage strategy: build a fast information-collection loop from store managers/employees about local micro-tastes, form quick-response merchandising teams, and partner with second/third-tier food manufacturers who have spare capacity to produce new private-label items cheaply and fast. In China, the pivot point was different — Chinese consumers were used to being treated as supplicants in retail settings, so 7-Eleven's existing standard of spotless stores and white-glove, bowing service (already part of its operating design, not a new investment) became a concentrated point of differentiation, producing double the sales-per-square-foot of competitors.

## Key Takeaways
1. Leverage isn't raw resource advantage — it's the combination of correctly anticipating what's already in motion, finding where a small push has outsized effect, and refusing to dilute that push across other targets.
2. Anticipation works best on what's already determined by past events and routines, not on genuinely unpredictable futures.
3. Look for gaps between claimed position and underlying reality — those gaps are where a modest, well-timed action can have disproportionate effect.
4. Threshold effects mean underfunding many initiatives can be worse than fully funding few — check whether your target domain has a real threshold before spreading resources.
5. A visible, complete win in a narrow area can generate more support and momentum than a larger but diffuse improvement.
