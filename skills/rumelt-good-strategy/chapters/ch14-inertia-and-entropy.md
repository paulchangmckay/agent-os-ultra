# Chapter 14: Inertia and Entropy

## Core Idea
Two distinct forces work against good strategy from inside an organization: inertia (resistance to changing established routines/culture even when the environment demands it) and entropy (the natural tendency of any unmanaged structure to become less organized and focused over time, even with no external change at all). Understanding both your own organization's and your rivals' susceptibility to each is often as strategically important as any external analysis.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Three types of organizational inertia**:
  1. **Inertia of routine** — standard procedures embed "the way things are done" so deeply that they filter perception itself; a routine that made sense under old conditions (e.g., airline fare-planning tools built for a regulated era) keeps producing decisions calibrated to a world that no longer exists. **Fixable** primarily through top management's perception change — new hires, acquiring firms with better methods, or redesigning routines — but usually requires replacing people invested in the obsolete methods.
  2. **Inertia of culture** — deeper and harder to change than routine: work norms and values held and enforced daily by small social groups taking cues from a high-status "alpha" member. A culture built for one purpose (AT&T Bell Labs' fundamental-research culture, built over decades as a regulated monopoly) actively resists a different purpose (shipping working consumer products) even when individual competence is high. Breaking cultural inertia requires, in sequence: **simplification** (strip administrative layers, disband coordinating committees, expose hidden cross-subsidies), **fragmentation** (break separable units apart to expose their real performance and dissolve political coalitions), **triage** (close/repair/build-on based on both performance and culture — a high performer with a toxic culture can infect the rest), then **replacing the alpha** in units being repaired, and only later reintroducing coordination once units function well independently.
  3. **Inertia by proxy** — apparent non-response to a threat that isn't actually organizational stickiness, but a rational (if fragile) choice not to cannibalize a still-profitable line of business, made possible by *customers'* inertia rather than the firm's own. Disappears suddenly once the firm decides defending market share matters more than protecting the old profit stream — but by then, switched customers (self-selected as most price-sensitive) may already be loyal to the new supplier.
  - When to use: diagnosing why an organization (yours or a rival's) isn't responding to an obvious competitive threat — the fix differs completely depending on which type of inertia is actually present.
- **Entropy**: the tendency of any unmanaged structure — physical, organizational — to become progressively less ordered even absent any external shock. Requires *constant* active maintenance to counteract, not a one-time fix. Most of a management/strategy consultant's actual bread-and-butter work is simply undoing accumulated entropy, not delivering novel strategic insight.
  - When to use: diagnosing underperformance in an organization where no dramatic external threat exists — the decline may simply be years of unweeded organizational "garden."
  - How: use a **hump chart** — rank units (stores, products, customer segments) by adjusted profit contribution and plot cumulative profit; a smooth rise to a maximum with no "hump" indicates no cross-subsidy; a hump that rises then sags indicates weak units are being propped up by strong ones, obscured by the measurement system itself.

## Key Concepts
- **Successful strategies often owe as much to rivals' inertia as to your own strength** — Netflix beat Blockbuster largely because Blockbuster couldn't abandon its retail-store commitment; understanding a rival's specific inertia can be as strategically valuable as understanding your own capabilities.
- **Gain to Operating (GTO)** — Rumelt's corrected profit measure (adjusting for distorted capital-cost accounting, like comparing a $5,000/acre 1950s store site to a $95,000/acre 1989 site on the same "return on investment" basis) used to reveal a business's real per-unit performance once accounting artifacts are stripped out.
- **Badge engineering / brand blur as visible entropy**: GM's 2008 product lineup offered nine different models clustered at the same $25,500 price point across four brands (Chevrolet, Saturn, Pontiac, Buick) — a direct decay of Alfred Sloan's 1921 design, which had deliberately given each GM brand a distinct, non-overlapping price range specifically to avoid this.
- **Internal competition can shrink, not grow, the total pie**: investment to win share from a sister brand or division (rather than an external competitor) wastes marketing/development spend on both sides and can push down both brands' prices — competition isn't automatically a "cleansing wind" inside a single corporate structure.

## Mental Models
- Before assuming a non-responsive rival is simply asleep at the wheel, check whether their lack of response might be inertia by proxy — a rational choice to protect a still-profitable line, propped up by their own customers' inertia rather than by the firm's incompetence; if so, expect the rival to respond suddenly and hard once they decide defending share matters more.
- Distinguish an org's underperformance driven by external shock-induced routine mismatch (fixable relatively quickly by changing perceptions/routines/people) from culture-driven underperformance (fixable only through a much slower simplify → fragment → triage → replace-the-alpha sequence).
- When auditing a multi-unit business for hidden waste, build a hump chart before trusting reported aggregate profit — cross-subsidies embedded in the measurement system (like Denton's incentive-payment allocation, which quietly transferred profit from strong stores to weak ones for years) can hide a badly underperforming half of the business inside a positive-looking total.

## Anti-patterns
- **Mistaking new leadership rhetoric for actual routine change**: Continental Airlines' CEO gave "animated speeches" about a new competitive spirit post-deregulation while the company's actual fare-forecasting tool was still literally the CAB-era cost-plus-markup model, dressed up as a "planning model" — the org's real routines hadn't moved at all.
- **Assuming individual talent guarantees organizational capability**: AT&T's Bell Labs held world-class researchers (inventors of the transistor, Unix, the C language) yet couldn't ship a working demo of a simple PC interface — Rumelt ended up writing the code himself in three weeks after Bell Labs quoted $3 million and two years. The blocker was culture (a research, not product-development, work norm), not competence.
- **Treating fragmented decentralization as harmless** — letting each GM division independently chase revenue (Chevrolet pushing upmarket, Pontiac pushing downmarket) without active resistance from corporate leadership is how Sloan's originally coherent 1921 design decayed into 2008's nine-models-at-one-price-point clutter.

## Worked Example
**Continental Airlines, 1980-81.** Two years after US airline deregulation, Rumelt's team forecast that deregulated fares would realign with actual costs — short-haul fares would rise (no longer subsidized) and long-haul fares would fall (no longer artificially propped up), the opposite of the industry's dominant "go long-haul, that's where the money is" consensus (United's CEO had just committed $3 billion to a long-haul fleet). Continental's executive committee rejected the analysis, citing their own "planning model" — which turned out to be the Boeing Fleet Planner, a tool that simply took operating costs and added a fixed 12% capital-return markup, because that was exactly the number the CAB had historically approved. It was a regulation-era fare-*justification* tool being used, unmodified, as a competitive fare *predictor* — an almost perfect illustration of the inertia of routine surviving underneath aggressive new rhetoric. The forecast proved essentially correct (long-haul fares fell 27% by 1984-85, short-haul fares rose 40%), but being right didn't save Continental: a strike, mounting losses, and a 1981 hostile takeover by Frank Lorenzo's Texas Air followed, and CEO Al Feldman took his own life at his desk that August.

**General Motors, 1921-2008 — entropy across nearly a century.** Alfred Sloan's 1921 "product policy" deliberately gave each GM brand (Chevrolet, Pontiac, Buick, Oldsmobile, Cadillac) a distinct, non-overlapping price range — "quality competition against cars below a given price tag, price competition against cars above it" — eliminating internal clutter and making GM the world's largest automaker by 1931. By 2008, entropy had fully reversed this design: nine different models across four brands clustered at the identical $25,500 price point, versus two models from Toyota at that price. Without constant active maintenance (which requires resisting each division's individual incentive to encroach on its neighbors' price range), the coherent design blurs into "badge engineering" — the same vehicle sold under multiple brand names. GM declared bankruptcy in June 2009.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before designing a fix for organizational underperformance, diagnose which force is actually at work — routine inertia, cultural inertia, inertia by proxy, or plain entropy — since each demands a different remedy.
2. A rival's inertia (especially inertia by proxy — protecting a profitable line by not responding) can be as valuable to understand and exploit as your own strengths.
3. Use a hump chart to check whether aggregate profitability is hiding a cross-subsidized set of badly underperforming units before assuming average performance reflects the whole.
4. Talented individuals inside an organization don't guarantee organizational capability — culture (the work norms actually enforced day to day) can block execution regardless of individual skill.
5. A coherent strategic design (like Sloan's GM brand architecture) decays without continuous active maintenance — expect entropy even in the complete absence of any external threat, and budget leadership attention accordingly.
