# Chapter 9: Using Design

## Core Idea
A master strategist is a designer, not a decision-maker choosing from a list of pre-set alternatives. Strategy is the adroit, mutually-adjusted configuration of resources and actions — like engineering a high-performance system — and the amount of clever integration needed trades off directly against the quality of resources available.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Strategy as design vs. strategy as decision**: formal decision theory (list alternatives, value outcomes, weigh probabilities) barely helps real strategists because they're rarely handed a clean menu of options — they face an ill-defined challenge and must construct a novel, coordinated response, closer to designing an aircraft than picking a forklift to buy.
  - When to use: any time a strategy problem is framed as "which of these options should we choose?" — reframe it as "what configuration of actions could we design?"
- **The resources/integration trade-off**: performance is the joint outcome of resource quality and design cleverness — the two are partial substitutes.
  - **Given fixed resources**, greater competitive challenge requires tighter, cleverer integration of actions.
  - **Given a fixed challenge level**, higher-quality resources reduce the need for tight integration.
  - When to use: deciding how much design effort a situation actually warrants, or diagnosing why a resource-rich incumbent has a sloppier strategy than a resource-poor upstart.
  - How: assess your resource position first; a strong protected resource (a patent, a dominant brand) can substitute for sophisticated coordinated strategy, while a weak resource position demands it.
- **The arc of enterprise**: a strong resource position generates profit with little ongoing strategic effort, which breeds laxity — success gets misattributed to recent actions rather than resources built in "planting seasons long past" — leading predictably to decline and an opening for a tightly-designed upstart. Study upstarts' early conquests (young Microsoft vs. IBM, young Wal-Mart vs. Kmart, Nvidia vs. Intel) to learn design-type strategy, not mature incumbents' current posturing.

## Key Concepts
- **Premeditation, anticipation, and coordinated design** — the three elements Rumelt draws from Hannibal's victory at Cannae (216 B.C.): the strategy was planned in advance (not improvised), it depended on correctly anticipating specific predictable Roman behavior (not assuming a generic "rational opponent"), and it required choreographing physically separated units (retreating center, flanking heavy infantry, encircling cavalry) acting in preplanned cohesion.
- **Strategic resource** — a long-lasting, hard-to-duplicate asset built or discovered by a company (Xerox's plain-paper-copying patents) that competitors cannot match without a net economic loss; distinct from ordinary purchasable inputs (trucks, office equipment, generic labor) that confer no advantage because rivals can buy the same things on the same terms.
- **Tight integration's cost** — a more tightly integrated design (a Formula 1 car) outperforms a looser one (a Subaru Forester) in its narrow domain but is more fragile, harder to build, and less flexible to changing conditions; tight integration is worth its cost only when the competitive challenge is genuinely high.

## Mental Models
- Before treating a strategic problem as a menu of pre-existing alternatives, ask whether it's actually a design problem — one where the "options" don't exist yet and must be constructed through mutual adjustment of many interacting parts.
- When a strategy looks effortless and "obvious" in hindsight (Xerox just "made and sold Xerox machines"), check whether it's coasting on an unusually strong prior resource position rather than genuinely clever coordination — and note that resource positions erode.
- To find real design-type strategy to study, look at the market invader (Canon vs. Xerox, Dell vs. HP/IBM, Enterprise vs. Hertz/Avis), not the mature incumbent it's displacing.

## Anti-patterns
- **Mistaking resource-driven ease for strategic skill**: Xerox's "strategic plans" in its patent-protected era were "merely financial projections" — the design-type work had already been done years earlier in creating the patent position, and the company coasted on it until challengers with better paper-handling technology (Canon, Kodak, IBM) exploited the gap Xerox never closed.
- **Attributing current results to recent, superficial actions**: books that recommend copying a successful firm's dress code or parking-space policy mistake correlation with a resource position built long before for causation.
- **Frankenstein-monster strategy** — assembling generic best-practice pieces from different competitors (a medium-price product line plus a premium dealer network plus a cost-cutting engineering culture) produces an incoherent whole that doesn't fit together, unlike a genuinely designed system where every part is specialized to the others.

## Worked Example
**Paccar's heavy-truck strategy** (Kenworth/Peterbilt brands): despite being in a low-growth, mature, highly competitive industry with only 25% market share behind Daimler's 38%, Paccar has averaged a 16% return on equity over 20 years (vs. 12% industry average) and hasn't lost money since 1939. The design: instead of treating "quality" as pure operating-cost reduction, Paccar frames quality through the *owner-driver's* eyes — the truck is their home, office, and identity, so Paccar builds a "Harley-Davidson aura" with Lexus-grade interiors, build-to-order customization via dealers using 3-D configurators, and shared parts across the line to keep inventory low without sacrificing perceived distinctiveness. This matters even to fleet buyers (who care mainly about cost per mile) because fleet drivers still prefer Paccar trucks, and driver preference affects turnover and idle time. The whole system — brand aura, dealer network, engineering culture, no small or economy trucks, no diversification into other vehicle types — is mutually reinforcing and consistent over decades; a rival assembling the pieces piecemeal (medium-price trucks + owner-driver dealers + cost-cutting engineers) would fail immediately. Paccar's risk isn't a specific competitive move — it's any structural change (e.g., NAFTA-driven shift to Mexican trucking, dealer-disintermediating online sales) that undermines the *logic* of the design itself.

## Key Takeaways
1. Treat genuinely novel strategic challenges as design problems (constructing a coordinated configuration) rather than decision problems (choosing among given alternatives).
2. Resource quality and design cleverness are substitutes — a strong resource position can carry a weak strategy for years, while a weak resource position demands a genuinely well-designed one.
3. Success built on a strong resource position tends to breed laxity, because current profits get wrongly credited to current actions instead of resources accumulated long before.
4. Study market invaders' early strategies, not mature incumbents' current behavior, to learn what real design-type strategy looks like.
5. A tightly integrated design trades flexibility for peak performance — reserve that cost for situations where the competitive challenge genuinely justifies it.
