# Chapter 2: Discovering Power

## Core Idea
The second source of a good strategy's power is insight: shifting perspective to reveal a source of strength or weakness that was hiding in plain sight — often by redefining the basic unit of the problem.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Discovering hidden power**: a decisive asymmetry that already exists in the situation but isn't visible until someone reframes what's actually being compared. Not every good strategy needs this kind of insight, but the ones that have it get "the extra kick that separates ordinary excellence from the extraordinary."
  - When to use: When conventional wisdom in your industry rests on an unexamined unit of analysis (a "store," a "product," a "weapons balance").
  - How: Ask what the standard framing takes for granted, then ask what happens if you redefine the unit itself.
- **Asymmetric cost-imposition**: compete not by matching the opponent's strengths head-on, but by taking actions that force them to spend disproportionately to counter you.
  - When to use: facing an opponent with more raw resources but rigid response patterns.
  - How: identify moves that are cheap for you but expensive for them to counter, and that don't in turn increase their offensive capability.

## Key Concepts
- **Redefining the unit of analysis** — Walton's insight wasn't a new tactic, it was realizing the *network* of stores, not the individual store, was Wal-Mart's real operating unit.
- **Complementary policies** — individual elements (bar-code scanners, satellite data, integrated logistics, large low-inventory stores) that are weak alone but powerful because they're specialized to reinforce each other; a competitor can't get the benefit by copying one piece.
- **Blocked or failed competition** — every big win has a mirror image of blocked or incompetent competition (Kmart's decentralization doctrine, its failure to link bar-code data to logistics); analyzing the winner alone shows only half the picture.
- **Breaking with doctrine** — Walton didn't just break a numeric rule of thumb (100,000-population minimum), he broke a philosophical commitment to decentralization that Kmart couldn't abandon without a near-death experience.

## Mental Models
- When a competitor's success looks inevitable in hindsight, ask "why didn't the obvious response beat them for a decade?" — the answer usually reveals a structural reason imitation was hard, not just execution skill.
- Treat a boxed "conventional wisdom" statement as a target for interrogation, not a constraint: ask what logic it rests on, then ask what changes if the underlying unit is redefined.

## Anti-patterns
- **Analyzing only the winner** — looking solely at what the successful firm did, ignoring that competitors' failures are an equal and necessary part of the explanation.
- **Piecemeal copying** — adopting one element of a complementary design (e.g., just the bar-code scanners) without the rest gains little to nothing, because the power is in the fit between parts, not any single part.

## Worked Example
Rumelt's MBA case discussion on Wal-Mart: students first list surface policies (everyday low prices, nonunion, computerized logistics) but can't explain why Kmart didn't simply copy them for a decade. The breakthrough comes when Rumelt pushes a student to role-play a store manager's father offering to buy out and run a single store independently — the class realizes the *individual store* has no viability alone; the *network* of ~150 stores sharing a distribution hub and data flows is the real unit. Walton hadn't broken the "100,000-population" rule, he'd broken the definition of "store" itself, and in doing so broke with Kmart's deeper doctrinal commitment to store-level decentralization — a commitment too costly for Kmart to abandon without crisis.

## Key Takeaways
1. Insight-driven advantage comes from redefining what's being measured or managed, not from working harder within the existing frame.
2. A winning strategy is only half-explained by what the winner did — always ask why competitors didn't or couldn't respond.
3. Complementary policies resist piecemeal imitation; that resistance is itself a source of durable advantage.
4. The deepest competitive barriers are often doctrinal (a philosophy the rival can't abandon), not just resource-based.
