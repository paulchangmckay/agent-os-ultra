# Chapter 1: Good Strategy Is Unexpected

## Core Idea
Good strategy is rare and looks surprising not because it's secret, but because most organizations default to spreading resources across many goals instead of concentrating them — so when a leader actually focuses and says no to almost everything, it reads as a revelation.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Focus as the first natural advantage**: A coherent strategy beats competitors partly because most competitors don't have one — they have "multiple goals and initiatives that symbolize progress" instead of a coordinated approach.
  - When to use: Any turnaround or competitive situation where rivals are visibly unfocused.
  - How: Identify the single fundamental problem, then cut everything that doesn't serve solving it — including options that are individually reasonable.
- **Envelopment as the general form of "unexpected"**: Schwarzkopf's Gulf War "left hook" was literally the U.S. Army's published doctrine ("Plan A"), available to anyone for $25 — yet it stunned observers because almost no one expected an organization to actually execute a pure, focused plan under internal and political pressure to hedge.

## Key Concepts
- **Coherence** — actions, policies, and resources coordinated toward one important end, not a list of disconnected initiatives.
- **Look-busy doorknob polishing** — Rumelt's term for activity (alliances, 360-degree feedback, new markets) that substitutes for a real strategy when executives can describe a competitor's winning strategy but not their own.
- **Envelopment** — feint at the front, main force strikes the flank/rear; the Army's standard offensive doctrine, and Rumelt's metaphor for how good strategy exploits an opponent's fixed attention.

## Mental Models
- Treat "surprising strategy" as a symptom, not a goal: if a strategy looks brilliant mainly because it was actually *executed* with focus, the organization's normal state is diffusion — build for focus, don't chase cleverness.
- When interviewing or auditing an organization, ask two questions separately: "how did the market leader win?" and "what is your own strategy?" The gap between a confident answer to the first and a vague answer to the second is the tell for bad strategy.

## Anti-patterns
- **Laundry-list strategy**: dedicating resources to many desirable but unconnected outcomes, and calling that a strategy — it avoids the hard work of choosing.
- **Avoiding "no"**: leaders who won't cut options to avoid offending internal interests end up with a strategy that tries to cover all the bases instead of focusing resources.

## Worked Example
Steve Jobs's 1997 Apple turnaround: Apple had 15 desktop models, multiple product groups, six national retailers, and was two months from bankruptcy. Jobs's strategy was almost entirely subtraction — one desktop model (Power Mac G3), one laptop, no printers/peripherals, five of six retailers cut, manufacturing moved offshore, an Internet Services push into direct sales. None of these moves were individually novel ("of course you cut back to survive"); what was unexpected was that someone actually did all of them together, coherently, instead of hedging. Jobs's own explanation to Rumelt: a friend couldn't tell Apple's models apart, and Jobs couldn't give her a clear answer either — that was the diagnosis that drove the whole coherent cut.

## Key Takeaways
1. A strategy doesn't need a secret insight to be powerful — it needs the discipline to actually focus and cut, which most organizations avoid.
2. If your organization can articulate a rival's winning strategy but not its own, that's a direct symptom of bad strategy, not a knowledge gap.
3. Strategy is at least as much about what you don't do as what you do — treat "no" as a core strategic output, not a side effect.
4. Published doctrine or an "obvious" playbook isn't disqualifying — most competitors won't execute it with real coherence anyway.
