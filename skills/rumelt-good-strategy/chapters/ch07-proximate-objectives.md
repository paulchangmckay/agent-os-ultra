# Chapter 7: Proximate Objectives

## Core Idea
A proximate objective is a target close enough at hand to be feasible — the leader's job is to absorb the situation's overwhelming ambiguity and hand the organization a problem it can actually solve, not to announce an inspiring but unreachable goal.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Proximate objective**: an objective that is (1) genuinely feasible with known methods/resources and (2) strategic — chosen because achieving it opens a cascade of further opportunity, not just because it's easy.
  - When to use: whenever facing an ill-structured, ambiguous, or highly dynamic situation — the more uncertain the future, the *more* proximate (not more distant) the objective should be.
  - How: state the objective, then check whether the org already has or can readily get the specific capability to hit it. If key steps require capabilities nobody has, it's not proximate — it's a blue-sky wish (see Ch 3).
- **Taking a strong position and creating options** (vs. forecasting far ahead): in dynamic, low-visibility situations, the right posture isn't "look further ahead" (foresight only gets worse with more uncertainty) — it's chess-master logic: make moves that increase your own future options and decrease the opponent's, accumulate positional advantage, then cash it in later with tactical moves.
  - When to use: any high-uncertainty competitive situation where long-range forecasting would be false precision.
  - How: ask "what move most increases my future options and narrows theirs?" rather than "what does the 10-year plan say?"
- **Hierarchies and layering of objectives**: proximate objectives cascade both down an organizational hierarchy (each unit derives its own proximate sub-objective) and through time (mastering one objective unlocks the next tier), and what's proximate for one organization may be impossible for another with fewer accumulated skills.
  - When to use: setting objectives for organizations at different maturity stages, or for the same organization over time.
  - How: identify what "rung" of capability the organization currently stands on (PJ the helicopter pilot's ladder: basic flight control → night flying → formation flying → shipboard landing) and set the objective one rung up, not three.

## Key Concepts
- **Absorbing ambiguity** — a leader's core responsibility: reduce the organization's overwhelming uncertainty into a simpler, solvable problem, even if the simplification isn't literally "the truth."
- **Threshold of feasibility** — the line between a genuinely proximate objective (moon landing: hard, but built on known rocket technology and clear engineering steps) and an infeasible one dressed up as a goal (the "War on Drugs," "energy independence" absent the political will to act) — the latter isn't achievable within the current framework, no matter how much effort is applied.
- **The layering/ladder of competence** — higher-order coordination (landing a helicopter on a pitching ship deck) is only possible once lower-order skills (basic cyclic/collective/pedal coordination) are fully automatic; asking an organization to concentrate on a rung it hasn't earned yet is pointless.

## Mental Models
- When a goal feels inspiring but vague, ask "do we already know *how* to do this, at least in outline?" If not, it's not a proximate objective yet — the real work is finding the version of the goal that is.
- In fast-moving, uncertain situations, resist demands for a longer-range forecast; instead ask "what position increases our future options the most?"
- Before setting an ambitious objective for an organization, check what rung of capability it currently occupies — an objective that assumes a skill not yet mastered will fail regardless of motivation.

## Anti-patterns
- **Blue-sky goals disguised as bold vision**: JFK's moon landing is often misremembered as visionary audacity; it was in fact a carefully vetted, feasible objective built on von Braun's technical analysis that the US had a real resource-based edge in *heavy-lift* rockets specifically. Genuinely infeasible goals (War on Drugs, energy independence without political commitment) get the "inspiring" treatment but fail because no one actually knows how to reach them within the current constraints.
- **Skipping rungs** — pushing a startup that hasn't mastered coordinating engineering/marketing/distribution to instead "expand into Europe," or pushing a newly international firm to arbitrage knowledge globally like a mature multinational before it has mastered operating across languages and cultures.

## Worked Example
NASA's Surveyor moon-lander program, where Rumelt worked as an engineer. Nobody knew the actual composition of the lunar surface (soft powder? needle crystals? boulder fields? — all had scientific advocates), and this ambiguity paralyzed the engineering design process, because any design could be attacked by someone else's theory of a hostile surface. JPL's Phyllis Buwalda resolved this by publishing an engineering specification describing the moon as resembling the Southwestern US desert: hard, grainy, slopes under 15 degrees, small scattered boulders. When Rumelt objected that nobody actually knew this, Buwalda's answer was the chapter's core lesson: "The engineers can't work without a specification... if it turns out to be a lot more difficult than this, we aren't going to be spending much time on the moon anyway." The specification wasn't a scientific claim — it was a strategically chosen proximate objective that absorbed the ambiguity and gave engineers a solvable problem. Five Surveyor probes landed successfully in 1966-67, and Surveyor 3's landing site proved close enough to reality that Apollo 12 landed 200 yards away in 1969.

## Key Takeaways
1. The right level of ambition scales *inversely* with certainty — more uncertainty calls for a more proximate objective, not a bolder long-range one.
2. A leader's job includes absorbing ambiguity on the organization's behalf — handing down a solvable problem, even an imperfectly accurate one, beats handing down an accurate description of how uncertain everything is.
3. Test any "bold" objective by asking whether the specific steps to achieve it are already known (proximate) or merely hoped for (blue sky).
4. In dynamic situations, favor moves that create future options and narrow a rival's, over moves justified by long-range forecasts.
5. Objectives cascade through a capability ladder — don't assign an objective that assumes a rung of skill the organization hasn't yet reached.
