# Chapter 10: Focus

## Core Idea
Focus means two things at once: coordinating a set of policies so their interacting effects produce extra power, and aiming that power at a specific target segment where it creates more value than competitors can match — even when the official, widely-repeated story of a company's strategy misses the real mechanism entirely.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Focus strategy**: attacking a deliberately narrow segment of a market with a business system that delivers more value to that segment than rivals' systems can — the source of advantage is concentration and coordination, not raw scale.
  - When to use: in a competitive industry where the obvious/conventional description of a successful firm's strategy doesn't actually explain its outsized returns.
  - How: identify the firm's policies, find which ones differ from the industry norm, then group the *targets* of those distinctive policies (not the policies themselves) to find the common underlying focus.
- **Strategy identification as independent analysis**: never accept a company's own stated strategy (or Wall Street's, or a case study's) at face value — CEOs and analysts sometimes hide the truth, sometimes misstate it, and sometimes genuinely don't know the real reasons for their own success.
  - When to use: any time you're analyzing why a company outperforms its industry.
  - How: (1) map the competitive environment and industry economics first; (2) list each policy that differs from the norm; (3) for each distinctive policy, ask specifically who benefits or what situation it targets (replace abstract categories with concrete examples — "smaller businesses," not "customers"); (4) look for the single underlying pattern that ties the targets together, testing candidate explanations against the full set of facts rather than accepting the first plausible one.

## Key Concepts
- **Captive producer trap** — the major can companies (Continental, National, American Can) built dedicated plants for single large customers (e.g., Miller Brewing) to capture long-production-run efficiencies, but this handed the buyer massive bargaining leverage (two similar suppliers competing for one customer, threat of the buyer building its own can line) — resulting in a structurally low-margin industry (4-5% return on assets).
- **Short runs as the pivot** — Crown Cork & Seal deliberately did the *opposite* of the majors: smaller plants, none captive, each serving multiple customers, excess capacity held ready, heavy technical assistance and rapid response — all policies that individually raise unit cost, but together target smaller customers, rush orders, new products, and seasonal demand spikes: situations willing to pay a premium (40-50% higher prices) for responsiveness.
- **Focus reverses the captive-producer bargaining trap**: instead of one customer with several competing suppliers (a "captive" major), Crown becomes one supplier with several customers per plant — flipping who holds the bargaining leverage.
- **Higher cost, higher margin** — focus does not mean lower cost; Crown's unit cost per can was almost certainly *higher* than the majors', but its focus let it capture a larger fraction of the value it created through pricing power, producing 50-60% higher profitability despite (not because of) higher costs.

## Mental Models
- When a company's stated strategy ("we focus on hard-to-hold applications like aerosols and soda") sounds plausible but doesn't actually explain superior returns (canning soda isn't a hard technical problem — any can maker can do it), keep digging: the real mechanism is usually structural, not product-category-based.
- To find hidden focus, build a two-column table: distinctive policy on the left, the specific customer/situation it targets on the right — then look for what circles all the targets, not what circles the policies.
- Resist the first plausible explanation that "seems reasonable" under classroom or meeting pressure (Reza's quick but incomplete guess that rapid response serves "less stable" small-firm demand) — test it against the full set of available facts before committing to it.

## Anti-patterns
- **Accepting the official narrative uncritically**: Fortune, Harvard Business School case writers, and stock analysts had all repeated "Crown focuses on hard-to-hold applications" for decades without noticing this doesn't actually explain the margin difference.
- **Confusing a "good thing a company does" with a strategic focus**: technical assistance and rapid response looked like generic customer-service virtues until analysis revealed they specifically target smaller customers and rush orders — random "best practices" without a unifying target aren't a focus strategy, just a list of nice policies.
- **Pursuing multiple goals without concentrating resources**: Rumelt's closing point — most large, complex organizations don't have a real strategic logic behind their scale because they spread resources across many goals rather than concentrating them enough to achieve a breakthrough in any one.

## Worked Example
Rumelt's classroom teardown of the Crown Cork & Seal case (developed under CEO John Connelly starting in the early 1960s, delivering a 19% average annual shareholder return over 35 years — vs. 4-5% ROA for major competitors). Students first repeat the textbook answer ("focus on hard-to-hold applications: aerosols and soft drinks"), which a student named Melissa correctly challenges as insufficient (canning soda isn't technically hard). Working policy by policy — technical assistance (targets smaller businesses lacking in-house canning expertise), rapid response (targets rush orders from new products or seasonal spikes), manufacturing structure (smaller, non-captive plants, each serving multiple customers, excess capacity on standby) — the class eventually converges (credited to a student, Julia) on "shorter runs" as the unifying target: Crown deliberately competes for the short-run, urgent, small-customer business the majors' captive long-run model structurally can't serve well, and in doing so avoids the captive-producer bargaining trap entirely.

## Key Takeaways
1. A company's own explanation of its success, and the conventional industry narrative around it, may both be incomplete or wrong — verify against the full pattern of distinctive policies before accepting either.
2. Focus is about coordinating multiple policies around a single underlying target, not any one policy in isolation — list distinctive policies, then find what target unifies them.
3. Focus strategies often *raise* unit costs while raising margins even more, by capturing pricing power in an underserved segment — don't assume higher profitability implies lower cost.
4. Deliberately serving the opposite segment from the industry's dominant players (short runs vs. long runs) can flip who holds bargaining power in a buyer-supplier relationship.
5. Most large organizations lack a coherent strategic logic not because none is possible, but because they don't concentrate resources enough on any one focus to make a breakthrough.
