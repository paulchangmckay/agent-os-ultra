# Chapter 17: Using Your Head

## Core Idea
The most fundamental obstacle in all strategy work isn't lack of frameworks — it's your own cognitive myopia and the unconscious tendency to seize the first plausible insight and then spend all your effort justifying it instead of questioning it. Overcoming this requires specific personal habits: forcing reflection through list-making, working the kernel from any starting point, deliberately trying to destroy your own ideas, and pre-committing judgments in writing so you can calibrate them against outcomes.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **"Make a list" as forced reflection, not organization**: the value of Frederick Taylor's famous advice to Carnegie ("list the ten most important things, then start on number one") isn't the list itself — it's that *constructing* it forces you to reflect on the intersection of what's important and what's actionable, counteracting the mind's tendency to lose sight of larger purposes while absorbed in immediate events.
  - When to use: any time you (or someone you're interviewing) haven't consciously surfaced priorities recently — Rumelt's own field interviews, which were nothing more than asking obvious strategy questions and writing down the answers, were repeatedly called "the most useful conversation" by executives simply because the act of answering forced reflection they hadn't otherwise made time for.
- **Quick closure and the "unconscious dodge"**: most people, even experienced executives, solve ill-structured problems by grabbing the *first* plausible insight that pops into mind, then spend their effort justifying it rather than questioning it or generating real alternatives — because staying oriented feels better than the discomfort of re-entering "choppy water." This is efficient for routine problems but dangerous for genuinely important, ill-structured strategic situations.
  - When to use: whenever a group converges suspiciously fast on a single course of action for a complex problem.
  - How: explicitly ask "how did you arrive at that?" — not to challenge the conclusion, but to surface whether it was actually considered against alternatives or just accepted as the first idea that arrived.
- **The Kernel as a reminder, not a sequence**: insight strikes in only *one* of the kernel's three elements at a time (a diagnosis, a guiding policy, or a specific action) — never as a complete strategy all at once. The kernel's job is to remind you to work the other two elements out from wherever the spark landed, rather than stopping at a single localized insight and mistaking it for a full strategy. (Rumelt's own working style: start from diagnosis and work outward to policy and action, though this isn't the only valid entry point.)
- **Problem-Solution** (working backward from policy to diagnosis): many "strategies" have a stated direction but no clear diagnosis behind it. Deliberately shift attention from "what is being done" to "why it is being done" — reframing TiVo's situation as "how do we time-shift TV" versus "who controls the set-top box bottleneck" produces entirely different, non-overlapping sets of viable strategies.
- **Create-Destroy**: the discipline of actively trying to destroy your own first insight — not just adding a token second option or a generic "do nothing" alternative, but genuinely exposing the fault lines in your existing idea to force a better one to emerge. Most people, even when explicitly asked for more alternatives, only tweak their first idea slightly rather than doing this.
  - How: build a personal **panel of experts** — real people (mentors, respected colleagues, public figures whose thinking you know well) held in memory as integrated personalities, not abstract frameworks, and mentally ask each "what's wrong with this? What would you do?" This works because humans are far better at simulating a remembered, well-understood personality's likely reaction than at applying an abstract theory consistently.
- **Pre-committing judgments in writing**: before a meeting, case discussion, or decision, privately write down your prediction/interpretation *before* hearing others' views. This is the only way to actually calibrate and improve judgment over time — without pre-commitment, it's easy to retroactively believe "I thought of that" for every idea raised by others, which teaches you nothing.

## Key Concepts
- **Myopia as the common obstacle across all strategic situations**: whether the specific challenge is industry structure, competitor anticipation, or resource analysis, being "strategic" fundamentally means being less shortsighted than your undeliberative self and than your rivals — not the same as claiming to predict the future.
- **Blink-style fast intuition has real but bounded value**: Gladwell's *Blink* argument (trust fast pattern-matching judgments, especially from experienced people) holds for person/social-pattern judgments, but research shows people are systematically poor at judging event probabilities, their own relative competence, and cause-and-effect — even when made quickly by experienced people. Reserve deliberate slow analysis for exactly the high-stakes, ill-structured situations (should we go to war, should we merge) where a "blink" judgment is inappropriate.
- **Corner solutions**: Rumelt's imagined counsel from his panel (economist David Teece, Steve Jobs) on TiVo converges on the same underlying point — good strategies tend to be "corner solutions" that focus on one aspect of a situation rather than trying to compromise across many; TiVo's problem was doing too many conflicting things (hardware, software, advertising, platform relationships) instead of picking one clear position (e.g., license the software, or fully integrate with a platform).

## Mental Models
- When a group reaches consensus unusually fast on a complex, high-stakes problem, ask each person individually how they arrived at their view — the answers usually reveal quick closure on a first idea, not real comparison of alternatives.
- Before finalizing a strategic recommendation, force yourself through create-destroy: actively argue against your own first idea using a real, specific person's likely objections (not a generic devil's advocate exercise) rather than a vague "what could go wrong" pass.
- Before any meeting or case discussion where you'll hear others' views, privately write your own prediction first — this is the only way to know afterward whether your judgment was actually good or merely felt good in hindsight.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a token second option as genuine alternative generation**: adding "walk away" or "do more study" as a second and third option doesn't count as create-destroy — those are generic placeholders that apply to any situation, not responses built from a real reconsideration of the specific facts.
- **Retroactive "I thought of that" self-congratulation** — sitting through a group discussion without having pre-committed a judgment, then unconsciously crediting yourself for every good point someone else raises, robs you of the only real mechanism for calibrating your own judgment over time.

## Worked Example
Rumelt's 2005 executive seminar on TiVo. Seventeen managers each wrote a one-paragraph strategy recommendation before class. When Rumelt asked not "what's your recommendation" but "how did you arrive at it," a clear pattern emerged: nearly every participant had identified a single problem area on their first pass through the case (manufacturing cost, cable-provider relationships, competitive threat from software rivals) and then generated a recommendation aimed at that area alone — without returning to reconsider whether that was even the right problem to focus on, and without seriously exploring a second course of action. Only one participant had genuinely considered more than one approach. Rumelt's own worked alternative reframings of the TiVo problem (via Problem-Solution) showed how differently the situation looks depending on where you locate the core obstacle: as a manufacturing-cost problem (unbundle hard-disk capacity, price-discriminate by usage), as a platform-dependency problem (cable/satellite companies bundle set-top boxes and can extract most of TiVo's potential profit — suggesting an antitrust-style legal strategy over more marketing spend), or as a positioning problem (TiVo is loved by consumers but structurally opposed to the TV industry's interests, akin to Napster). None of these framings was objectively "correct" — the point was that most of the room had locked onto just one without testing it against the others.

## Key Takeaways
1. The single most valuable strategy skill is managing your own thinking — recognizing and resisting the pull toward quick closure on your first plausible idea.
2. "Making a list" works because constructing it forces reflection, not because the resulting list itself has magic value — the same logic applies to structured interviews and self-questioning generally.
3. Insight always arrives as a fragment (a diagnosis, a policy, or an action) — use the kernel to deliberately work out the other two elements rather than mistaking a fragment for a complete strategy.
4. Build and actually use a mental panel of real people whose judgment you respect to critique your own ideas — a remembered personality generates sharper, more specific criticism than an abstract framework.
5. Pre-commit your judgments in writing before hearing others' views; it's the only way to genuinely calibrate and improve your strategic judgment over time.
