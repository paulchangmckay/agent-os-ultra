# Chapter 4: Why So Much Bad Strategy?

## Core Idea
Bad strategy isn't caused by miscalculation or lack of training — it's the active avoidance of the hard work of choice, and it flows through three recurring pathways: unwillingness to choose, template-style strategy, and "New Thought" positive-thinking doctrine.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three pathways to bad strategy**:
  1. **Unwillingness or inability to choose** — when leaders can't set aside some goals in favor of others, they compromise on vague statements that offend no one and commit to nothing.
  2. **Template-style strategy** — filling in a vision/mission/values/strategies template as a one-size-fits-all substitute for analyzing actual challenges and opportunities.
  3. **New Thought** — the doctrine (descended from 19th-century positive-thinking religion) that visualizing success and banishing negative thoughts is itself sufficient for achieving it.
  - When to use: as a diagnostic lens — when you see bad strategy, ask which of the three produced it, since the fix differs for each.
- **Condorcet's paradox applied to strategy debate**: with three or more people ranking three or more strategic options, majority-preference voting can cycle with no stable winner (A beats B, B beats C, C beats A) — proven formally by Kenneth Arrow's impossibility result. Forcing "consensus" among genuinely conflicting strategic views therefore tends to produce an empty compromise, not a synthesis.
  - When to use: recognizing why a leadership team stuck on "reaching consensus" among several coherent-but-conflicting strategies (e.g. DEC's "Boxes" vs. "Solutions" vs. "Chips") ends up with fluff instead of a decision.
  - How: a leader must actually decide and set aside the losing options rather than search for a synthesis that satisfies everyone — disciplined conflict, not consensus, produces the strongest arguments.

## Key Concepts
- **Strategy as scarcity's child** — strategy exists because resources are scarce; having one means choosing a path and saying no to others, which is inherently costly to internal interests.
- **Universal buy-in as a red flag** — if everyone in the room agrees enthusiastically, that's evidence no real trade-off was made, not evidence of a good strategy.
- **Charisma-in-a-can** — the mutation of genuine charismatic/transformational leadership theory (Weber, Bennis, Bass) into a bureaucratic vision/mission/values template that produces "pious statements of the obvious" (e.g., a university's mission being "we are a university").
- **New Thought lineage** — traced from Prentice Mulford's *Thoughts Are Things* (1889) through Wattles's *The Science of Getting Rich*, Napoleon Hill, Norman Vincent Peale, to *The Secret* (2007); its modern strategy-adjacent form shows up as Peter Senge's "shared vision" doctrine, which Rumelt argues misattributes real successes (Apple, Ford) to unified vision rather than to specific technical/competitive insight plus execution.

## Mental Models
- Whenever a strategic plan is stuffed with vision/mission/values boilerplate, ask "what would change if we deleted this page?" — if nothing, it's template fluff, not strategy.
- Treat forced consensus among strongly-held, mutually exclusive strategic views as a warning sign, not a success: the paradox means group agreement may be structurally unstable, and only a decision (someone accountable saying yes to one path) breaks the cycle.
- Distinguish leadership (which motivates people to accept painful change) from strategy (the craft of determining which purposes are worth pursuing and achievable) — they're often needed together but are not the same skill or output.

## Anti-patterns
- **Compromise-as-consensus**: when a leader asks a divided group to "reach consensus" on strategy, the result is typically an amorphous statement that offends no faction but commits to nothing (DEC's "DEC is committed to providing high-quality products and services and being a leader in data processing").
- **Visualizing outcomes instead of confronting obstacles**: New Thought-influenced strategy skips consideration of what could go wrong; Rumelt: "I would not care to fly in an aircraft designed by people who focused only on an image of a flying airplane and never considered modes of failure."
- **Crediting "shared vision" for what was really specific competence plus luck**: Apple's and Ford's success are commonly retold as triumphs of unified vision, when the actual drivers were narrow technical insights (Wozniak's chip trick, Ford's industrial engineering) executed by pockets of real expertise.

## Worked Example
DEC's 1992 strategy meeting: three executives championed incompatible strategies — Alec ("Boxes," stay an integrated hardware/software company), Beverly ("Solutions," pivot to customer problem-solving since boxes had commoditized), Craig ("Chips," focus on semiconductor technology). Their pairwise preferences formed a Condorcet cycle (Boxes beats Chips, Solutions beats Boxes, Chips beats Solutions) with no stable majority. CEO Ken Olsen asked the group to reach consensus; unable to resolve the cycle, they settled on a fluffy, contentless mission statement. DEC's CEO was replaced in 1992 by Robert Palmer, who unilaterally chose Chips — five years too late to save the company, which was acquired by Compaq in 1998. Contrast: Intel's Andy Grove and Gordon Moore faced an analogous crossroads (exit memory chips, focus on microprocessors) and made the hard call directly — Grove: "Why shouldn't you and I walk out the door, come back and do it ourselves?" — becoming the world's largest semiconductor company by 1992.

## Key Takeaways
1. Bad strategy is a choice-avoidance problem, not a knowledge problem — more analytical training doesn't fix it.
2. When a leadership team can't agree, forcing consensus produces fluff; someone with the authority to choose must actually choose and accept that some interests lose.
3. Universal buy-in on a strategic statement is evidence the hard trade-offs were dodged, not evidence of alignment.
4. Watch for "shared vision" and New Thought language creeping into strategy discussions — they redirect attention from obstacles to affirmations.
5. Leadership (motivating people through painful change) and strategy (deciding what's worth pursuing and achievable) are different jobs; having one without the other is a known failure mode.
