# Chapter 3: Bad Strategy

## Core Idea
Bad strategy isn't the absence of strategy — it's a specific, recognizable pattern with four hallmarks: fluff, failure to face the challenge, mistaking goals for strategy, and bad strategic objectives. Learning to detect these four dramatically improves your ability to judge and fix strategy.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Four Hallmarks of Bad Strategy** — Rumelt's core diagnostic checklist:
  - When to use: Any time reviewing an existing "strategic plan," before trying to improve it.
  - How: Scan the document/plan for each of the four independently; any one present is disqualifying.
    1. **Fluff** — gibberish dressed as insight using inflated "Sunday words" (e.g. "customer-centric intermediation" = "we are a bank").
    2. **Failure to face the challenge** — no defined obstacle, so the strategy can't be evaluated or improved (International Harvester's plan never named its real problem: inefficient work rules).
    3. **Mistaking goals for strategy** — a statement of desire (e.g. "20% growth, 20% margin") standing in for a plan to get there.
    4. **Bad strategic objectives** — subgoals that are either a "dog's dinner" (an unprioritized laundry list) or "blue sky" (as hard to achieve as the original challenge, e.g. demanding "transformational leadership" with no theory of why leadership is currently weak).
- **Goals vs. objectives distinction**: use "goal" for overall values/desires (freedom, growth, being a good workplace) and "objective" for specific, operational, achievable targets. Strategy is the bridge that turns vague goals into a short list of actionable objectives — this bridge-building is "a leader's most important job."

## Key Concepts
- **Sunday words** — inflated, unnecessarily abstruse vocabulary used to manufacture the illusion of high-level thinking (fluff's main tool).
- **Dog's dinner objectives** — an unprioritized scramble of "things to do" from a planning meeting, mislabeled as strategy (one real example: 47 "strategies," 178 "action items," one of which was "create a strategic plan").
- **Blue-sky objectives** — objectives that just restate the desired end state without a theory of how to get there; adds no value over the original challenge.
- **The elephant in the elevator** — Rumelt's phrase for an obvious, named-but-unaddressed root obstacle a plan conspicuously talks around (Harvester's labor/work-rule dysfunction).

## Mental Models
- Before judging any strategy, ask "what is the challenge this claims to solve?" If you can't state it in one sentence, the plan has already failed hallmark #2 regardless of its length or polish.
- Read a strategic plan as if you had to bet money on it: if every sentence could be true of almost any competitor in the industry, it's fluff, not strategy.
- Separate what leaders *want* (goals) from what they've decided *to actually do next* (objectives) — a plan overflowing with the former and thin on the latter is a warning sign.

## Anti-patterns
- **Fluff as expertise theater**: complex diagrams and buzzwords ("evolution toward a knowledge space," "sophisticated value extraction") standing in for real analysis — Enron's Arthur Andersen bandwidth-trading pitch is the canonical example, delivered 14 months before Enron's collapse.
- **Motivation substituting for strategy**: treating "the will to win" or "one last push" as sufficient (Chad Logan's "20/20" plan, echoing the WWI Passchendaele doctrine that cost hundreds of thousands of lives for five miles of ground) — motivation is necessary but the leader's job is to build the lever that makes the push effective, not just to demand it.
- **Annual rolling budgets mislabeled "strategy"**: resource/financial plans are legitimate management tools but they are not strategy and can't substitute for identifying and overcoming a specific obstacle.

## Worked Example
Chad Logan, CEO of a graphics-arts firm, presented Rumelt with a "20/20" plan (20% annual revenue growth, 20% margin) and a list of seven "key strategies" that were purely goals and values ("we will delight our customers," "we will have a culture of commitment"). When Rumelt asked "what has to happen for this to be realized — where's the point of leverage?", Logan had no answer beyond citing Jack Welch on stretch goals and "the will to win." Rumelt's advice: stop trying to align people around the 20/20 number, and instead spend a month with a small team identifying the company's most promising actual opportunities (internal bottlenecks or external market shifts), then narrow to one or two pivotal objectives. Logan declined and hired a "Visioning" consultant instead, who ran an exercise asking the team to imagine the company "twice as big" — reinforcing the goals-as-strategy error rather than fixing it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Detecting bad strategy is a faster, more reliable skill than trying to build good strategy from scratch — learn the four hallmarks first.
2. A plan that never names its central obstacle cannot be evaluated, improved, or trusted, no matter how detailed its projections look.
3. Ambitious financial goals (X% growth, Y% margin) are not strategy; they're the *result* you want a strategy to produce.
4. Bad strategic objectives fail in one of two directions: too scattered (dog's dinner) or too vague/unreachable (blue sky) — both skip the hard work of finding a genuinely achievable point of leverage.
5. Motivation and "the will to win" cannot substitute for a leader's responsibility to design conditions under which effort actually pays off.
