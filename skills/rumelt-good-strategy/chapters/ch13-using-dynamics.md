# Chapter 13: Using Dynamics

## Core Idea
Industries shift in "waves of change" — largely exogenous forces (technology, regulation, cost structure) beyond any one firm's control — and a strategist's job is not to forecast the future precisely, but to understand the underlying forces early enough to channel resources toward the positions that will become the new high ground.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Waves of change**: like wind in a racing boat's sails — raw, sometimes turbulent power that the strategist must harness rather than create. Two ways to gain new high ground: pure innovation (create it yourself) or exploiting an exogenous wave (the chapter's focus).
  - When to use: any time industry structure feels unusually fluid or turbulent, rather than the normal stable state most industries occupy most of the time.
  - How: don't try to forecast the endpoint; dig beneath surface commentary to find the *fundamental forces* driving observed shifts, and watch for second-order/derivative effects, not just the obvious main effect (TV's obvious effect was competing with movies for audience attention; its second-order effect was the collapse of studio-system B-movies and the rise of independent film production).
- **Five guideposts for reading a wave of change**:
  1. **Rising fixed costs** — escalating (especially R&D/product-development) fixed costs force industry consolidation, since only the largest players can amortize them (black-and-white → color film pushed out Ilford and Ansco, consolidating around Kodak/Fuji; piston → jet engines cut the field to three players).
  2. **Deregulation** — regulated prices almost always subsidize some buyers at others' expense; when deregulation hits, newly freed competitors chase the old "profitable" segments long after the subsidy (and the profit) has vanished, partly because formerly regulated firms don't actually know their own real costs (their accounting systems were built to justify regulated prices, not reveal true costs).
  3. **Predictable forecasting biases** — three specific recurring errors: (a) failing to predict a durable-good sales *peak* and drop-off (only frequently-repurchased goods grow smoothly forever; durables like fax machines or flat-screen TVs saturate and crash); (b) over-predicting a "battle of the titans" between large incumbents when the real disruption bypasses both (AT&T vs. IBM "convergence" never happened — Cisco and the Internet ate the ground under both); (c) advising firms to copy whoever currently looks like the winner, without checking whether the winner's profits came from a source (regulatory subsidy, unsustainable growth-stage economics) about to disappear.
  4. **Incumbent response** — assess how structurally locked-in incumbents are likely to resist a transition that threatens their accumulated skills and positions (elaborated fully in Ch 14).
  5. **Attractor states** — a description of how an industry "should" evolve given technology and the structure of demand, pulling toward greater efficiency — not a guarantee, but a gravity-like pull. Distinct from a corporate "vision" because it's based on overall system efficiency, not one company's desire to capture the whole pie. Look for **accelerants** (a demonstration effect — Napster made "a song is just a 2.5MB file" viscerally obvious to millions overnight) and **impediments** (regulatory/licensing friction — nuclear power's obvious efficiency case blocked for decades by the US licensing process) that speed or slow the pull toward the attractor state.

## Key Concepts
- **The microprocessor as a common root cause of two "mysterious" shifts**: Rumelt traces both (a) software becoming the dominant source of competitive advantage and (b) the "deconstruction" of the vertically-integrated computer industry into a horizontal one (Intel makes chips, others make disks, Microsoft makes OS software, assembled by whoever) back to the same underlying cause — cheap microprocessors let every component become "smart" (a keyboard, a hard disk each got their own onboard chip), which trivialized the systems-integration expertise that had been IBM's and DEC's core moat.
- **Software's cost advantage over hardware**: not that software engineers are cheaper, but that the cost of *prototyping, testing, and fixing mistakes* is far lower — a hardware bug means months of physical redesign; a software bug means retyping, recompiling, and trying again in minutes. This asymmetry, not raw engineering cost, is why software became the preferred medium for embedding proprietary logic.
- **Historical perspective as a bias check** — Rumelt's own point-by-point comparison of 1875–1925 (electrification, streetcars, automobiles, telephone, radio, most major consumer brands founded) against 1942–1992 (television, jet travel, computers, the Internet) to argue that "we live in an age of unprecedented change" is usually an ahistorical claim that inflates the perceived novelty of the present.

## Mental Models
- Before accepting "the rate of change is accelerating" as a premise, check it against a genuinely comparable historical 50-year window — often it isn't true, and believing it drives bad urgency-based strategic decisions.
- When a wave of change is visible but its cause is debated, look for the single underlying technical or structural driver behind multiple seemingly separate "mysterious" shifts (as the microprocessor explained both software's rise and vertical deconstruction).
- Before copying the current apparent winner in a fast-changing industry, check whether their advantage rests on a subsidy, a growth-stage economic quirk, or a structural position about to disappear (Delta's Atlanta hub profits came from soon-to-vanish regulated rural-route subsidies; WorldCom's per-unit cost advantage evaporated once sleepy incumbents finally cut prices).

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating a network of loosely coupled firms as the interesting phenomenon** rather than asking why the previously integrated firm (IBM) disappeared — the "network of relationships" that academic sociologists love to map is, per Rumelt, "the ghostly remnant of the old IBM's nerve, muscle, and bone," not itself the cause.
- **Predicting convergence as a "battle of the titans"** — NEC chairman Koji Kobayashi's 1977 "C&C" (computers and communications) vision, and the AT&T/IBM anticipated showdown it inspired, both missed that the ground itself (via microprocessors, software, deconstruction, the Internet) would give way under both giants simultaneously, rather than either winning.
- **Consultant advice to imitate the current highest-flying competitor** without checking the source of their apparent advantage — three documented failures: airlines copying Delta's hub-and-spoke (built on soon-to-vanish regulated subsidies), telecoms copying WorldCom's fiber-ring strategy (built on temporary low-volume cost advantages that vanished once incumbents cut prices), and web start-ups copying the "portal" model (Yahoo!/AOL) right before the open web made walled-garden portals obsolete.

## Worked Example
**Cisco Systems**, as diagnosed by Rumelt in a 1996 conversation with Matra Communications CEO Jean-Bernard Lévy. Matra couldn't understand how a Stanford spin-off with no scale advantage had beaten IBM, AT&T, Alcatel, NEC, and Siemens into the emerging internetworking market — the traditional barrier to entry, "significant market presence in at least two legs of the [Japan/Europe/North America] triad," should have protected the giants. Lévy's own explanation: Cisco's router advantage came down to roughly 100,000 lines of exceptionally skillful firmware code, written by a team of two to five people — not patents, not scale. Rumelt's insight, arrived at later that evening: this wasn't a one-off skill story, it was a symptom of a much deeper shift from economies-of-scale-based competition (coordinating hordes of engineers on billion-dollar hardware projects) to economies-of-skill-based competition (small teams writing clever software) — "as if military competition suddenly shifted from large armies to single combat." Cisco then rode four simultaneous waves to dominance: (1) the microprocessor's software-centrality, letting Cisco outsource hardware manufacturing entirely and focus on software/sales/service; (2) the rise of corporate data networking, which needed exactly Cisco's multi-protocol router capability; (3) the emergence of IP as a vendor-neutral protocol that none of the incumbents (each protecting their own proprietary protocol — IBM's SNA, DEC's DECnet, Apple's AppleTalk) would fully embrace; (4) the explosion of public Internet use starting in 1993. By the time the public Internet wave hit, Cisco already controlled two-thirds of the corporate networking market and was positioned to capture the resulting Internet backbone router business as well.

## Key Takeaways
1. Waves of change are largely exogenous — the strategist's job is to read them early and channel resources toward the future high ground, not to create or control the wave itself.
2. Look past the obvious main effect of a change to its second-order and derivative consequences — that's usually where the real strategic opportunity or threat lives.
3. Use the five guideposts (rising fixed costs, deregulation, forecasting biases, incumbent response, attractor states) as a checklist when trying to make sense of an unusually turbulent industry.
4. Never copy a current apparent winner without first checking whether their advantage rests on a soon-to-vanish subsidy or temporary condition.
5. When multiple "mysterious" shifts appear simultaneously in an industry, look for a single common root cause (as the microprocessor explained both software's rise and industry deconstruction) rather than treating them as separate stories.
