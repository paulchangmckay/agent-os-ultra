# Chapter 7: Naive Intervention

## Core Idea
The urge to "do something" causes harm when it ignores the break-even point between probabilistic benefit and probabilistic damage — this net harm from well-intentioned action is **iatrogenics**, and it is most dangerous exactly where we deny a system's own antifragility.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Iatrogenics**: net harm caused by the healer/helper/intervener, in excess of any benefit — from Greek *iatros* (healer). Applies far beyond medicine: to policy, regulation, parenting, editing, and economic stimulus.
  - How to apply: before any intervention, explicitly estimate probabilistic benefit *minus* probabilistic cost — not benefit alone. The 1930s tonsillectomy study (389 children, ~45% recommended unnecessary surgery by each successive panel of doctors) is the concrete illustration.
- **Primum non nocere ("first, do no harm")**: the Hippocratic principle that took medicine roughly 24 centuries to actually operationalize — Taleb extends it as the default posture toward any complex, antifragile system: absence of intervention implies absence of iatrogenics.
- **Theory vs. phenomenology**: theories are fragile (they "come and go"), phenomenology — observing an empirical regularity without needing a theory for it — is robust. Physics is the rare domain where theories are reliable; importing theory-driven confidence into economics, social science, or policy is "asking a whale to fly like an eagle."
- **Non-naive interventionism**: the corrective to a misreading of the chapter's message — Taleb is not against intervention, he's against *unexamined* intervention. He explicitly favors intervening to limit size, concentration, and speed (large corporations, pollution sources, highway speed) because these carry asymmetric, nonlinear risk and minimal iatrogenics — while opposing intervention that removes a system's own antifragile self-regulation (e.g., over-regulating street signs reduces driver alertness; the Drachten, Netherlands experiment found removing signs *increased* safety).
- **Fabian procrastination**: modeled on the Roman general Fabius Maximus ("Cunctator," the Procrastinator), who beat a superior opponent by deliberately delaying engagement — deliberate non-action can be a legitimate, high-skill strategy, even though it is systematically under-rewarded (the surgeon who operates unnecessarily gets the Rolls-Royce; the doctor who lets a back heal itself gets nothing).

## Key Concepts
- **Agency/principal-agent problem** — a doctor, broker, or politician whose own interests (fees, reelection) diverge from the person they're serving, which compounds iatrogenics by incentivizing intervention regardless of net benefit.
- **Semmelweis** — the 19th-century doctor who correctly identified that hospital births killed more mothers than street births (via what he called doctors "a bunch of criminals" for the "hospital fever" they spread) — punished, institutionalized, and killed by the very disease he warned about, before being vindicated posthumously.
- **Concealed/distributed iatrogenics** — harm that's easy to see when a surgeon amputates the wrong leg, but nearly invisible when, e.g., a child is medicated for a loosely diagnosed condition and the long-term harm never gets counted as an "error."
- **The under-reward of prevented harm** — there's no name and no bonus for the manager, doctor, or general who avoids a catastrophe that therefore never happened; interventionist "look what I did for you" is structurally more rewarded than "look what I avoided for you."

## Mental Models
- Run the **break-even test** before any "helpful" intervention: what's the probability and size of the benefit, versus the probability and size of the harm — including harm that's hidden, delayed, or distributed?
- Use the **Drachten test** for regulation: does removing/reducing a rule increase the system's own self-regulating attention (as with street signs), or does the domain have genuinely nonlinear risk that justifies a hard limit (as with highway speed)? Don't overgeneralize either answer to the other case.

## Anti-patterns
- **Treating "intervention" and "non-intervention" as a single political axis**: Taleb explicitly rejects both the "hyper-regulate everything" and "deregulate everything" postures — the right question is domain-specific risk structure, not ideology.
- **Rewarding visible action over invisible prevention**: bonus and recognition systems that only pay for "look what I did" create systemic pressure toward naive intervention, because doing nothing (even when correct) is invisible and unrewarded.
- **Borrowing physics-level theory confidence into non-physical domains**: economics, social policy, and medicine outside a few settled areas don't have physics' track record of shrinking errors between theories — treating a model there as reliably predictive is the "whale flying like an eagle" mistake.

## Key Takeaways
1. Before intervening in any system, explicitly weigh probabilistic benefit against probabilistic (including hidden, delayed) harm — don't assume benevolent intent guarantees net benefit.
2. Some interventions are justified and even under-applied: limiting size, concentration, and speed in domains with nonlinear risk (corporations, pollution, highway speed) tends to reduce Black Swan exposure with low iatrogenic cost.
3. Removing external control can sometimes *increase* safety by reactivating a system's own antifragile self-regulation (attention, adaptation) — but this doesn't generalize to every domain.
4. Expect institutional incentives to bias toward visible, rewarded intervention and away from correct, unrewarded restraint — plan for this bias rather than assuming good outcomes will be recognized.
