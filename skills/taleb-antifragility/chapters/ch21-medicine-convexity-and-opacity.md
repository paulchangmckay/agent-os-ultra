# Chapter 21: Medicine, Convexity, and Opacity

## Core Idea
Medical intervention should be reserved for cases where potential benefit is large and clearly outweighs potential harm (severe illness); for near-healthy people, "doing nothing" (via negativa) beats intervention, because the convexity structure flips — small, uncertain benefit against large, hidden, delayed potential harm.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **First principle of iatrogenics (empiricism)**: you don't need evidence of harm to be suspicious of an unnatural (via positiva) intervention — the burden of proof is on the intervention to show benefit, not on the skeptic to show harm, because harm is often in the *future*, not yet visible in the data (trans fat, thalidomide, DES — years-to-decades delayed harm following small, immediate, visible benefit).
- **Second principle of iatrogenics (nonlinearity in response)**: treatment benefit is convex to how sick the patient is — mild hypertension: ~5.6% benefit from treatment (1 in 18); severe hypertension: ~72% benefit (2 in 3) — while the iatrogenic risk of the drug stays roughly constant across severity. Conclusion: **focus intervention heavily on severe cases, and largely leave near-healthy people alone** — the same intervention that's a clear net-positive in the sick can be a net-negative "sucker's trade" in the healthy.
  - How to apply: before treating/intervening, locate the patient/system on the severity spectrum — near-healthy → let nature/default process run; severely compromised → intervene aggressively, "no holds barred."
- **The "no free lunch for the healthy" heuristic**: because illness is rare and its rarity increases nonlinearly with severity (an illness 5 standard deviations from normal is over a million times rarer than normal), nature would likely have already found any "unconditional" enhancement available to a healthy person — any drug/intervention that claims to make the healthy unconditionally better (steroids, trans fat) conceals a hidden trap, precisely as in a market "sucker's trade."
- **Jensen's inequality in medicine (convexity bias)**: since biological responses are often nonlinear, a *randomly varying* exposure can produce better outcomes than the same average delivered steadily — mechanical ventilation delivering variable pressure (mimicking natural breathing's noise) outperforms constant pressure for sick lungs, because humans are convex/antifragile to lung pressure variation up to a point.

## Key Concepts
- **"Do you have evidence?" fallacy** — mistaking *absence of evidence of harm* for *evidence of absence of harm* (parallel to mistaking "no evidence of disease" for "evidence of no disease") — a logical error that disproportionately snares educated, credentialed people who demand narrowly-scoped statistical proof before acting on a plausible harm.
- **Iatrogenics is in the patient, not the treatment** — the same drug/procedure can be strongly net-positive for a critically ill patient and net-negative for a near-healthy one; risk-benefit must be evaluated per severity level, not as a fixed property of the treatment itself.
- **The swollen-nose emergency room anecdote** — Taleb's own broken nose: the doctor recommended icing the swelling (his body's own healing response) with no compelling evidence that reducing it helps outside rare threat-to-life cases — a lived example of "sucker-rationalism" and unexamined interventionism.
- **The reclassification trap** — pharmaceutical incentive structures push toward reclassifying mild/borderline conditions (e.g., "pre-hypertensive") to expand the treatable population, exploiting doctors' interventionist instincts rather than genuinely serving patient benefit.

## Mental Models
- Apply the **severity-gated intervention rule**: is this patient/system near-normal or seriously compromised? Convexity of benefit (and the corresponding tolerance for iatrogenic risk) flips sharply between these two regimes.
- Use the **"nature would have found it" check** before trusting a claimed unconditional benefit for a healthy person: given how thoroughly evolutionary selection pressure operates, an easy, side-effect-free universal enhancement is unlikely to have gone undiscovered by nature already.

## Anti-patterns
- **Demanding narrow statistical "evidence of harm" before acting on plausible risk**: this fallacy (used to defend smoking, trans fat, and pollution in their early years) inverts the correct burden of proof — the non-natural intervention should have to prove safety, not the skeptic prove danger.
- **Treating near-healthy and severely ill patients with the same intervention threshold**: because benefit is convex to severity while iatrogenic risk is roughly flat, a uniform intervention policy systematically over-treats the healthy and can under-treat the critically ill.
- **Assuming steady/constant delivery is always better than variable delivery**: for convex biological responses, deliberately varying exposure (as with intermittent ventilator pressure) can outperform steady delivery at the same average — via negativa doesn't mean "avoid all variation," it means avoid unnecessary generalized intervention.

## Key Takeaways
1. Reserve medical/policy intervention for cases with large, clear benefit that visibly exceeds potential harm (severe illness) — default to non-intervention for marginal/near-healthy cases.
2. Put the burden of proof on the unnatural/interventionist option to show it's safe, not on the skeptic to show it's harmful — especially when harm could be delayed by years or decades.
3. Before trusting a claimed "free" health/performance benefit with no downside, be suspicious — nature's long selection process makes genuinely free lunches rare.
4. Consider that variable, noisy delivery of a convex-response input can outperform steady delivery at the same average — check for nonlinearity before assuming steadiness is the safe default.
