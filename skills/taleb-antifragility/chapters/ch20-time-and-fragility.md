# Chapter 20: Time and Fragility

## Core Idea
Time is a form of disorder — the fragile eventually breaks under it, the antifragile survives and even strengthens. This gives a rigorous method for forecasting: predict the future by *subtracting* what is fragile (via negativa), not by adding imagined new things to the present (the common but backward approach).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The Lindy Effect**: for nonperishable things (technologies, books, ideas — anything with an *informational* rather than organic nature), every additional day survived *increases* expected remaining life expectancy; for perishable things (humans, single physical objects), every additional day *decreases* it. A technology in use for 40 years can be expected to last roughly another 40; a person aged 40 has a *falling* remaining life expectancy each year.
  - How to apply: to judge whether something will persist, check how long it has already persisted — for nonperishable/informational things, longevity itself is evidence of continued future longevity, not a reason to expect imminent obsolescence.
- **Via negativa / subtractive prophecy**: the only rigorous way to forecast the future is to identify what is currently fragile and subtract it (it will eventually break), rather than imagining new additions to the present. Positive Black Swans (genuinely new things) are far less predictable than negative ones (the fragile breaking) — so predict by removal, not by addition.
- **Neomania**: "a love of change for its own sake" — a modern disease/bias that leads people to overestimate how much of the future will be *new* and underestimate how much will simply be what has already survived (the wheel, the chair, wine, silverware, shoes, cooking with fire) — most of what fills a person's ordinary evening (Taleb's restaurant dinner example) is millennia old, not futuristic.
- **Technology at its best is invisible**: the most valuable technologies displace *fragile, unnatural* prior technologies (the Internet dismantling bureaucratic gatekeeping, "barefoot" running shoes replicating the calloused hunter-gatherer foot) rather than adding new visible complexity — self-subtracting technology.

## Key Concepts
- **Perishable vs. nonperishable** — perishable things (an organism, a single physical object) have a hard-wired organic expiration; nonperishable things (a technology's *design*, a book's *content*, a gene's *code*) don't, and their survival is governed by the Lindy effect rather than a fixed lifespan.
- **Longer-term predictions can be more reliable than short-term ones for what's fragile** — you can be quite confident that a currently fragile, Black-Swan-prone thing will eventually be swallowed by history (time increases the probability of its exposure being tested), even though you can't say when; ordinary short-term forecasts (sales, profits) instead degrade in accuracy the further out they go.
- **The retrospective-distortion penalty for correct prophets**: being right early gets you reviled; being right in hindsight gets your idea dismissed as "trivial" — Taleb recommends the Fat Tony approach (bet on it financially rather than seek credit) as the only sane response to this asymmetric treatment.

## Mental Models
- Apply the **restaurant-dinner test** to any prediction of "the future": how much of tonight's ordinary experience (shoes, wine, chairs, silverware, cooked food, cab rides) is actually ancient, versus how much is genuinely novel? Calibrate your expectations about the future accordingly — most of it will look like survivors of the past, not new inventions.
- Use the **Lindy check** before betting on the persistence of anything nonperishable: how long has it already survived? Longer survival is evidence *for* continued survival, not a reason to expect it's "due" for replacement.

## Anti-patterns
- **Forecasting by imaginative addition**: picturing the future by adding speculative new technologies onto the present (as in most futurist literature, from Jules Verne to modern tech conferences) has an extremely poor track record — almost everything imagined this way never materializes, while what actually persists is systematically underestimated.
- **Treating technological "innovation" hype as inherently valuable**: technothinker culture (Taleb's critique of contemporary tech conferences) tends toward "engineering minds," a lack of literary/historical grounding, and a bias toward adding complexity rather than removing fragility — a marker of neomania, not of genuine progress.
- **Judging an old technology's "obsolescence" by its age alone**: for nonperishable/informational things, age is evidence of robustness (Lindy), not fragility — the opposite intuition from how we correctly reason about human aging.

## Key Takeaways
1. To predict the future responsibly, subtract the currently fragile rather than add imagined novelties — via negativa is the only defensible forecasting method under real uncertainty.
2. Apply the Lindy effect correctly by first classifying an entity as perishable (organic, gets less likely to persist with age) or nonperishable/informational (gets more likely to persist with age).
3. Expect most of the ordinary future to resemble long-surviving elements of the present and past, not the speculative additions imagined by futurists or technologists.
4. Value technologies that remove fragility and unnecessary complexity (invisible, self-subtracting technology) over those that merely add visible novelty.
