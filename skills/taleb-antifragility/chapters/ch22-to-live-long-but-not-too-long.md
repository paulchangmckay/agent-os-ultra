# Chapter 22: To Live Long, but Not Too Long

## Core Idea
Rising life expectancy is misattributed almost entirely to medicine in general, when the real drivers are convex-case interventions (severe/lethal conditions) plus societal factors (sanitation, lower crime, legal enforcement) — while medicine's intervention in marginal, non-severe cases (mammogram overtreatment, elective surgery) can actively *shorten* life. Subtraction — removing unnatural additions, doctors, and interventions for mild cases — is often the higher-return health strategy.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Deconstructing the "we live longer" argument**: increased life expectancy is a composite of sanitation, penicillin, lower crime, and emergency/severe-case medicine — not evidence that all medical treatment helps. You must subtract the cost of "diseases of civilization" (cardiovascular disease, cancer, dental cavities largely absent in primitive societies) from the credit given to modern medicine.
  - How to apply: never infer "medicine as a whole extends life" from "life expectancy has risen" — decompose the causes and check which specific interventions (severe-case, convex) versus which impose net harm (marginal-case, concave).
- **The mammogram/overtreatment case study**: annual mammograms for women over 40 don't increase overall life expectancy (and may decrease it) — breast-cancer-specific mortality falls, but death from *other causes* (side effects of unnecessary surgery, chemo, radiation on tumors that would never have killed) rises to offset or exceed it. A concrete instance of the severity-gated intervention rule from Ch 21: treating what wasn't going to kill you can be the thing that kills you.
- **Affirming the consequent (the cancer-panic logical error)**: "many who die of cancer had a malignant tumor" does *not* imply "all malignant tumors lead to death" — the ancestral/emotional overreaction to any cancer diagnosis (evolutionarily useful as an overreaction heuristic) leads to systematic overtreatment when applied indiscriminately to modern diagnostics.
- **Subtraction adds to your life**: Taleb and Spyros Makridakis' estimate that cutting elective/marginal medical expenditure (while preserving/increasing spend on severe, high-iatrogenic-margin cases) would likely *extend* average life in rich countries — reasoning backward from iatrogenics to treatment decisions, not forward from diagnosis to automatic treatment.

## Key Concepts
- **"The good is mostly in the absence of bad" (Ennius)** — happiness/health are better approached as subtractive concepts (remove what's harmful) than additive ones (add what's claimed beneficial) — mirrors via negativa applied to well-being generally, not just medicine.
- **Ancestral dietary heuristics** — Taleb's personal rules: eat only fruits with an ancient Mediterranean name (skip mangoes, papayas, modern-bred sweet oranges/apples), drink only liquids at least a thousand years old (wine, water, coffee — no soda, no juice), avoid removing fiber from carbohydrates (whole fruit vs. juice) — all instances of "the non-natural needs to prove its benefit" from Ch 21, applied to diet.
- **"What is called 'healthy' is generally unhealthy"** — Taleb's rule of thumb for marketing-driven health claims (orange juice, "social" networks, "knowledge" economy) — treat a product's health branding as a reason for skepticism, not reassurance.
- **Removing offensive irritants** — Taleb reports his own health improved measurably from removing modern stressors (news media, commute, air conditioning, gym machines, market forecasts) rather than adding any new "health" intervention — a lived instance of the subtractive strategy applied broadly to lifestyle, not just medicine or diet.

## Mental Models
- Apply the **decomposition test** whenever "medical progress" is credited with a societal outcome: which specific factor (sanitation? severe-case surgery? legal/crime reduction?) actually drove it, and which factors are being smuggled in by association?
- Use the **would-this-have-killed-me-anyway filter** before accepting aggressive treatment for a mild/borderline diagnosis: is the condition itself likely fatal, or does the anxiety of diagnosis (affirming the consequent) push toward treatment whose iatrogenic risk exceeds the disease's own risk?

## Anti-patterns
- **Crediting all medical treatment for rising life expectancy**: this conflates convex (severe-case, clearly beneficial) intervention with concave (marginal-case, net-harmful) intervention — the aggregate statistic hides the sign flip between them.
- **Reflexive aggressive treatment of any abnormal finding (a tumor, a lab deviation) regardless of actual lethality risk**: this is the logical error of affirming the consequent applied to diagnosis — not all detected abnormalities are on a path to killing the patient, and treating them as if they are can be the actual cause of harm.
- **Trusting "healthy" branding on manufactured or reformulated foods/drinks**: marketing-driven health claims (orange juice, low-fat products, sports drinks) frequently mask exactly the kind of unnatural intervention via negativa warns against.

## Key Takeaways
1. Don't infer "medicine works" broadly from "life expectancy rose" — separate convex (severe-case) medical contribution from societal factors and from net-harmful marginal-case overtreatment.
2. Before treating a mild or borderline diagnosis aggressively, check whether the condition itself carries meaningful mortality risk — if not, the treatment's iatrogenic risk may exceed the disease's own risk.
3. Apply subtraction broadly to personal health: removing unnatural additions (processed foods, unnecessary medications, chronic modern stressors) is often higher-return than adding a new "healthy" intervention.
4. Treat aggressive marketing of a product's health benefits as a signal for scrutiny, not reassurance — "healthy" branding often marks exactly the unnatural intervention that needs proving, not trusting.
