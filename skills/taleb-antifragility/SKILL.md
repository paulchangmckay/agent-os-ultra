## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**The Triad (Ch 1, 8) — the book's central classification.** Every exposure to disorder is Fragile (harmed by volatility, more downside than upside), Robust (indifferent to it), or Antifragile (benefits from it, more upside than downside). Antifragile is not "very robust" — it is a distinct third category, the true opposite of fragile. Test anything — a portfolio, a person, an institution — by asking "does it gain or lose from volatility?"

**The Foundational Asymmetry (Ch 10) and Convexity (Ch 18).** Fragility = more downside than upside from a source of variation (unfavorable asymmetry, curves inward like a frown — concave). Antifragility = more upside than downside (favorable asymmetry, curves outward like a smile — convex). Detect fragility directly: **if harm accelerates as a shock's intensity increases, the thing is fragile** — this alone predicted Fannie Mae's 2008 collapse in 2003, without predicting the trigger.

**Barbell Strategy (Ch 11).** Combine two extremes — maximally safe (bounded, known max loss) plus maximally speculative (small known cost, large/unbounded upside) — and avoid the "medium risk" middle, which is a sucker's game because it's hardest to estimate accurately. Reduces risk of ruin to near-zero while preserving upside. Nearly every antifragile structure in the book has a barbell component.

**Optionality (Ch 12).** "The right, but not the obligation" — pay a small bounded cost for exposure to large uncertain upside. You don't need to predict or understand the outcome if the payoff structure is already asymmetric in your favor (Thales of Miletus and the olive presses). Many valuable options are free or embedded (an unsolicited invitation, a rent-controlled lease).

**Via Negativa / Subtraction (Ch 20, 22).** The only rigorous way to forecast the future or improve a complex system (health, policy) is to *remove* what is fragile or unnatural, not to *add* speculative novelty. "Look what I avoided" is systematically under-rewarded compared to "look what I did" — expect this bias in every incentive system.

**Skin in the Game (Ch 23–24).** Anyone offering a forecast, policy opinion, or professional judgment should bear personal, symmetric exposure to being wrong (Hammurabi's builder-under-the-house-that-collapses logic). An agent with no downside from their opinion is not offering a reliable opinion — discount accordingly. Profession shapes belief more often than belief shapes profession (check the causal arrow).

**Iatrogenics (Ch 7, 21).** Net harm from the healer/intervener, in excess of benefit. Naive intervention ignores the break-even point between probabilistic benefit and probabilistic (often hidden, delayed) harm. Rule: reserve intervention for cases where benefit is large and clearly exceeds harm (severe illness, genuine emergencies); default to non-intervention for marginal cases, where the same convexity math flips against acting.

**Lindy Effect (Ch 20) and Green Lumber Fallacy (Ch 14).** For nonperishable/informational things (technology, books, institutions), every day survived *increases* expected future longevity — the old is more trustworthy than the new, not less. Separately: the knowledge that looks necessary for success from the outside (credentials, narrated theory) is often not what actually drives it — tacit, market-selected, practiced knowledge routinely outperforms articulated theory (Fat Tony, foreign exchange traders, cathedral builders).
