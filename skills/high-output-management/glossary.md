# Glossary

**Achiever** — a person driven to test the limits of their own capability by an abstract need for achievement, regardless of domain; identified in the ring-toss experiment alongside gamblers and conservatives (Ch 11).

**Black box** — a model of any production process as input (raw material + labor) flowing in and output flowing out, with "windows" (indicators) cut in to reveal internal state (Ch 2, Ch 3).

**Build to forecast** — producing based on anticipated future demand rather than confirmed orders; carries inventory risk but is necessary when throughput times are long (Ch 2).

**Build to order** — producing only after a confirmed customer order; avoids inventory risk but means longer customer wait times (Ch 2).

**CUA factor** — an informal composite index of a working environment's complexity, uncertainty, and ambiguity, used to determine the appropriate mode of control (Ch 10).

**Cultural values (mode of control)** — governance based on the group's interest taking precedence over the individual's, requiring trust built from shared experience; appropriate under high group-interest motivation and high CUA (Ch 10).

**Dual reporting** — an employee reporting to two supervisors, each governing a different dimension of the work (typically technical standards vs. operational priorities); the mechanism that makes hybrid organizations function (Ch 9).

**Free-market forces (mode of control)** — governance based on clearly priced self-interested exchange, needing no supervision; appropriate for high self-interest, low CUA transactions (Ch 10).

**Functional group** — an organizational unit organized around a specialized capability (e.g., finance, personnel) that serves multiple mission-oriented units, acting as an internal subcontractor (Ch 8).

**Grove's Law** — all large organizations with a common business purpose end up in a hybrid organizational form; the only exception is a conglomerate, which lacks a common purpose (Ch 8).

**Hybrid organization** — an organization combining mission-oriented business units and functional groups, since neither pure extreme is viable at scale (Ch 8).

**Key result** — a specific, dated milestone by which a person paces progress toward an objective in an MBO system; must be worded with enough precision that meeting or missing it is unambiguous (Ch 6).

**Knowledge power vs. position power** — the divergence, common in fast-changing know-how businesses, between authority based on formal position and authority based on current technical knowledge (Ch 5).

**Leverage (managerial)** — the ratio of output generated to a given unit of managerial activity; can be positive or negative (Ch 3).

**Life-relevant maturity** — the parent-child analogue of task-relevant maturity; the appropriate parenting style shifts as a child's readiness/independence grows, structure moving from externally imposed to internally held (Ch 12).

**Limiting step** — the slowest, most difficult, most sensitive, or most expensive step in a production flow; the step around which the entire flow and its time offsets should be planned (Ch 1).

**Linearity indicator** — a chart plotting actual progress against the ideal straight-line path to a period target, flashing an early warning if actual progress falls behind (Ch 2).

**Managerial output** — the output of a manager's organization plus the output of neighboring organizations under his influence; expressed as Output = ΣLᵢ × Aᵢ (leverage times activity, summed) (Ch 3).

**Maslow's hierarchy of needs** — physiological, safety/security, social/affiliation, esteem/recognition, self-actualization; a satisfied need stops motivating, and the dominant unsatisfied need drives behavior (Ch 11).

**MBO (Management by Objectives)** — a planning discipline pairing an objective (where I want to go) with key results (how I'll pace and know I'm getting there) (Ch 6).

**Meddling** — a supervisor exploiting superior knowledge to take over a subordinate's task rather than letting him work it through, producing negative managerial leverage by training subordinates to take less initiative (Ch 3).

**Mission-oriented meeting** — an ad hoc meeting designed to produce a specific output, usually a decision, as opposed to a regularly scheduled process-oriented meeting (Ch 4).

**Mission-oriented unit** — an organizational unit fully responsible for and focused on a specific business/product/market outcome (Ch 7, Ch 8).

**Mode of control** — one of three invisible, pervasive means by which behavior in organizations is governed: free-market forces, contractual obligations, or cultural values (Ch 10).

**Nudging** — influencing a decision or direction without issuing a firm directive; distinct from decision-making (Ch 3).

**One-on-one** — a regularly scheduled meeting between supervisor and subordinate for mutual teaching and information exchange, run as the subordinate's meeting with his own prepared outline (Ch 4).

**Operation review** — a formal presentation meeting connecting people several organizational levels apart who have no other regular contact, structured around organizing manager, reviewing manager, presenters, and audience roles (Ch 4).

**Output measures** — plottable results from the "black box" of a subordinate's work (completed designs, quotas met); contrasted with internal measures, which capture what's happening inside that will drive future output (Ch 13).

**Peer-group syndrome** — the phenomenon where a group of organizational equals talks in circles without noticing, each waiting for consensus or a senior signal before risking an individual opinion; fixed via the "peer-plus-one" approach (Ch 5).

**Peer-plus-one** — bringing a senior or otherwise invested individual into a peer-group meeting explicitly to break stalemate and provide confidence to decide (Ch 5).

**Performance bonus** — the at-risk portion of compensation tied to performance, scaled to represent a higher percentage of total pay as seniority/total compensation rises (Ch 15).

**Peter Principle** — the tendency for a person to be promoted until reaching a level where they only "meet requirements" and stop being promoted further; Grove frames this as an unavoidable oscillation, not a one-way trap to avoid (Ch 15).

**Plane (organizational)** — a distinct hierarchy serving one specific purpose (operating, coordinating, task force); a person can sit on multiple planes with different, even reversed, reporting relationships without genuine conflict (Ch 9).

**Present status** — a person's or unit's current capabilities plus pipeline output, the second input (alongside environmental demand) to the three-step planning process (Ch 6).

**Process-oriented meeting** — a regularly scheduled meeting for exchanging knowledge and information (one-on-ones, staff meetings, operation reviews), as opposed to an ad hoc mission-oriented meeting (Ch 4).

**Recycling** — openly moving a person promoted beyond their demonstrated capability back into a role they've proven they can excel in, framed as correcting management's misjudgment rather than the employee's failure (Ch 15).

**Stagger chart** — a chart tracking how a forecast for a given future period changes across successive months of re-forecasting, revealing forecast reliability and trend better than a single trend line (Ch 2).

**Strategy vs. tactics** — strategy is the most abstract, general summary of planned actions; tactics is the specific implementation; one manager's strategy is often the next level down's tactical concern (Ch 6).

**Task-relevant maturity (TRM)** — a combination of achievement orientation, readiness for responsibility, education, training, and experience, specific to a particular task; the primary variable determining the appropriate management style (Ch 12).

**The three L's** — Level, Listen, Leave yourself out: the discipline for delivering a performance review (Ch 13).

**Time offset** — scheduling each step of a process to finish at the right moment relative to the limiting step, so nothing arrives early or late (Ch 1).

**Work simplification** — a three-step technique (flowchart every actual step, count them, target a 30–50% reduction) for eliminating unnecessary steps that persist by tradition alone (Ch 2).
