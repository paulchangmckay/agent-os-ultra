# Chapter 6: Planning: Today's Actions for Tomorrow's Output

## Core Idea
Planning is an ordinary, everyday activity — not a lofty separate function — whose sole real output is the set of actions taken today to close the gap between what your environment will demand and what your current trajectory will actually deliver; Management by Objectives (MBO) makes this concrete by pairing an objective (where I want to go) with key results (how I'll know I'm getting there).

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The three-step planning process**: (1) establish projected environmental demand — what will customers/the environment want from you now and roughly a year out?; (2) establish present status — what will you be producing if nothing changes, including your work-in-process pipeline and a realistic loss/attrition factor; (3) reconcile the two — decide what more or less to do to close the gap. The resulting set of actions is your strategy.
  - When to use: any planning horizon, personal or organizational — the gas-gauge decision on your morning commute is the same three-step structure as an annual corporate plan.
  - How: never let step 2's constraints creep back into step 1's demand estimate — if marketing quietly shrinks its forecast because it assumes manufacturing can't deliver more, the real gap is hidden and never gets addressed.
- **Strategy vs. tactics**: the most abstract, general summary of your planned actions is your strategy; the specific implementation is tactics — and one manager's strategy is frequently the next-level-down manager's tactical concern (a communications VP's "install email" strategy becomes the mailroom manager's tactical problem).
- **Management by Objectives (MBO)**: answers two questions — (1) Where do I want to go? (the objective); (2) How will I pace myself to know I'm getting there? (the key results/milestones). Time frame should be short enough that feedback arrives while it's still actionable (quarterly or monthly if the overall plan is annual).
  - How to apply: keep the number of objectives small — "if we try to focus on everything, we focus on nothing" — and word key results with enough specificity and a hard date that there's no ambiguity about whether they were met.
- **The planning-horizon rule**: plan several years out (Intel used five), but recognize only the next year's portion is actually implemented before the next planning cycle replans everything again — so don't over-invest precision in years you'll revisit anyway, and don't replan so often that you lose the feedback needed to judge whether prior decisions were right.
- **Objectives vs. output measures (MBO's real function)**: an MBO system paces and self-monitors a person — it is not a legal contract for grading performance. A person can meet every key result and still miss the objective (Columbus), or miss the stated objective and still perform outstandingly (Columbus again, having found the New World instead of an Asia trade route).

## Key Concepts
- **Environmental demand**: what customers, vendors, and neighboring internal groups will require of you, examined at present and roughly a year forward.
- **Present status**: your current capabilities plus your realistic pipeline output, expressed in the same "currency" as the demand estimate, adjusted for a realistic attrition/scrap rate on in-flight projects.
- **The gap**: the difference between projected demand and present status — planning's entire purpose is deciding what to do about it.
- **Key result**: a milestone stated with enough specificity and a deadline that meeting or missing it is unambiguous.

## Mental Models
- "What do I have to do today to solve — or better, avoid — tomorrow's problem?" Forcing yourself to focus only on today's crisis (like scrambling for gas after the car has stopped) means the planning failure already happened in the past.
- Treat a bound, unread "Annual Plan" document as a red flag — the real output of planning is the decisions and actions it triggers throughout the organization, not the document itself.

## Anti-patterns
- **Letting demand estimates be pre-compromised by capacity fears**: if a group privately caps its own forecast because it assumes production can't deliver more, the organization never learns the true size of the opportunity or gap.
- **Planning too frequently**: replanning before you've had time to judge the impact of your last round of decisions removes the feedback loop planning depends on.
- **Treating MBO mechanically**: using key results as a rigid legal document (either a manager grading strictly against them, or a subordinate refusing to seize an unplanned opportunity because "it wasn't in my objectives") is petty and unprofessional — MBO is a pacing tool, not a contract.

## Worked Example
Grove retells Columbus's voyage as an MBO case study: Queen Isabella's objective was to increase Spain's wealth; Columbus's negotiated objective (agreed after free discussion) was to find a new trade route to the Orient; his key results were concrete and dated — obtaining ships, training crews, a shakedown cruise, setting sail. Every key result was met, yet the objective (a new route to Asia) was never achieved — he found the New World instead, an outcome of incalculable value to Spain despite "failing" by strict MBO terms. Grove then mirrors this with a real Intel case: the Far East construction manager's objective ("obtain decision on Philippine plant expansion") was supported by four specific, dated key results (land study by June, financial analysis, present to the steering group, get Grove's ratification by October) — each completed, and the objective met, illustrating MBO working exactly as designed.

## Key Takeaways
1. Planning's only real output is the actions and decisions it causes today — not the plan document itself.
2. Run the three-step process (environmental demand → present status → reconcile) without letting capacity assumptions contaminate your demand estimate.
3. An MBO objective and its key results serve to pace and self-monitor a person, not to serve as a rigid contract for judging performance.
4. Keep the number of objectives small on purpose — too many objectives is functionally the same as having none.
5. Plan on a horizon long enough to see around corners, but only fully commit to the portion of the plan you'll implement before the next planning cycle.
