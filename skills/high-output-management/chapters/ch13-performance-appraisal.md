# Chapter 13: Performance Appraisal: Manager as Judge and Jury

## Core Idea
A performance review's true purpose is to improve the subordinate's future performance, not to retrospectively justify a rating — and doing it well requires assessing real output (not activity, potential, or appearances) using output and internal measures weighted by time-offset, then delivering the assessment with total candor, total listening, and total focus on the subordinate rather than the reviewer's own comfort.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Output measures vs. internal measures**: output measures are the black box's plottable results (completed designs, sales quotas met, yield improvements); internal measures capture what's happening inside the black box that will drive future output (team morale, positioning for future tasks, whether current results are being achieved sustainably). There's no fixed formula for weighting the two — the weighting can be 50/50, 90/10, or 10/90, and can shift month to month, but a reviewer must consciously know which trade-off is being made.
  - When to use: every performance assessment — never rely on output measures alone, since they can look excellent while masking a deteriorating internal state (or vice versa).
- **The time-offset in performance**: a subordinate's output during a review period may reflect work done long before that period — "the light from distant stars." Grove recounts giving a manager a superior rating based on excellent output that actually reflected work from a prior year, while ignoring internal-measure warning signs (high turnover, grumbling) that predicted the next year's collapse; he later concluded the internal measures should have overridden the lagging output numbers.
  - How to apply: identify the typical time lag between a subordinate's actual activity and its visible output, and weight internal measures more heavily when that lag is long or output is otherwise likely to be misleading.
- **The "potential trap"**: always assess actual performance and real output, never "potential," form, or how someone "acts like" a good manager. Grove refused to approve a high rating for a general manager who "handled himself well" but whose unit missed every output target — rating appearance over substance would signal to the whole organization that acting the part matters more than delivering results.
- **The three L's of delivering a review**: Level (be totally frank — the credibility of the whole system depends on it), Listen (use every sensory channel to confirm the message actually landed in the subordinate's head, not just that words were spoken), Leave yourself out (the review is about and for the subordinate — the supervisor's own anxiety, guilt, or need for validation has no place in it).
- **The worksheet method for "on the one hand" reviews**: brain-dump every observation about the subordinate onto a blank page without editing; then look for patterns connecting items into a small number of coherent "messages"; then select only as many messages as the subordinate can realistically absorb and remember (a person's capacity to process critical feedback in one sitting is finite — 3–4 real messages beats 7 diluted ones).
- **The stages of resolving a serious performance problem ("the blast")**: ignore → deny → blame others → assume responsibility → find solution. The hardest transition is from blaming others to assuming responsibility (an emotional step); once responsibility is assumed, finding a solution is comparatively easy (an intellectual step). Any outcome that includes genuine commitment to a course of action is acceptable — full agreement is not required, only real commitment.

## Key Concepts
- **The "reviewing the ace" bias**: Grove found that reviews of strong performers were well-written but rarely specified how they could improve further, while poor performers got detailed corrective-action plans — he argues this is backwards, since improving a star (a high-leverage activity, given stars' outsized share of total output) has greater organizational impact than marginal improvement of a weak performer.
- **Self-review as a bad substitute for supervisor judgment**: asking a subordinate to write his own review and then merely reformatting it undermines the supervisor's actual leadership function — the review must represent the supervisor's own independent, committed judgment.
- **Timing of delivery**: giving the written review before the face-to-face discussion (rather than during or after) lets the subordinate privately digest, react, and prepare, producing a more productive conversation than reading it live or delivering it verbally first.

## Mental Models
- Before finalizing any rating, ask explicitly: am I weighting output measures against internal measures deliberately, or defaulting to whichever is easiest to point to?
- When you find yourself with a "surprising" negative message on your worksheet, remember: if the review's purpose is genuinely to improve performance, you must deliver the surprising message rather than omit it for comfort.
- Distinguish an outcome you'd personally feel better about (full agreement) from the operational requirement (genuine commitment to act) — don't confuse your own emotional comfort with what the business actually needs.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rating a manager higher than the organization he runs performed**: "the performance rating of a manager cannot be higher than the one we would accord to his organization" — rewarding good form over bad results teaches the whole company to prioritize appearing competent over being effective.
- **Delivering laundry lists instead of a few clear messages**: overloading a subordinate with every observed data point produces sensory overload and improves nothing — less is genuinely more when the goal is retained, actionable feedback.
- **Skipping detailed improvement suggestions for top performers**: assuming a star "doesn't need" specific feedback wastes the highest-leverage improvement opportunity available to a manager.

## Worked Example
Grove reproduces an actual (lightly disguised) Intel performance review for "John Doe," annotated to show the framework in action: it opens with a job description, lists genuine accomplishments (production planning process significantly improved), then delivers specific, example-supported criticism (difficulty setting clear objectives and key results, mistaking "having a meeting" for progress on manufacturing specs), balanced against a genuine strength (finance background helping the Purchasing group). It explicitly ties a promotion decision to demonstrated independent results on complex projects, not stated potential, and is co-signed across the dual-reporting structure (immediate supervisor, approving supervisor, and the Material Managers' Council chair) — illustrating both the "level" and "output vs. internal measures" principles simultaneously.

## Key Takeaways
1. A review's purpose is improving future performance — treat every other reason (justifying pay, providing discipline, reinforcing culture) as secondary to that.
2. Weight output measures against internal measures deliberately, and account explicitly for the time offset between a subordinate's activity and when its output becomes visible.
3. Never rate potential, form, or appearance — a manager's rating cannot exceed what his organization's actual results earned.
4. Deliver only as many messages as a subordinate can genuinely absorb and act on; use Level, Listen, and Leave-yourself-out as the discipline for the conversation itself.
5. Invest disproportionate effort improving your strongest performers, not just your weakest — it is the higher-leverage activity.
