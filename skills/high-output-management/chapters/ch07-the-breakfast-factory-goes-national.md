# Chapter 7: The Breakfast Factory Goes National

## Core Idea
As soon as a single operation grows into a network of units, the centralization-vs-decentralization dichotomy becomes one of the most pervasive and recurring management questions — and it must be answered separately for each function (advertising, purchasing, quality, real estate, personnel) rather than resolved once for the whole organization.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The centralization–decentralization trade-off, function by function**: for every shared resource or activity (advertising, egg purchasing, quality standards, menu items, real estate, tableware, new-location decisions), ask separately whether local responsiveness or corporate economies of scale matters more — the answer differs by function even within the same company.
  - When to use: any time a single successful unit is being replicated or franchised into a network.
  - How: default to local control when local knowledge (neighborhood taste, local labor markets) drives better outcomes; default to central control when scale economies (bulk purchasing, specialized equipment, brand consistency) dominate; expect most real answers to be a regional compromise (regional egg-purchasing centers, regional warehouses) rather than either extreme.
- **The scaling paradox**: the very growth that created the opportunity (many successful franchise locations) also creates the coordination overhead (a corporate personnel manager, a traffic manager wanting a routing computer) that didn't exist when the founder ran one location and knew everyone by name.

## Key Concepts
- **Mission-oriented unit**: an operating unit responsible for and focused on a specific business/product/market outcome (each individual Breakfast Factory location).
- **Functional group**: a group organized around a specialized capability that serves multiple mission-oriented units (a centralized purchasing or personnel department).

## Mental Models
- When your organization scales, expect every previously implicit local decision (advertising, purchasing, real estate standards, hiring) to resurface explicitly as a centralize-or-not question — treat this as a normal, recurring by-product of growth, not a sign something has gone wrong.
- Ask, function by function: does responsiveness to local variation matter more here, or does scale economy matter more? Resist the urge to answer the question once for the whole organization.

## Anti-patterns
- **Assuming one answer fits every function**: fully centralizing everything (like the totally functional extreme) or fully decentralizing everything (the totally mission-oriented extreme) each optimizes one dimension while badly damaging the other — real organizations need a deliberate, per-function mix.
- **Nostalgia for the pre-growth state**: wishing to return to the days of running one location by hand is understandable but not actionable — the coordination complexity that comes with scale (a personnel manager, a traffic manager) is the direct, unavoidable cost of the growth that created the opportunity in the first place.

## Worked Example
Grove walks the Breakfast Factory Corporation function by function as it franchises nationally: advertising (local knowledge of "who reads the Daily Blatt" argues for local control, but a coherent national image argues for at least agency selection at the corporate level); egg purchasing (centralizing entirely in Chicago would sacrifice freshness, so the compromise is regional purchasing centers a few hours by truck from each franchise); quality standards (uniform and centrally monitored, because brand reputation depends on consistency everywhere); menu (a common core with regional discretion for local taste); real estate and tableware (centrally-set standards with regional warehousing for practical fulfillment). Each function lands in a different place on the centralization spectrum, illustrating that there is no single correct organizational answer — only a correct answer per function.

## Key Takeaways
1. Growth from a single unit into a network always resurfaces the centralization-decentralization question — expect it, don't treat it as dysfunction.
2. Resolve the question separately for each function; local responsiveness and scale economies pull in opposite directions and rarely resolve the same way twice.
3. Most real answers land as a regional compromise between full centralization and full local autonomy, not at either extreme.
4. The coordination overhead that appears with scale (new corporate functions, new managers) is the direct cost of the growth that created the opportunity — not a symptom of mismanagement.
