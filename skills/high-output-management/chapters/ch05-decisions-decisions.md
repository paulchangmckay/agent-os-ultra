# Chapter 5: Decisions, Decisions

## Core Idea
Good decision-making in a knowledge-based organization requires a deliberate three-stage process — free discussion, a clear decision, full support — precisely because position power and knowledge power diverge, and the biggest threats to this process are the peer-group syndrome and the fear of sounding dumb or being overruled.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The ideal decision-making model**: (1) free discussion — all views openly welcomed and debated, with "free" mattering most exactly when disagreement is greatest; (2) a clear decision — ambiguity to avoid controversy only postpones and inflames it; (3) full support from everyone involved — support means committing to back the decision, not necessarily agreeing with it.
  - When to use: any decision in a knowledge-power environment where the person with formal authority is not the most technically current.
  - How: run free discussion until you're confident all real (not superficial) viewpoints have surfaced, then push for consensus, and only if that fails does the senior person (or "peer-plus-one") use position power to decide — never earlier.
- **Position power vs. knowledge power**: in fast-changing, know-how-driven businesses, the person with formal authority is rarely the most technically current (managers get "a little more obsolete every day"), so a decision process must deliberately combine both types of power rather than default to hierarchy.
- **The peer-group syndrome**: a group of organizational equals talks in circles without noticing, because each person waits either for a senior person or for group consensus to emerge before risking an individual opinion. Fix: the "peer-plus-one" approach — bring in someone (often more senior, not necessarily most competent) whose explicit job is to break the stalemate and give the group confidence to proceed.
- **The six decision-structuring questions**, to be settled before or as a decision is made: What decision needs to be made? When does it have to be made? Who will decide? Who needs to be consulted first? Who will ratify or veto it? Who needs to be informed afterward?
  - How to apply: assign explicit answers to all six in advance for any consequential decision to avoid a late "Johnny-come-lately" veto that demoralizes everyone who invested in the process.
- **Lowest competent level**: decisions should be made as close as possible to the people who know the situation best — provided their technical knowledge is "tempered with judgment... developed from the many errors made in one's career," not raw expertise alone.

## Key Concepts
- **Fear of sounding dumb**: silences senior people from asking needed questions and junior people from voicing insights — each suppressed question or fact makes the eventual decision worse than it could have been.
- **Fear of being overruled**: keeps junior people from advocating a position, driven more by fear of losing face in front of peers than fear of formal sanction.
- **Status symbols and egalitarian style**: informal dress, no reserved parking, open offices at Intel are framed not as affectation but survival — visible hierarchy suppresses the free flow of facts and opinions needed when knowledge-power and position-power people must jointly decide.

## Mental Models
- Before pushing for a decision, ask: has genuine free discussion happened, or are people still performing consensus-seeking or waiting for a signal from the senior person present?
- When facing peer-group paralysis, deliberately invoke "peer-plus-one" rather than letting the group drift — someone with real stakes (or seniority) needs to explicitly take charge.
- Remember: "nobody has ever died from making a wrong business decision" — self-confidence to speak up in free discussion comes partly from internalizing that the downside of a wrong call is rarely as catastrophic as the fear suggests.

## Anti-patterns
- **Waiting to hear the boss's view before speaking**: an auto executive's account of employees who "wait until they hear their superiors express their view" before contributing produces systematically worse decisions, because the fullest information is withheld.
- **Obscuring a controversial decision to avoid an argument**: this only defers and intensifies the conflict — people who dislike a decision are angrier without a prompt, clear explanation.
- **The "John Wayne" fantasy of unilateral decision-making**: a manager who left Intel believing individual decision-making without consultation was superior returned four months later, having learned that unstructured autonomy at another company produced worse outcomes, not better ones.

## Worked Example
Grove walks through Intel's actual decision on where to expand its Philippine manufacturing plant, applying all six questions explicitly: the decision (build a high-rise next to the existing plant vs. a low-rise at a new site); the deadline (one month, derived by time-offsetting from a two-to-two-and-a-half-year capacity need); who decides (a balanced group combining the construction organization and the plant-management organization, since each has different sensitivities); who ratifies (Grove himself, given his familiarity with the Philippine operation, plus the president for a deal of that size); who is informed (Gordon Moore, the chairman, though not directly involved). After free discussion of land costs, construction costs, and traffic patterns, the group reached a compromise (build next to the existing plant, capped at four stories), which Grove ratified after probing the group's reasoning — demonstrating the full model end to end.

## Key Takeaways
1. Run free discussion to genuine completion before pushing toward a decision — position-power authority used too early is destructive, used too late wastes the benefit of open discussion.
2. Support does not require agreement — commitment to execute the decision is the only requirement, and it's the only thing a manager can legitimately expect from everyone.
3. Answer the six structuring questions (what, when, who decides, who's consulted, who ratifies, who's informed) explicitly and in advance to prevent late, demoralizing vetoes.
4. Watch for the peer-group syndrome whenever organizational equals meet to decide something — use "peer-plus-one" deliberately rather than letting the group drift in circles.
5. Egalitarian visible symbols (informal dress, shared space) are a practical tool for merging knowledge-power and position-power people into one effective decision process, not mere style.
