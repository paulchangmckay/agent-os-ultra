# Patterns & Techniques

## Find and Build Around the Limiting Step
**When to use**: designing or troubleshooting any multi-step process with a delivery deadline.
**How**: identify the slowest/most difficult/most expensive step; sequence and time-offset every other step backward from it so all components finish together, on time.
**Trade-offs**: requires re-identifying the limiting step whenever conditions change (e.g., toaster capacity replacing egg-boiling as the constraint) — a static plan built around yesterday's limiting step will misallocate effort.

## Inspect at the Lowest-Value Stage
**When to use**: any quality-control decision in a production or administrative flow.
**How**: reject defective material or work as early as possible — at receiving inspection, not final inspection — so less invested value is wasted when a defect is caught.
**Trade-offs**: requires investment in earlier-stage inspection capability; skipping it saves short-term cost but multiplies the cost of every downstream defect.

## Paired Indicators
**When to use**: whenever tracking any single quantity/output metric.
**How**: pair every output/quantity indicator with a counter-effect or quality indicator (inventory level + stockout rate; vouchers processed + errors found) so optimizing one doesn't silently damage the other.
**Trade-offs**: doubles the measurement and reporting overhead, but prevents the "steer where you're looking" over-optimization failure mode.

## Work Simplification
**When to use**: reducing the step-count and cost of any repeated administrative or production process.
**How**: (1) flowchart every actual step with no omissions; (2) count the steps; (3) target a 30–50% reduction; then question why each step exists and cut what only tradition justifies.
**Trade-offs**: requires an honest, unflattering flowchart of current practice — sanitizing the "as-is" picture beforehand defeats the exercise.

## Managerial Time as a Production Line
**When to use**: managing your own calendar and daily workload.
**How**: identify your true limiting steps (immovable commitments); batch similar tasks to amortize mental set-up time; use the calendar actively rather than as a passive order log; build in slack; say "no" early rather than aborting a commitment late.
**Trade-offs**: requires discipline to say no proactively, which feels less accommodating in the moment than accepting requests and later disappointing people.

## The Ideal Decision-Making Model
**When to use**: any consequential decision in a knowledge-power environment.
**How**: run free discussion until confident all real viewpoints have surfaced; reach and state a clear decision; secure full support (commitment to execute, not necessarily agreement) from everyone involved.
**Trade-offs**: takes longer than unilateral decision-making and requires genuine discipline not to invoke position-power authority too early — but produces better-informed, more durable decisions.

## The Six Decision-Structuring Questions
**When to use**: before or while making any decision with multiple stakeholders.
**How**: explicitly answer — what decision, by when, who decides, who's consulted first, who ratifies/vetoes, who's informed afterward.
**Trade-offs**: feels like added process overhead for a small decision, but prevents late, demoralizing vetoes and confused accountability on a large one.

## Peer-Plus-One
**When to use**: when a group of organizational equals is stuck in the peer-group syndrome, talking in circles without resolution.
**How**: bring in someone (often more senior, not necessarily most expert) whose explicit role is to break the stalemate and give the group confidence to decide.
**Trade-offs**: works only if the group and the peer-plus-one both trust the process — used as pure hierarchy imposition, it recreates the position-power-too-early failure mode.

## Three-Step Planning Process
**When to use**: any planning horizon, from a daily commute decision to an annual corporate plan.
**How**: (1) establish projected environmental demand; (2) establish present status (capabilities + pipeline, adjusted for realistic attrition); (3) reconcile the gap — the resulting actions are your strategy.
**Trade-offs**: requires keeping step 1 uncontaminated by step 2's constraints (don't let capacity fears shrink your demand estimate) or the true size of the gap never surfaces.

## MBO (Objective + Key Results)
**When to use**: pacing yourself or a subordinate on a specific goal over a defined period.
**How**: state one objective (where I want to go) supported by a small number of specific, dated key results (how I'll know I'm on pace); keep the total objective count small on purpose.
**Trade-offs**: meeting every key result doesn't guarantee meeting the objective (Columbus reached every milestone but never found the Asia route) — don't treat MBO as a rigid legal contract for grading performance.

## Function-by-Function Centralization Analysis
**When to use**: structuring a growing network of units (franchise-like operations, business divisions).
**How**: for each function (advertising, purchasing, quality, real estate), separately ask whether local responsiveness or scale economy matters more; expect most functions to land as a regional compromise, not a pure extreme.
**Trade-offs**: resolving every function separately is more work than a single company-wide policy, but a single policy sacrifices real value on whichever functions it gets wrong.

## Dual Reporting
**When to use**: a role that genuinely spans a technical specialty (best judged by a functional expert) and business-priority responsiveness (best judged by a local/divisional manager), with no single supervisor competent in both.
**How**: split supervision explicitly along its two dimensions; rely on strong shared corporate culture and trust to make the resulting ambiguity workable rather than dysfunctional.
**Trade-offs**: creates real ambiguity ("who's really my boss") that only functions with a genuinely shared culture underneath it — imposed without that trust, it degrades into turf conflict.

## Mode-of-Control Matching (CUA Matrix)
**When to use**: diagnosing why a governance approach isn't working for a given person or situation.
**How**: assess the person's motivation (self-interest vs. group-interest) and the environment's complexity/uncertainty/ambiguity (CUA); match to free-market forces (self-interest, low CUA), contractual obligations (moving toward group-interest), or cultural values (group-interest, high CUA).
**Trade-offs**: cultural-values control feels most desirable but only works with real, earned trust — imposing it prematurely on a new hire or low-trust group produces confusion, not cooperation.

## Capability/Motivation Diagnostic
**When to use**: any underperformance situation.
**How**: ask "if this person's life depended on doing the task, could they do it?" Yes → not motivated (fix the environment). No → not capable (fix via training).
**Trade-offs**: a quick heuristic, not a full diagnosis — real situations often mix both, but the test forces you to separate the two levers before reaching for either.

## Sports-Analogy Racetrack
**When to use**: chronic mediocre performance in a role with clear, measurable output that hasn't been made competitively visible.
**How**: create a visible scoring/comparison mechanism (e.g., a "building czar" scoring facility upkeep across locations) framed the way the workers themselves see their work.
**Trade-offs**: motivation from competition evaporates the moment the competition is removed (the merged-newspaper columnist) — the racetrack must be sustained, not a one-time intervention.

## Task-Relevant Maturity Style-Matching
**When to use**: choosing how tightly to supervise any subordinate on any specific task.
**How**: assess TRM for this task specifically (not general seniority); apply structured/task-oriented style at low TRM, individual-oriented/communicating style at medium TRM, minimal-involvement/objective-setting style at high TRM — always monitoring enough to avoid surprises.
**Trade-offs**: TRM can regress on a previously mastered task if the environment or pace changes abruptly — reapplying a high-TRM hands-off style after such a change under-supervises a now-lower-TRM situation.

## Output-vs-Internal-Measures Weighting
**When to use**: assessing any subordinate's performance for a review period.
**How**: weight plottable output measures against internal measures (team health, sustainability, positioning for future output) deliberately, and adjust for the typical time offset between activity and visible output in that specific role.
**Trade-offs**: no fixed formula exists (50/50, 90/10, or the reverse are all situationally valid) — the discipline is knowing which trade-off you're making, not finding a universal ratio.

## Worksheet Method for Delivering a Review
**When to use**: preparing a mixed positive/negative ("on the one hand... on the other hand") performance review.
**How**: brain-dump every observation without editing; look for patterns connecting items into a handful of coherent "messages"; select only as many messages as the subordinate can realistically absorb in one sitting.
**Trade-offs**: cutting real observations to fit the subordinate's absorption capacity means deferring some feedback to a later review — accept this rather than overloading the current one.

## Structured Interview by Four Categories
**When to use**: any hiring interview.
**How**: probe technical/skills knowledge, what the candidate did with that knowledge, discrepancies between capability and results, and operational values; steer toward ground both parties can actually judge (use a structured hypothetical if domain expertise doesn't overlap).
**Trade-offs**: still high-risk regardless of discipline — structure improves your odds of a good hire, it doesn't eliminate the fundamental information constraint of an hour-long conversation.

## Immediate-Response Retention Protocol
**When to use**: the moment a valued subordinate raises the possibility of resigning.
**How**: drop what you're doing immediately; let him talk fully without arguing; don't try to change his mind in the first conversation; escalate to your own supervisor as a shared problem; pursue every real avenue (including transfer) before accepting the loss.
**Trade-offs**: costs real, immediate time and disrupts your schedule — but any delay past the first mention itself confirms the employee's suspicion that he doesn't matter, making the eventual outcome worse.

## Recycling Over Exit
**When to use**: an employee promoted beyond their demonstrated capability, now underperforming.
**How**: openly move them back to a role they've proven they can excel in, framed as management's own misjudgment to correct, with public support rather than treating it as a hidden embarrassment.
**Trade-offs**: requires management to visibly own an error, which feels less clean than a quiet exit — but preserves a genuinely capable performer and avoids wasting the company's own past investment in them.
