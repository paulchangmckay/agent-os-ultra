## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

**A manager's output (Ch 3).** The single most important sentence in the book: *a manager's output is the output of the organizational units under his supervision or influence.* Activities (meetings, judgments, decisions) are not output — they're how output gets produced. Judge yourself and your subordinates by team/organizational results, never by visible busyness.

**Managerial leverage (Ch 3).** Managerial Output = Σ(Leverage × Activity) across all your activities. Productivity rises three ways: speed up an activity, raise its leverage, or shift your time-mix toward higher-leverage work. High leverage comes from one action affecting many people, a brief action affecting someone's work over a long time (a review, a tickler file), or unique knowledge affecting a large group. Negative leverage is just as real — meddling, waffling, insincere delegation, and contagious bad mood can destroy more output than any single positive-leverage activity creates.

**Task-relevant maturity, TRM (Ch 12).** There is no single best management style. The right style is a function of the subordinate's task-relevant maturity — achievement orientation, readiness, education, training, experience — *for this specific task*, not a fixed trait of the person. Low TRM → structured (tell what/when/how). Medium TRM → individual-oriented, two-way communication. High TRM → minimal involvement, set objectives and monitor. Always monitor enough to avoid surprises — that's the line between delegating and abdicating. TRM can regress if the task or environment suddenly changes.

**The ideal decision-making model (Ch 5).** Free discussion (all views genuinely aired, especially when disagreement is high) → a clear decision (ambiguity to avoid conflict only postpones it) → full support (commitment to execute, not necessarily agreement). Position-power authority is legitimate only once free discussion is exhausted and consensus fails — never earlier. Watch for the peer-group syndrome (a group of equals talking in circles, each waiting for a signal) and fix it with "peer-plus-one."

**Meetings are the medium of managerial work (Ch 4), not overhead.** Process-oriented meetings (one-on-ones, staff meetings, operation reviews) are regularly scheduled and handle routine information exchange; mission-oriented meetings are ad hoc and produce a specific decision. A well-run organization handles ~80% of issues in process-oriented meetings. A one-on-one is the *subordinate's* meeting — he sets the agenda; the supervisor's tool is "ask one more question."

**Planning and MBO (Ch 6).** Planning's only real output is the actions it triggers today — not the plan document. Three steps: establish environmental demand, establish present status, reconcile the gap (the reconciliation *is* your strategy). MBO pairs one objective (where I want to go) with a few specific, dated key results (how I'll know I'm getting there) — it's a pacing tool, not a legal contract for grading performance. Keep objective count small on purpose.

**Hybrid organizations and dual reporting (Ch 8, Ch 9).** Grove's Law: all large organizations with a common business purpose end up hybrid — part mission-oriented (responsive, decentralized), part functional (scaled, centralized). Dual reporting — one person answering to two supervisors along two real dimensions (technical standards vs. operational priorities) — is the mechanism that makes this work, but it requires real trust from a shared culture to function rather than degrade into confusion.

**Modes of control (Ch 10).** Behavior is governed by exactly three modes: free-market forces (self-interest, low complexity/uncertainty/ambiguity — no supervision needed), contractual obligations (rules + monitoring), or cultural values (group-interest, high CUA — requires earned trust). Match the mode to the person's motivation and the environment's CUA factor; using the wrong mode for the situation predictably fails.

**Motivation and the sports analogy (Ch 11).** Motivation must come from within — a manager can only build an environment where it flourishes. Maslow's hierarchy explains why people show up (physiological/safety/social) vs. why they perform once there (esteem/self-actualization). Self-actualization is boundless — it never extinguishes on satisfaction. Turning work into a visible, competitive "racetrack" (a scoring system, public comparison) taps this drive more reliably than money or pressure alone.

**Performance reviews (Ch 13).** The review's real purpose is improving future performance, not justifying a rating. Weight output measures (plottable results) against internal measures (team health, sustainability) deliberately, accounting for the time offset between activity and visible output. Never rate potential or form over substance — "the rating of a manager cannot be higher than the one we'd give his organization." Deliver with the three L's: Level, Listen, Leave yourself out.
