# Cheatsheet

## Decision Rules

| Situation | Do | Because |
|---|---|---|
| Choosing what to work on | Pick the highest-leverage activity, not the most urgent-feeling one | Managerial output = Σ(leverage × activity); shifting time-mix toward high-leverage work beats working faster |
| Deciding whether to intervene in a subordinate's work | Check his TRM for *this specific task* | TRM is task-specific, not a general trait — a star performer can have near-zero TRM on a genuinely new task |
| A subordinate isn't performing | Ask: "if his life depended on it, could he do it?" | Yes → motivation/environment problem. No → capability/training problem. Different fixes, don't guess |
| Deciding whether to call a meeting | Ask: what am I trying to accomplish? Is a meeting necessary? Desirable? Justifiable? | If any answer isn't yes, don't call it — a 10-person 2-hour meeting costs ~$2,000 |
| A group of peers is stuck talking in circles | Invoke peer-plus-one (bring in a senior/invested party to break the stalemate) | The peer-group syndrome — everyone waiting for consensus or a signal — won't self-resolve |
| Facing a controversial decision | Push through free discussion to genuine completion before deciding | Obscuring the decision to avoid an argument only postpones and inflates it |
| A subordinate mentions resigning | Drop everything immediately, listen fully, don't argue | Any delay confirms his belief that he isn't important to you |
| Rating a manager whose team missed every target | Rate no higher than the team's actual output | "The rating of a manager cannot be higher than the one we'd give his organization" — never reward form/potential |
| Deciding how much of pay should be at-risk bonus | Scale % upward with seniority (~10–25% middle manager, up to ~50% senior) | Absolute dollars matter less to senior people; bonus works better as a relative measure of achievement for them |
| Someone is promoted beyond their capability | Recycle them openly to a role they've proven they can do | Forcing them out wastes a real performer and hides management's own misjudgment |

## Decision Tree: Choosing a Management Style (Task-Relevant Maturity)

- **TRM low** (new to this specific task) → Structured: tell what, when, how.
- **TRM medium** (building competence) → Individual-oriented: two-way communication, support, mutual reasoning.
- **TRM high** (proven on this task) → Minimal: set objectives, then monitor.
- **Always, regardless of TRM** → Monitor enough to avoid surprises (the line between delegating and abdicating).
- **TRM was high, but the task/environment/pace just changed** → Revert to structured until TRM rebuilds for the new conditions.

## Decision Tree: Mode of Control to Use

- Self-interest + low CUA (complexity/uncertainty/ambiguity) → **Free-market forces**. No supervision needed.
- Motivation shifting toward group-interest → **Contractual obligations**. Set rules, monitor adherence.
- High group-interest + high CUA → **Cultural values**. Requires real trust from shared experience.
- Self-interest + high CUA → **No mode works well.** Expect chaos; this combination has no good fix.
- New hire → assign low-CUA, clearly structured work (matches typical self-interest starting point); raise CUA as shared experience builds.

## Trade-off Matrix: Centralize or Decentralize a Function?

| Function type | Lean toward | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Needs local market/taste knowledge | Mission-oriented (local) | Local responsiveness is the one real advantage of decentralization |
| Benefits from expensive shared equipment/expertise | Functional (central) | Economies of scale; idle capacity if duplicated per unit |
| Brand/quality consistency matters everywhere | Functional (central), monitored | Any one unit's failure damages the whole brand |
| Ambiguous, high-volume resource conflicts | Middle managers, not top allocators | Central allocation of everything breaks down under real transaction volume (the Hungary failure mode) |

## Thresholds & Defaults

- **Subordinates per primarily-supervisory manager**: 6–8 (roughly half a day/week each). 3–4 is too few; 10+ is too many.
- **One-on-one minimum length**: ~1 hour. Anything less pushes subordinates toward only raising simple issues.
- **Meetings consuming your time**: >25% in ad hoc, mission-oriented meetings specifically signals your process-oriented meeting system is failing — not just "too many meetings" in general.
- **Mission-oriented decision-meeting attendance cap**: 6–8 people, 8 absolute max. Decision-making is not a spectator sport.
- **Manager's fully-loaded time cost**: ~$100/hour (Grove's estimate) — use this to size-check whether a meeting is worth its cost.
- **Work simplification first-pass target**: 30–50% step reduction is realistic on a first honest pass.
- **MBO objective count**: keep it small on purpose — too many objectives functions the same as having none.
- **MBO stretch target**: set objectives so even strong effort yields roughly 50/50 odds of full success.
- **Performance bonus size**: ~10–25% of total comp for middle managers; up to ~50% for senior managers.
- **Visual aid pacing in a presentation**: ~4 minutes of discussion per visual aid, as a rule of thumb.

## Tells & Smells

- **"He has to make his own mistakes, that's how he learns"** → red flag: the tuition is being paid by the subordinate's customers, internal or external. The supervisor still owns the teaching responsibility.
- **A manager's self-rated style is "communicating/delegating"** → about 90% of the time, subordinates rate the same manager as more structured/controlling than the manager believes. Verify against subordinate perception, don't trust self-assessment.
- **Excellent output measures but rising turnover/grumbling** → possible time-offset illusion: today's good output may reflect last year's work, while today's actual performance is already declining (the "light from distant stars" trap).
- **A review packed with 7 different critical points** → sensory overload; a person can typically absorb only a handful of messages in one sitting. Cut to the most important ones and defer the rest.
- **A star performer's review has no suggestions for further improvement** → likely a missed high-leverage opportunity; Grove found managers systematically under-coach top performers relative to poor ones.
- **A subordinate objects "you're only doing this because I forced your hand"** (after a retention save) → address it directly rather than avoiding it — reframe as a mutual wake-up call, not blackmail.
- **A group of organizational peers keeps circling the same point with no resolution** → classic peer-group syndrome; someone is waiting for a senior signal, someone else for consensus. Bring in peer-plus-one.
- **A senior manager praised as "he handles himself well" despite missed targets** → the "potential trap" / form-over-substance; do not rate above what the organization's actual output would earn.
