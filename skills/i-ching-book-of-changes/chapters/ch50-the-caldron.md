# Hexagram 50: Ting / The Caldron

## Core Idea
The vessel and act of nourishing what is highest — feeding able people, culture, and the spirit — through refinement rather than raw abundance.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Li (Fire)
- Lower: Sun (Wood, the Gentle)

## The Judgment
> THE CALDRON. Supreme good fortune. Success.

**Meaning**: When the visible material of life (wood) is offered up to feed the invisible (fire, spirit), civilization reaches its culmination — this consecration brings great good fortune.

## The Image
> Fire over wood: The image of THE CALDRON. Thus the superior man consolidates his fate By making his position correct.

**Meaning**: Just as fire depends on wood beneath it to keep burning, a person secures their fate by putting life and destiny into right relationship — getting one's position correct sustains the flame.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "A ting with legs upturned. Furthers removal of stagnating stuff. One takes a concubine for the sake of her son. No blame." — Even a lowly start is no fault if it clears out what's stagnant and opens a path to genuine merit.
- **Nine in the second place**: "There is food in the ting. My comrades are envious, But they cannot harm me. Good fortune." — Focus on real achievement; envy from others cannot touch someone anchored in substance.
- **Nine in the third place**: "The handle of the ting is altered. One is impeded in his way of life. The fat of the pheasant is not eaten. Once rain falls, remorse is spent. Good fortune comes in the end." — Being unrecognized blocks your effectiveness for a time, but genuine inner substance eventually finds its release.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "The legs of the ting are broken. The prince's meal is spilled And his person is soiled. Misfortune." — Taking on responsibility beyond your character or strength, and keeping poor company, brings failure and disgrace.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "The ting has yellow handles, golden carrying rings. Perseverance furthers." — A modest, approachable leader attracts able helpers; hold to that humility rather than straying from it.
- **Nine at the top**: "The ting has rings of jade. Great good fortune. Nothing that would not act to further." — Wisdom offered with mildness and purity (jade) benefits everyone it touches.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan reads the whole hexagram as a literal picture of a ting from legs to carrying rings, and frames its purpose as twofold: sacrifice to God and nourishment of the worthy and holy.
- Gentleness (Sun) sharpening eye and ear (Li) is what lets the yielding fifth line rise and find correspondence with the firm second line — the mechanism behind "supreme success."
- The Image contrasts this hexagram with The Well (48): The Well nourishes the people broadly, while the Caldron nourishes the cultured and worthy specifically, through refined ceremony rather than sheer volume.
- Confucius's aside on the broken-legs line (nine in the fourth) makes explicit the moral: weak character in an honored place, meager knowledge with large plans, and limited power with heavy responsibility rarely escape disaster.

## Worked Example
Consider the six in the fifth place: "The ting has yellow handles, golden carrying rings. Perseverance furthers." This is the ruling line of the hexagram — a receptive, modest leader in the central position. The Book III commentary explains that this line's "hollow" (yielding) nature is precisely what lets it receive the wisdom of the sage above it (the nine at the top), and that the handle and ear share the same character, hinting the leader must listen. In practice: a person in authority who wants able people's real help must stay open and self-effacing rather than assert control — that openness is what draws the counsel that makes the whole enterprise work.

## Key Takeaways
- Real achievement is the best defense against envy — do not fight jealousy directly, let substance speak.
- If you feel unrecognized despite genuine capability, hold your inner quality steady; recognition follows in time.
- Do not accept responsibility that exceeds your character or preparation — mismatch here brings public failure.
- Stay approachable and modest in leadership; that stance is what attracts the capable people you need.
- Wisdom should be given as jade is worn — softly, without forcing — so it is received rather than resisted.

## Connects To
- Ching (48), The Well — explicitly paired in the Book III sequence and contrasted in meaning: The Well nourishes the people, the Caldron nourishes the worthy and refined.
- Ko (49), Revolution — structurally inverse to this hexagram; Book III notes Ko shows revolution's negative, destructive aspect while Ting shows the correct, constructive way to reorganize.
