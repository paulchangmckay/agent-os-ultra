# Hexagram 28: Ta Kuo / Preponderance of the Great

## Core Idea
Four strong lines packed inside two weak outer lines picture a ridgepole thick and heavy in the middle but too weak at its ends — a load excessive for its supports, an exceptional, unstable condition demanding extraordinary, urgent, gentle action rather than force.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Tui, the lake
- Lower: Sun, wind/tree

## The Judgment
> PREPONDERANCE OF THE GREAT. The ridgepole sags to the breaking point. It furthers one to have somewhere to go. Success.

**Meaning**: The load is too heavy for its supports and demands quick transitional action; because the excess strength sits centrally rather than causing revolution, gentle penetration to the meaning of the situation, not forceful measures, brings success.

## The Image
> The lake rises above the trees: the image of PREPONDERANCE OF THE GREAT. Thus the superior man, when he stands alone, is unconcerned, and if he has to renounce the world, he is undaunted.

**Meaning**: Like floodtimes when the lake rises over the treetops, extraordinary times are temporary; the superior person stands firm alone like a tree and remains joyously undaunted even when the world must be renounced.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "To spread white rushes underneath. No blame." — In extraordinary times, extreme caution in laying a foundation — even to the point of seeming excessive — is never a mistake.
- **Nine in the second place**: "A dry poplar sprouts at the root. An older man takes a young wife. Everything furthers." — An extraordinary reanimation of growth, however unusual the situation, turns out entirely favorable, as with joining the lowly for renewal in exceptional times.
- **Nine in the third place**: "The ridgepole sags to the breaking point. Misfortune." — Someone who insists on pushing ahead in a time of excess, accepting no advice and thus receiving no support, only hastens the breaking of the structure.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "The ridgepole is braced. Good fortune. If there are ulterior motives, it is humiliating." — Friendly relations with those of lower rank let a responsible person master a difficult situation, but misusing those connections for personal power turns success into humiliation.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "A withered poplar puts forth flowers. An older woman takes a husband. No blame. No praise." — Flowering exhausts a withered poplar's last energy rather than renewing it; the anomaly is observed correctly but produces no real, lasting good.
- **Six at the top**: "One must go through the water. It goes over one's head. Misfortune. No blame." — Courageously pressing on despite danger that proves fatal carries misfortune, but no blame, because giving up one's life for the sake of the good and the right is a value beyond mere survival.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram's rulers are the nine in the second place (firm, central, not too heavy) and the nine in the fourth place (a beam that does not sag to the breaking point) — together the two lines that manage the excess strength successfully.
- The Commentary explains "preponderance" structurally: the four yang lines outnumber the two yin lines, but what makes it "preponderance of the great" specifically is that the great sits within while by nature belonging without — the opposite structural pattern from hexagram 62 (Preponderance of the Small), where weak lines fill the outer positions.
- The Appended Judgments trace ancient burial practice to this hexagram's structure — two yin lines (earth and burial-site trees) surrounding yang lines (the coffin) — framing this hexagram as the counterpart to the preceding one (Corners of the Mouth): nourishment for the living there, care for the dead here.
- The extensive Note explains a governing pattern unique to this hexagram (shared with hexagrams 27, 61, and 62): ordinary line correspondence doesn't apply; instead, lines are lucky when their place opposes their nature (yielding in a firm place, or firm in a yielding place) and unlucky when place and nature match, since matching creates overweighting rather than balance.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fourth place: "The ridgepole is braced. Good fortune. If there are ulterior motives, it is humiliating." The commentary contrasts this directly with the third line: the third is a firm line in a firm place (too much rigidity, prone to breaking), while the fourth is a firm line in a yielding place, whose firmness is tempered by its position's flexibility, giving it real security. This deepens the Judgment's core insight about times of preponderance: strength alone doesn't produce good outcomes — it's strength moderated by the right kind of placement and relationship (here, friendly ties to those below, used for the good of the whole rather than personal advantage) that braces the structure instead of breaking it.

## Key Takeaways
- In exceptional, high-stakes situations, exaggerated caution at the outset is never wasted effort.
- Address structural imbalance with movement and adaptation, not rigid insistence on one's own way — obstinacy in a crisis accelerates collapse.
- Use relationships and support from others to bear excessive load, but never exploit them for personal gain — that turns strength into humiliation.
- Recognize when an apparent renewal (or late flowering) is actually just the last of a spent force, not real, lasting growth.
- Some outcomes carry genuine misfortune yet remain blameless — when the cause is right, the willingness to risk everything is itself noble, not a failure.
