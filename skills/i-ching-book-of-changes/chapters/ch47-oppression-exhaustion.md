# Hexagram 47: K'un / Oppression (Exhaustion)

## Core Idea
Adversity, like a lake drained of its water, is a fate that must be borne — but if it befalls the right person, the very pressure of exhaustion becomes the source of an inner stability stronger than the fate itself.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Tui (the Lake, the Joyous)
- Lower: K'an (Water, the Abysmal, Danger)

## The Judgment
> OPPRESSION. Success. Perseverance. The great man brings about good fortune. No blame. When one has something to say, it is not believed.

**Meaning**: Times of adversity are the reverse of success, yet they can lead to success for the right person — a strong man remains cheerful despite danger, and this cheerfulness itself is the source of later triumph; only the great man, strong within and sparing of words while his outward influence is denied, achieves this.

## The Image
> There is no water in the lake: the image of EXHAUSTION. Thus the superior man stakes his life on following his will.

**Meaning**: When the water has flowed out and the lake dries up, this is simply fate — and in such times there is nothing to do but acquiesce to the outer circumstance while remaining true, at the deepest level, to oneself.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "One sits oppressed under a bare tree and strays into a gloomy valley. For three years one sees nothing." — Weakness in the face of adversity lets a man sink into ever-deepening gloom and hopelessness, an inner delusion that must be overcome rather than accepted.
- **Nine in the second place**: "One is oppressed while at meat and drink. The man with the scarlet knee bands is just coming. It furthers one to offer sacrifice. To set forth brings misfortune. No blame." — Even amid apparent comfort, exhaustion from the commonplaces of life sets in; help approaches from a high place, but obstructions remain, so patient inward preparation, not premature action, is called for.
- **Six in the third place**: "A man permits himself to be oppressed by stone, and leans on thorns and thistles. He enters his house and does not see his wife. Misfortune." — Restless and indecisive, pushing recklessly against real obstructions and leaning on unstable supports, one arrives home only to find fresh disappointment.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "He comes very quietly, oppressed in a golden carriage. Humiliation, but the end is reached." — A well-meaning man, wishing to help those below him, hesitates and becomes entangled with powerful associates, causing embarrassment; but his underlying strength of character still brings the goal within reach.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "His nose and feet are cut off. Oppression at the hands of the man with the purple knee bands. Joy comes softly. It furthers one to make offerings and libations." — Oppressed from both above and below, finding no help even from those whose duty is to assist, one must turn to inner composure and prayer while things gradually improve.
- **Six at the top**: "He is oppressed by creeping vines. He moves uncertainly and says, 'Movement brings remorse.' If one feels remorse over this and makes a start, good fortune comes." — Bound by ties that could easily be broken, hesitating out of habitual fear, a firm decision to move breaks the remaining oppression and brings good fortune.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The Appended Judgments state directly: "OPPRESSION is the test of character. OPPRESSION leads to perplexity and thereby to success. Through OPPRESSION one learns to lessen one's rancor" — adversity is framed not merely as something to survive but as a formative discipline with its own positive function.
- The Commentary on the Decision explains the hexagram's core dynamic through its trigrams: "Danger and joyousness. The superior man alone is capable of being oppressed without losing the power to succeed" — the combination of K'an's danger below and Tui's joyousness above is read as showing that cheerfulness sustained through real danger, not the absence of danger, is what defines the great man here.
- The Judgment's line "when one has something to say, it is not believed" is explained structurally: Tui suggests speech, but K'an means "earache," i.e., a structural disinclination to listen — the hexagram encodes its own warning about being unheard directly into the trigram symbolism.
- The Sequence explicitly ties this hexagram to the one before it: "If one pushes upward without stopping, he is sure to meet with oppression" — exhaustion is presented as the natural, almost inevitable consequence of unchecked ascent (Hexagram 46).

## Worked Example
Consider the nine in the fifth place: "His nose and feet are cut off. Oppression at the hands of the man with the purple knee bands. Joy comes softly. It furthers one to make offerings and libations." Someone in a position of responsibility — trying to do right by people depending on them — may find themselves blocked on every side, unable to make progress upward or downward, and unsupported even by those who should be natural allies. This line does not promise quick relief; it counsels turning inward, toward composure and something like ritual patience, while trusting that improvement will come "softly," incrementally. It deepens the Judgment's core claim that the great man succeeds in oppression not by escaping it but by remaining inwardly steady and outwardly restrained until conditions shift.

## Key Takeaways
- When adversity strikes, guard against letting weakness turn into a self-reinforcing spiral of gloom — the danger often comes more from inner delusion than from the circumstance itself.
- If help is approaching but real obstacles remain, prepare and wait rather than setting out prematurely.
- Avoid leaning on unstable supports or forcing your way through real obstruction out of restlessness — it compounds the difficulty rather than resolving it.
- When your position entangles you with associates who slow your good intentions, recognize the humiliation as temporary; underlying strength of character still reaches the goal.
- When you feel bound by something that could easily be released, notice when hesitation itself has become the obstacle — a firm decision to move is often what finally breaks the oppression.
