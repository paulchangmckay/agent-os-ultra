# Hexagram 64: Wei Chi / Before Completion

## Core Idea
The final hexagram of the Book of Changes: a time of transition not yet complete, where success is genuinely possible but requires the deliberate caution of an old fox crossing ice — and where every ending opens into a new beginning.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Li (Fire)
- Lower: K'an (Water, the Abysmal)

## The Judgment
> BEFORE COMPLETION. Success. But if the little fox, after nearly completing the crossing, Gets his tail in the water, There is nothing that would further.

**Meaning**: Leading the world from confusion back to order is a difficult, weighty task that promises success — but only with the deliberation and caution of an old fox on ice; a young fox's overconfident final rush risks undoing the whole crossing at the very last step.

## The Image
> Fire over water: The image of the condition before transition. Thus the superior man is careful In the differentiation of things, So that each finds its place.

**Meaning**: Fire flaring upward and water flowing downward move in opposite, unrelated directions; achieving real effect requires first understanding each force's proper nature and place — and getting one's own standpoint correct before trying to arrange anything else.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "He gets his tail in the water. Humiliating." — Rushing to accomplish something visible before the time is truly ripe leads only to failure; holding back spares you the humiliation of a premature attempt.
- **Nine in the second place**: "He brakes his wheels. Perseverance brings good fortune." — Real patience here means actively restraining your own strength while still developing toward the goal, not passive waiting — hold the brake without losing sight of where you're headed.
- **Six in the third place**: "Before completion, attack brings misfortune. It furthers one to cross the great water." — Lacking the strength to force a transition alone, seek out capable allies and take the decisive step together rather than trying to push through by yourself.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "Perseverance brings good fortune. Remorse disappears. Shock, thus to discipline the Devil's Country. For three years, great realms are awarded." — The time for real struggle has come; silence your misgivings, commit fully to the fight against decay, and the effort lays the foundation for lasting reward.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Perseverance brings good fortune. No remorse. The light of the superior man is true. Good fortune." — Victory achieved through genuine steadfastness allows a true leader's light to shine forth again, drawing people who trust it — the new era arrives all the more brilliant against the old misery.
- **Nine at the top**: "There is drinking of wine In genuine confidence. No blame. But if one wets his head, He loses it, in truth." — Celebrating a new era's arrival among trusted friends is blameless, but only within proper bounds — losing moderation in that celebration forfeits the good the moment offered.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan finds hope in the hexagram's underlying structure despite its outward disorder: although no line sits in its "correct" place, all the firm and yielding lines still correspond to one another — order is preformed within even though it doesn't yet show on the surface.
- Book III explicitly frames this hexagram as both the inverse and opposite of Chi Chi, After Completion (63): the same two trigrams, K'an and Li, have simply swapped positions, marking the transition from P'i, Standstill (12), back toward T'ai, Peace (11).
- The nine in the second line's caution is explained as the mirror of the nine at the beginning in the previous hexagram — the same wheel-and-braking image, but here applied to a stronger, more centrally placed line, making the outlook more favorable.
- A closing editorial note observes that the Book of Changes deliberately ends on this open, hopeful hexagram — "every end contains a new beginning" — rather than on a note of final resolution, giving the whole work a future-facing character; it further notes the Miscellaneous Notes on the Hexagrams close on a related note with Kuai, Break-Through (43): "the way of the superior man is in the ascendant."

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fourth place: "Perseverance brings good fortune. Remorse disappears. Shock, thus to discipline the Devil's Country. For three years, great realms are awarded." Book III notes this line mirrors the third line's "disciplining of the Devil's Country" from the previous hexagram (63), but with a far better outcome — there, three years of fighting; here, three years of being rewarded with great realms. The commentary explains this line represents a strong minister assisting the gentle ruler in the fifth place, genuinely carrying out the ruler's will. Applied practically: when a real struggle against entrenched decay or dysfunction is unavoidable, committing to it fully — silencing second-guessing — is what turns hardship into lasting foundation, provided the effort serves a legitimate, larger purpose rather than personal ambition alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Do not rush to force a premature conclusion; the caution of an old, experienced fox — not the boldness of a young one — is what actually completes a difficult crossing.
- Practice active patience: restrain your own momentum without losing sight of the goal, rather than either forcing action or drifting passively.
- When you lack the strength to complete a transition alone, actively seek capable allies rather than trying to force it single-handedly.
- When real struggle against decay becomes necessary, commit fully and silence hesitation — this is the moment that lays the foundation for lasting gain.
- Celebrate a hard-won new beginning, but keep it within bounds; losing moderation at the threshold of success can still forfeit what you've earned.

## Connects To
- Chi Chi (63), After Completion — the hexagram immediately preceding this one and its explicit structural inverse and opposite; where that hexagram moves from Peace toward Standstill, this one moves from Standstill back toward Peace, closing the Book of Changes on an open, hopeful note.
- Kuai (43), Break-Through — noted in Book III as the hexagram placed at the end of the Miscellaneous Notes sequence, closing on a related theme of the superior man's way ascending.
