# Hexagram 63: Chi Chi / After Completion

## Core Idea
A moment of perfect equilibrium, where every element sits exactly in its proper place — a triumph that is dangerous precisely because it is complete, since any further movement can only unravel it back into disorder.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'an (Water, the Abysmal)
- Lower: Li (Fire, Clarity)

## The Judgment
> AFTER COMPLETION. Success in small matters. Perseverance furthers. At the beginning good fortune, At the end disorder.

**Meaning**: The great transition is already accomplished; what remains is only attention to detail. But precisely because things now seem to run of their own accord, complacency becomes the root of decay — only sustained caution avoids the disorder that naturally follows completion.

## The Image
> Water over fire: the image of the condition In AFTER COMPLETION. Thus the superior man Takes thought of misfortune And arms himself against it in advance.

**Meaning**: Fire and water in balance generate power but are fundamentally hostile forces — if the water boils over, the fire dies; if the fire flares too high, the water evaporates. Only the sage recognizes the hidden danger in a moment of apparent perfect harmony and prepares for it in advance.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "He brakes his wheels. He gets his tail in the water. No blame." — When everyone else surges forward in the excitement of new progress, checking your own pace in time keeps you from real harm, even if you're touched slightly by the general momentum.
- **Six in the second place**: "The woman loses the curtain of her carriage. Do not run after it; On the seventh day you will get it." — When those in power withhold the trust or recognition you need, do not chase after it or throw yourself at the world seeking it; developing your own worth quietly, what is truly yours returns in its own time.
- **Nine in the third place**: "The Illustrious Ancestor Disciplines the Devil's Country. After three years he conquers it. Inferior people must not be employed." — After a great achievement, the expansion or campaign that follows must be pursued with real, capable people and sustained effort — not treated as a dumping ground for those unfit at home.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "The finest clothes turn to rags. Be careful all day long." — Even amid flourishing conditions, occasional glimpses of hidden trouble are grave warnings the thoughtful person does not dismiss just because the surface still looks fine.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "The neighbor in the east who slaughters an ox Does not attain as much real happiness As the neighbor in the west With his small offering." — Elaborate, showy devotion without real sincerity behind it is worth less than a modest offering given with genuine, timely piety.
- **Six at the top**: "He gets his head in the water. Danger." — Having safely crossed a danger, turning back to admire the peril overcome is what actually draws you back into it; only continuing forward without looking back avoids the risk.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan explains the paradox of the Judgment structurally: every single line sits in its correct place — unique among all sixty-four hexagrams — which is exactly why "any deviation brings misfortune"; there is nowhere left to go but astray.
- Book III identifies this hexagram as the direct evolution of T'ai, Peace (11), and warns that its very completeness sets up the reversion into P'i, Standstill (12) — perfect order contains the seed of its own undoing.
- The commentary reads K'an (danger, striving downward) and Li (clarity, striving upward) as fundamentally opposed forces held in unstable balance — the tension itself is the source of both the hexagram's power and its fragility.
- The fifth line's "small offering" versus "slaughtered ox" contrast is explained structurally: the central, correctly-placed six in the second line (offering little, but at the right time) achieves more real happiness than the off-center six in the fourth (offering much, but out of place) — centrality and timing outweigh grandeur.

## Worked Example
Take the six at the top: "He gets his head in the water. Danger." Book III frames this as the final, culminating warning of the hexagram — the head is the counterpart to the first line's tail, and getting it wet means turning back mid-crossing rather than continuing forward. The commentary asks pointedly, "how can one endure this for long?" — the danger isn't the crossing itself, but the backward glance, the "vain self-admiration" of dwelling on a peril already overcome. Applied practically: once you've successfully navigated a difficult transition, the temptation to relive or revisit that danger — rather than simply continuing onward — is itself what pulls you back into risk. Completion is not a place to linger and admire; it's a place to keep moving through carefully.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat a moment of apparent perfect success as inherently unstable — the very completeness invites complacency, which is the actual danger.
- When your effort surges past what circumstances call for, rein yourself in early; overshooting invites the setback you're trying to avoid.
- If recognition or trust from above is withheld, resist the urge to chase it; quietly building real worth brings it back in its own time.
- Do not dismiss small signs of trouble just because the overall situation still looks prosperous — they are your best early warning.
- Once you've navigated a real danger successfully, keep moving forward rather than looking back at it — dwelling on a past peril is what draws you back into danger.

## Connects To
- Wei Chi (64), Before Completion — the final hexagram of the Book of Changes, explicitly the structural inverse and opposite of this one: where this hexagram moves from Peace (11) toward Standstill (12), Wei Chi moves from Standstill (12) back toward Peace (11).
- T'ai (11), Peace — Book III identifies this hexagram as the direct evolution of Peace, its perfected but unstable culmination.
