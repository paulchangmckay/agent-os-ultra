# Hexagram 48: Ching / The Well

## Core Idea
Political and social forms change, but the well — the structure that meets humanity's deepest, most basic needs — remains constant; the well symbolizes an inexhaustible source of nourishment available equally to all who come to draw from it.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'an (Water, the Abysmal)
- Lower: Sun (Wood/Wind, Gentleness, Penetration)

## The Judgment
> THE WELL. The town may be changed, but the well cannot be changed. It neither decreases nor increases. They come and go and draw from the well. If one gets down almost to the water and the rope does not go all the way, or the jug breaks, it brings misfortune.

**Meaning**: The well represents the unchanging foundation of human life beneath all changing political forms; but a satisfactory social order requires going all the way down to life's true foundations — a superficial or careless approach (a rope too short, a broken jug) is as disastrous as never attempting order at all.

## The Image
> Water over wood: the image of THE WELL. Thus the superior man encourages the people at their work, and exhorts them to help one another.

**Meaning**: Just as wood draws water upward to nourish every part of the plant, the superior man organizes society so that all its parts cooperate for the benefit of the whole.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "One does not drink the mud of the well. No animals come to an old well." — A man who wanders into swampy neglect and lets his life sink into uselessness is no longer sought out by anyone; time passes him by.
- **Nine in the second place**: "At the wellhole one shoots fishes. The jug is broken and leaks." — Good qualities left unused and neglected cause a person to deteriorate, drift toward inferior company, and accomplish nothing worthwhile despite their underlying capacity.
- **Nine in the third place**: "The well is cleaned, but no one drinks from it. This is my heart's sorrow, for one might draw from it. If the king were clear-minded, good fortune might be enjoyed in common." — A capable, prepared person goes unused, a sorrow to those who recognize his worth; if only those in authority became clear-minded enough to draw on him, the benefit would be shared by all.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "The well is being lined. No blame." — Though temporarily unusable while under repair, this inward work is not wasted; enhancing one's own capabilities now enables greater accomplishment for others later.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "In the well there is a clear, cold spring from which one can drink." — A person of this quality is born to be a leader and source of life for others, though the crucial point remains that such water must actually be drawn and used, not merely admired as potential.
- **Six at the top**: "One draws from the well without hindrance. It is dependable. Supreme good fortune." — The well's water is available to all who come, dependable and never running dry — as with a truly great man, the more people draw from him, the greater his inner wealth becomes.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The Appended Judgments state: "THE WELL shows the field of character... THE WELL brings about discrimination as to what is right" — the hexagram is explicitly grouped with Hexagram 5 (Waiting), Hexagram 27 (The Corners of the Mouth), and Hexagram 50 (The Caldron) as hexagrams centrally concerned with nourishment.
- The Commentary on the Decision explains the Judgment's two-part structure through the trigrams directly: the upper trigram K'an (the well) accounts for the unchanging first half, while Sun (rope), the nuclear trigram Li (hollow vessel), and the nuclear trigram Tui (breaking) together account for the warning about a broken jug in the second half.
- The Image is extended into a specific historical institution: the ancient agrarian well-field system, in which eight families' fields surrounded a shared ninth field containing the well and settlement, cultivated cooperatively for the common government — grounding the hexagram's cooperative ideal in a concrete social structure.
- The text notes that, unlike the Judgment's overall note of danger, the six individual lines show "ever increasing clarification and auspiciousness" from bottom to top — the hexagram's structure itself traces a progression from neglect (mud, no visitors) to full, dependable usefulness (supreme good fortune).

## Worked Example
Consider the nine in the third place: "The well is cleaned, but no one drinks from it. This is my heart's sorrow, for one might draw from it. If the king were clear-minded, good fortune might be enjoyed in common." Someone who has done the work to become genuinely capable — skilled, prepared, ready to contribute — may still find themselves overlooked by those in a position to make use of them. This line does not counsel the capable person to stop preparing or to force recognition; it names the situation honestly as a shared sorrow, and locates the missing piece in the clear-mindedness of whoever holds the authority to draw on that readiness. It extends the Judgment's warning about care and depth: even a well properly dug and cleaned still depends on someone actually lowering the rope all the way.

## Key Takeaways
- Do not let your own capability go to waste through neglect — an unused well fills with mud, and the world eventually stops seeking it out.
- If you are prepared and capable but currently unused, recognize this as a shared loss, not a personal failure, and keep the well maintained for when it is finally drawn upon.
- Treat a period of necessary internal repair or development as valuable in itself, even when it makes you temporarily unavailable to others.
- Judge value by whether it is actually put to use, not merely by its potential — the clearest, coldest spring means nothing until someone drinks from it.
- Aim to become a source others can draw from without limit — true value, unlike material wealth, only grows the more it is shared.
