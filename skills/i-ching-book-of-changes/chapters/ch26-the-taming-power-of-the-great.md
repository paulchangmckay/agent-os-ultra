# Hexagram 26: Ta Ch'u / The Taming Power of the Great

## Core Idea
Great strength is tamed and stored up when two weak lines — a minister and a prince — restrain four strong lines, giving this hexagram a threefold meaning: holding together, holding back, and caring for and nourishing what is genuinely worthy.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Kên, the mountain (keeping still)
- Lower: Ch'ien, heaven (the Creative)

## The Judgment
> THE TAMING POWER OF THE GREAT. Perseverance furthers. Not eating at home brings good fortune. It furthers one to cross the great water.

**Meaning**: Storing up great creative power requires a strong, clear-headed person honored by the ruler, whose daily self-renewal of character keeps him at the height of his powers; because such a person is in harmony with heaven, even great and difficult undertakings succeed.

## The Image
> Heaven within the mountain: the image of THE TAMING POWER OF THE GREAT. Thus the superior man acquaints himself with many sayings of antiquity and many deeds of the past, in order to strengthen his character thereby.

**Meaning**: The hidden treasure of past words and deeds should not be mere historical knowledge but should be actively applied to strengthen and elevate one's own character.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Danger is at hand. It furthers one to desist." — Facing an obstacle to vigorous advance, it is better to compose oneself and wait than to force a dangerous advance.
- **Nine in the second place**: "The axletrees are taken from the wagon." — Where the restraining force is absolutely superior, one submits and waits rather than struggles, letting energy accumulate for a later, vigorous advance.
- **Nine in the third place**: "A good horse that follows others. Awareness of danger, with perseverance, furthers. Practice chariot driving and armed defense daily. It furthers one to have somewhere to go." — With the hindrance cleared and a strong ally in the same direction, one advances, but must stay aware of remaining danger and keep both forward skill and defensive readiness sharp.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "The headboard of a young bull. Great good fortune." — Restraining wild force is best done by forestalling it early, before the danger has fully developed — an easy and great success.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "The tusk of a gelded boar. Good fortune." — Rather than combating wild force directly, its roots should be eradicated indirectly, altering the nature of the threat itself.
- **Nine at the top**: "One attains the way of heaven. Success." — When the time of obstruction passes, long dammed-up energy achieves great success, as with a person honored by the ruler whose principles now prevail.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram's rulers are the six in the fifth place and the nine at the top, referenced in the Commentary's "the firm ascends and honors the worthy" — the strong top line rises above the ruler's own place and is honored by that ruler precisely because of its worth.
- The Commentary lays out all three of the hexagram's meanings explicitly: keeping still and collecting oneself (Kên holding Ch'ien firm, producing daily renewal of virtue), holding fast and keeping still (Kên restraining the strong lower trigram), and holding fast and nourishing (the ruler in the fifth place nourishing people of worth, explaining why "not eating at home brings good fortune").
- As the inverse of the preceding hexagram (Innocence), the sequence notes that holding fast to heavenly virtue is the prerequisite for innocence, while innocence in turn is the indispensable condition for holding fast to that same pristine virtue — the two hexagrams are mutually dependent.
- The Note observes that unlike most hexagrams, the yin-yang line relationships here are not ones of correspondence and mutual furtherance but of obstruction: the lower trigram's lines are obstructed by the upper trigram's lines, and only the third and top lines — both yang, in harmony with each other — escape the pattern of restraint entirely.

## Worked Example
Take the six in the fourth place: "The headboard of a young bull. Great good fortune." The commentary explains the image of fastening a headboard to a bull's forehead before its horns even grow, so that once they appear they cannot cause harm. This deepens the Judgment's overall theme of taming and storing great power: the most effective restraint isn't a fight against force already unleashed, but a forestalling action taken early, before danger has the chance to develop — which is precisely why this line's outcome is "great good fortune" rather than merely adequate.

## Key Takeaways
- Build real strength through daily, deliberate self-renewal of character, not through habit or inertia alone.
- When facing superior restraining force, submit and wait rather than force a doomed advance — the delay itself stores energy for later.
- Address a growing threat before it fully manifests; forestalling is easier and more effective than confrontation after the fact.
- When direct opposition to a wild or destructive force is unwise, look for an indirect way to alter its underlying nature instead.
- Recognize when accumulated restraint has finally run its course — pent-up energy, once released at the right time, achieves real and lasting success.
