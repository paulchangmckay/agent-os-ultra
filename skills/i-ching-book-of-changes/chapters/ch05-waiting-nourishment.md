# Hexagram 5: Hsü / Waiting (Nourishment)

## Core Idea
Strength positioned before danger does not plunge ahead but bides its time — like clouds gathering in the sky before rain falls, waiting here is an active, confident readiness, not passive hoping.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'an (the Abysmal, danger)
- Lower: Ch'ien (Heaven, strength)

## The Judgment
> WAITING. If you are sincere, you have light and success. Perseverance brings good fortune. It furthers one to cross the great water.

**Meaning**: Waiting succeeds only when grounded in uncompromising honesty with oneself about the danger ahead; that clarity produces the light by which the right path is seen, followed by resolute, persevering action strong enough to cross the great water.

## The Image
> Clouds rise up to heaven: the image of WAITING. Thus the superior man eats and drinks, is joyous and of good cheer.

**Meaning**: Since rain will fall in its own time regardless of our interference, the superior man fortifies body and spirit with food, drink, and good cheer while fate works itself out, rather than anxiously forcing events before they're ripe.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Waiting in the meadow. It furthers one to abide in what endures. No blame." — With danger still remote, keep to a regular, steady life rather than wasting strength on premature worry.
- **Nine in the second place**: "Waiting on the sand. There is some gossip. The end brings good fortune." — As danger draws nearer, minor discord and gossip arise; staying calm and not answering slander keeps things headed toward a good outcome.
- **Nine in the third place**: "Waiting in the mud brings about the arrival of the enemy." — Pushing too energetically toward danger, without adequate preparation, invites attack; only caution and seriousness prevent defeat.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Waiting in blood. Get out of the pit." — In a moment of true life-or-death gravity, standing fast and not aggravating the danger through action is the only way out.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "Waiting at meat and drink. Perseverance brings good fortune." — Even amid ongoing danger there are intervals of ease; using them to fortify oneself, without losing sight of the goal, sustains the path to victory.
- **Six at the top**: "One falls into the pit. Three uninvited guests arrive. Honor them, and in the end there will be good fortune." — When the danger finally arrives and seems to overwhelm, an unexpected intervention appears; meeting it with respect rather than sullen refusal turns it to good fortune.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine in the fifth place is the ruler of the hexagram: central, correct, and occupying "the place of heaven," which is why sincerity here produces both light and good fortune — waiting is deferral, never abandonment of the goal.
- The T'uan Chuan reads the trigram pairing directly: strength (Ch'ien) below danger (K'an) means one is firm enough not to fall into perplexity or bewilderment while the danger is still ahead.
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams explains Hsü's place after Mêng: things that are still small and undeveloped must not be left without nourishment — hence "the way to eating and drinking" follows youthful folly directly.
- A closing structural note traces the whole hexagram's arc through the individual lines: the beginning is safely distant from danger, the second and third lines approach it with calm or over-eagerness respectively, the fourth is overtaken but yields and escapes, and the top is at the peak of danger yet survives through deference — self-control and deference are named as the operating principle throughout.
- The commentary distinguishes this hexagram from Hexagram 39 (Chien, Obstruction): here, even while waiting amid danger, one remains sure of one's cause and so keeps an inner cheerfulness that mere obstruction does not allow.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "Waiting in the mud brings about the arrival of the enemy." The commentary explains this is a strong line in a strong place — too energetic for its position — so instead of gathering full strength before crossing, it makes a premature start and gets stuck at the muddy bank, exposed. This isn't a failure of courage; it's a failure of timing, and it shows that strength itself can become a liability when applied a moment too soon. The remedy the line offers is not retreat but heightened seriousness and caution, which alone prevent the exposure from becoming a real defeat.

## Key Takeaways
- When danger is still distant, keep living normally and don't burn energy worrying prematurely — that reserve is what you'll need later.
- As tension and minor conflict increase, don't answer gossip or friction with injured retorts; calm silence lets it dissipate on its own.
- Don't mistake eagerness for readiness — pushing toward a real risk before you've gathered sufficient strength invites the very attack you're trying to avoid.
- In a genuinely dangerous, cornered moment, the discipline of standing fast without escalating is itself the way out.
- Use calm intervals during an ongoing struggle to actively recover and prepare, without losing track of the larger goal.
- When help or resolution arrives in an unexpected or strange-looking form, meet it with openness and respect rather than suspicion — refusing it out of pride can cost you the good outcome it offers.
