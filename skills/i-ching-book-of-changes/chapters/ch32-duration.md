# Hexagram 32: Hêng / Duration

## Core Idea
Duration is not standstill but self-renewing movement of an organized, firmly integrated whole — like marriage itself, the enduring union that follows courtship — where an end is always followed by a new beginning, in accordance with immutable laws.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Chên, thunder (movement)
- Lower: Sun, wind (gentleness)

## The Judgment
> DURATION. Success. No blame. Perseverance furthers. It furthers one to have somewhere to go.

**Meaning**: Duration is the self-contained, self-renewing movement seen in heavenly bodies moving in fixed orbits and the seasons following fixed law; the dedicated man likewise embodies an enduring meaning in his way of life, and by understanding what gives things duration, one comes to understand the nature of all beings in heaven and earth.

## The Image
> Thunder and wind: the image of DURATION. Thus the superior man stands firm and does not change his direction.

**Meaning**: Thunder and wind are extremely mobile yet the laws governing their coming and going endure; likewise the superior man's independence isn't rigidity but an unswerving inner directive that determines all his actions even as he keeps abreast of changing times.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "Seeking duration too hastily brings misfortune persistently. Nothing that would further." — What truly endures can only be created gradually through long, careful work; demanding too much too soon ends in succeeding at nothing.
- **Nine in the second place**: "Remorse disappears." — Even when one's force of character exceeds available material power, the time of duration allows one to control that inner strength and avoid excess, so cause for remorse disappears.
- **Nine in the third place**: "He who does not give duration to his character meets with disgrace. Persistent humiliation." — Remaining at the mercy of external hopes and fears destroys inner consistency, and the resulting humiliations are logical consequences of one's own nature, not merely external misfortune.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "No game in the field." — Persistence alone isn't enough; searching in the wrong place, however doggedly, never finds what isn't sought in the right way.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Giving duration to one's character through perseverance. This is good fortune for a woman, misfortune for a man." — A woman rightly holds conservatively to one lifelong course, but a man must stay flexible and adaptable, guided by the duty of the moment rather than persistent conformity.
- **Six at the top**: "Restlessness as an enduring condition brings misfortune." — Perpetual hurry without ever attaining inner composure prevents thoroughness and becomes especially dangerous in positions of authority.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine in the second place is the hexagram's ruler: though the fifth place is also central, it is weak, while the second is both central and strong — and the Commentary explains "remorse disappears" for this line "because it is permanently central."
- Unlike the preceding hexagram (Influence), where correspondence between lines was more hindrance than help, here the Commentary treats the correspondence of every single line (first with fourth, second with fifth, third with top) as proof of a firm inner organization that itself guarantees duration.
- The Commentary distinguishes this hexagram from the last structurally and socially: Influence pictures the youngest son and youngest daughter entering into courtship and marriage, while Duration pictures the eldest son and eldest daughter already united — the enduring condition of marriage itself, not its beginning.
- On the fourth line's "no game in the field," the Commentary contrasts it directly with the third line: the third has "character but no duration" (a strong line in a strong place, but excited and superficial), while the fourth has "duration but no character" (a strong line in a weak place, ceaselessly active but in the wrong place) — two different ways of failing to combine steadiness with substance.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "He who does not give duration to his character meets with disgrace. Persistent humiliation." The commentary explains this line sits exactly at the transition between the two trigrams — not yet fully in the movement of Chên, but already past the gentleness of Sun — so "it does not come to rest anywhere." This deepens the Judgment's central teaching about duration: real endurance requires settling into a consistent, integrated character rather than drifting between moods and postures. A person who never lets their character take real, lasting form has no inner ground to stand on, and the resulting instability produces genuine, repeated humiliation rather than mere bad luck.

## Key Takeaways
- Build what should endure gradually; rushing to force lasting results produces the opposite outcome.
- Manage your own capability against what circumstances actually allow — restraint within one's true strength prevents regret.
- Anchor your character in something consistent rather than reacting to every passing hope or fear from outside.
- Persistence alone doesn't guarantee results — check whether you're even looking in the right place before continuing.
- Recognize the difference between principled steadiness and mere restlessness mistaken for endurance; constant activity without composure is dangerous, especially in authority.
