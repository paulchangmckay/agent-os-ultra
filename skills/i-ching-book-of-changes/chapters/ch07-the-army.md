# Hexagram 7: Shih / The Army

## Core Idea
Military strength stored in the mass of a people, like ground water stored in the earth — invisible in peacetime, always ready — and effective only when a single capable leader disciplines the many without resorting to raw force.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'un (Earth, devotion/obedience)
- Lower: K'an (the Abysmal, danger)

## The Judgment
> THE ARMY. The army needs perseverance and a strong man. Good fortune without blame.

**Meaning**: An undisciplined mass becomes a fighting force only through a strong leader who wins the people's hearts rather than commanding by force; war itself should be entered only as a last recourse, grounded in a just and clearly explained cause.

## The Image
> In the middle of the earth is water: the image of THE ARMY. Thus the superior man increases his masses by generosity toward the people.

**Meaning**: Just as ground water is invisibly held within the earth, a people's military strength is invisibly held within its economic and social well-being — generous, humane government is what makes a strong army possible at all.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "An army must set forth in proper order. If the order is not good, misfortune threatens." — A military venture (or any collective undertaking) that starts without proper discipline and a valid cause is doomed from the outset.
- **Nine in the second place**: "In the midst of the army. Good fortune. No blame. The king bestows a triple decoration." — A leader who stays among the ranks, sharing hardship and good fortune with those he leads, earns recognition that reflects honor on the whole group, not personal preferment.
- **Six in the third place**: "Perchance the army carries corpses in the wagon. Misfortune." — When authority is usurped by those without the standing to hold it, or command is interfered with, defeat follows.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "The army retreats. No blame." — Facing a superior force, an orderly retreat is the correct, blameless choice — not a failure of courage, since insisting on a hopeless fight would be worse.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "There is game in the field. It furthers one to catch it. Without blame. Let the eldest lead the army. The younger transports corpses; then perseverance brings misfortune." — A real provocation justifies decisive response, but only under an experienced leader; putting an inexperienced person in command leads to defeat regardless of how much perseverance is shown.
- **Six at the top**: "The great prince issues commands, founds states, vests families with fiefs. Inferior people should not be employed." — After victory, rewards should go to those with genuine merit; unworthy people should be paid off, never granted lasting power.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine in the second place and the six in the fifth place are the hexagram's dual rulers: the strong general below and the yielding ruler above who has "capacity to employ" him — the general's authority depends on the ruler's trust and correspondence, not on independent power.
- The T'uan Chuan reads the whole hexagram structurally: a single strong line surrounded by yielding lines represents a disciplined mass under one central command — danger is indicated by the lower trigram K'an, devotion/obedience by the upper trigram K'un, giving the army its dual character of "dangerous at the core, disciplined on the outside."
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams places Shih directly after Sung (Conflict): "when there is conflict, the masses are sure to rise up" — the army is the natural consequence of unresolved conflict escalating.
- Line-level commentary reinforces that legitimate authority is everything: the nine in the second place draws its "grace from heaven" through its correspondence with the six in the fifth; the defeat implied at the third line stems specifically from authority being seized by someone other than the rightful leader; and the top line's reward of merit is tied directly back to the second line's service, while the "inferior people" who should not be employed are identified with the disordered third line.

## Worked Example
Take the six in the fifth place: "There is game in the field. It furthers one to catch it... Let the eldest lead the army. The younger transports corpses; then perseverance brings misfortune." The commentary is explicit that "the eldest" refers to the strong nine in the second place — the experienced general — and that if someone without that standing (identified with the disordered six in the third place) takes command instead, defeat results no matter how much determination is shown. The line teaches that legitimate provocation justifies a real response, but the outcome depends entirely on whether command is held by the person qualified to hold it — perseverance under the wrong leadership is not a virtue here, it's the mechanism of the defeat.

## Key Takeaways
- Build any large collective effort on a genuinely just cause and clear discipline from the very start — a shaky foundation guarantees later misfortune.
- Lead from within the group you're responsible for, sharing its conditions directly, rather than directing from a safe distance.
- Watch for authority being seized by people without standing to hold it — that specific failure mode, not lack of resources, is what produces the described defeat.
- Retreating from a fight you cannot win is not weakness; treat orderly withdrawal as the professionally correct choice, not a personal failure.
- When responding to a genuine threat, make sure command sits with the most experienced person available — determination cannot substitute for putting the right leader in charge.
- After a hard-won success, direct rewards strictly by merit; don't hand lasting power to people who merely helped, however useful they were in the moment.
