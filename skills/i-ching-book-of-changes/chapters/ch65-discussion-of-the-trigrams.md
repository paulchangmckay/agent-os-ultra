# The Eight Trigrams (Shuo Kua)

## Core Idea
Every hexagram is built from two of eight three-line trigrams, and each trigram carries a fixed bundle of associations — attribute, natural image, family role, animal, body part — that the Duke of Chou drew on when writing the individual line texts. Learn these associations once, and cryptic line imagery (a horse, a pheasant, a mountain path) stops looking arbitrary and starts reading as compressed reference to a trigram's known symbolism.

## The Eight Trigrams

| Trigram | Attribute | Image | Family Role | Animal | Body Part |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ch'ien** ☰ | strong | heaven | father | horse | head |
| **K'un** ☷ | yielding | earth | mother | cow | belly |
| **Chên** ☳ | movement | thunder | eldest son | dragon | foot |
| **Sun** ☴ | penetrating | wind/wood | eldest daughter | cock | thighs |
| **K'an** ☵ | dangerous | water | middle son | pig | ear |
| **Li** ☲ | dependence/clinging | fire | middle daughter | pheasant | eye |
| **Kên** ☶ | standstill | mountain | youngest son | dog | hand |
| **Tui** ☱ | pleasure/joyous | lake | youngest daughter | sheep | mouth |

Use the "Attributes" column first when a line reads as an abstract quality (strength, danger, standstill). Use "Image" when a Judgment or Image text is describing a physical scene. Use family role when a hexagram's structure is explicitly about generational or household relationships — the source text derives the six "children" trigrams from where a single moving line of the opposite sex enters Ch'ien or K'un: Chên, K'an, Kên gain their determining line in the bottom, middle, and top place respectively when K'un (the mother) is entered by Ch'ien's yang; Sun, Li, Tui gain theirs the same way when Ch'ien (the father) is entered by K'un's yin.

**Additional symbols worth knowing per trigram** (from the source's "Additional Symbols" section — use these when a line text mentions an object that doesn't obviously map to the core image):
- **Ch'ien**: jade, metal, cold, ice, deep red, a good/old/lean/wild horse, tree fruit
- **K'un**: cloth, a kettle, frugality, a cow with a calf, a large wagon, the multitude, black soil
- **Chên**: dark yellow, a great road, bamboo, reed and rush, pod-bearing plants
- **Sun**: a guideline (commands), the white, advance-and-retreat (indecision), odor, gray-haired people
- **K'an**: ditches, ambush, bow and wheel, the moon, thieves, blood, wood that is firm with much pith
- **Li**: the sun, lightning, coats of mail and weapons, the big-bellied, the tortoise/crab/shell creatures
- **Kên**: a bypath, little stones, doors and gates, fruits and seeds, eunuchs and watchmen, gnarled trees
- **Tui**: a sorceress, mouth and tongue, smashing and breaking apart (ripe fruit bursting), hard salty soil

## Key Concepts
- **Trigram (kua)** — a three-line figure of firm (unbroken) and yielding (broken) lines; the basic unit hexagrams are built from, two per hexagram.
- **Sequence of Earlier Heaven (Primal Arrangement)** — the trigram layout attributed to Fu Hsi, organized as opposite pairs (Ch'ien/K'un, Kên/Tui, Chên/Sun, Li/K'an) around a static, cosmological axis; used to read underlying, timeless polarity.
- **Sequence of Later Heaven (Inner-World Arrangement)** — King Wên's layout, which instead sequences the trigrams through the year's seasonal cycle (Chên in spring/east through Kên in late winter/northeast); used to read a hexagram's position in unfolding time.
- **Family of the trigrams** — the father/mother/three sons/three daughters scheme: each "child" trigram is named for the one line (bottom, middle, or top) where the opposite-sex parent's line has entered.
- **Nuclear trigrams (hu kua)** — introduced in ch67, but rooted here: the two trigrams formed by a hexagram's four middle lines, overlapping with the primary trigrams.
- **Attribute vs. image vs. family role** — three independent layers of meaning a single trigram carries simultaneously; a line text may draw on any one of them.

## Key Takeaways
- When a line's imagery seems arbitrary (a specific animal, plant, or object), check which trigram governs that line's position first — the symbol is almost always drawn from that trigram's fixed association list, not invented fresh for the line.
- Use the Primal Arrangement (opposite pairs) when the hexagram's meaning turns on a static polarity (e.g., heaven vs. earth, fire vs. water as complementary opposites).
- Use the Inner-World Arrangement (seasonal sequence) when the hexagram's meaning turns on development through time — growth, culmination, decline, dormancy.
- When a hexagram concerns family, authority, or generational relationships, read its two trigrams by family role (father/mother/son/daughter) before reaching for anything else — this is often the fastest route to the hexagram's core situation.
- Body-part correspondences are most useful for hexagrams that describe illness, injury, or physical constraint line by line (e.g., a line naming "the foot" or "the ear" is invoking Chên or K'an, not just describing an anatomical detail).
