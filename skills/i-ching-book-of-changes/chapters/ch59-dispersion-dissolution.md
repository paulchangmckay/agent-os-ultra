# Hexagram 59: Huan / Dispersion [Dissolution]

## Core Idea
Breaking up rigidity, egotism, and divisive blockage — through gentle, sustained force, so that what was frozen apart can eventually be reunited on a new, freer basis.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Sun (Wind, the Gentle)
- Lower: K'an (Water, the Abysmal)

## The Judgment
> DISPERSION. Success. The king approaches his temple. It furthers one to cross the great water. Perseverance furthers.

**Meaning**: Dissolving the rigidity of egotism and division requires shared, unifying effort — like collective ritual or a common great undertaking — carried out by someone free of selfish motive and persevering in justice.

## The Image
> The wind drives over the water: The image of DISPERSION. Thus the kings of old sacrificed to the Lord And built temples.

**Meaning**: Just as spring winds dissolve winter ice and reunite scattered water, hearts hardened by selfishness must be moved by shared reverence and devotion to dissolve the isolation that egotism creates.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "He brings help with the strength of a horse. Good fortune." — Act quickly to dissolve misunderstanding and distrust at the very start, before division has hardened into something worse.
- **Nine in the second place**: "At the dissolution He hurries to that which supports him. Remorse disappears." — When you notice alienation or ill will beginning within yourself, actively seek out what genuinely supports a fair, good-willed view of others; that restores your outlook and removes cause for regret.
- **Six in the third place**: "He dissolves his self. No remorse." — When a task truly demands it, set aside personal concerns entirely; renouncing self-interest for a larger goal is the source of real strength here.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "He dissolves his bond with his group. Supreme good fortune. Dispersion leads in turn to accumulation. This is something that ordinary men do not think of." — Rising above private loyalties and party interests for the sake of the general welfare, though it costs what is near, wins something far greater — and paradoxically produces new accumulation.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "His loud cries are as dissolving as sweat. Dissolution! A king abides without blame." — In times of general breakdown, a compelling, rallying idea from someone in the right position of leadership becomes the crisis point that leads to recovery.
- **Nine at the top**: "He dissolves his blood. Departing, keeping at a distance, going out, Is without blame." — Beyond saving yourself, actively helping those close to you escape danger, or keep clear of it, is the right and blameless action.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan explains the hexagram's success through the interlocking roles of three lines: the strong nine in the second gives an "inexhaustible" foundation, the nine in the fifth (the king) provides central concentration to hold things together, and the yielding six in the fourth receives and completes the ruler's work.
- Book III notes the hexagram carries a double meaning: wind over water suggests the breaking up of ice (dissolution), while Sun penetrating K'an also suggests the reverse task of eventual reunion — dispersion and re-gathering are two faces of the same process.
- The hexagram's language is explicitly compared to Ts'ui, Gathering Together (45): dispersion is described as "the way that leads to gathering together," which is why the two texts read so similarly despite opposite subjects.
- The Appended Judgments credit this hexagram as the traditional inspiration for the invention of boats and oars — wood (Sun) over water (K'an) — turning the image of separation into a means of connection and communication.

## Worked Example
Take the six in the fourth place: "He dissolves his bond with his group. Supreme good fortune. Dispersion leads in turn to accumulation. This is something that ordinary men do not think of." Book III explains this line, structurally, has detached from what would otherwise be a K'un-group formation, dissolving a narrower bond in favor of receiving the ruler above and gaining the strong second line as an ally — so "accumulation does in fact follow upon dispersion." Applied practically: this is the line for someone who must choose between loyalty to a small in-group and service to a larger, general good — the counterintuitive lesson is that deliberately dissolving the narrower bond, though it looks like a loss, is what actually produces something greater. Most people never think to make this trade.

## Key Takeaways
- Address division and misunderstanding at the earliest possible moment; the longer rigidity sets in, the harder it becomes to dissolve.
- When you notice alienation or ill will forming in yourself, actively seek fair, good-willed judgment rather than letting resentment compound.
- Be willing to set personal interest aside entirely when a larger task genuinely requires it — that renunciation is the source of real capability.
- Recognize when loyalty to a narrower group is blocking a greater good; dissolving that bond, though costly, can produce more than it gives up.
- In a moment of general breakdown, a clear, unifying idea from the right person can serve as the turning point toward recovery — provide or rally around one.

## Connects To
- Ts'ui (45), Gathering Together — explicitly compared in Book I: the two hexagrams' texts closely resemble each other, since Dispersion is described as "the way that leads to gathering together."
- Chieh (60), Limitation — the hexagram immediately following in the Sequence: "after joy comes dispersal... hence there follows the hexagram of DISPERSION," and Book III notes Chieh is structurally the inverse of this hexagram, holding water together where Huan disperses it.
