# Hexagram 61: Chung Fu / Inner Truth

## Core Idea
A truth so genuine and free of self-interest that it reaches even the least receptive minds — the kind of inner sincerity that transforms whole communities, unlike mere alliance or shared interest.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Sun (Wind, the Gentle)
- Lower: Tui (Lake, the Joyous)

## The Judgment
> INNER TRUTH. Pigs and fishes. Good fortune. It furthers one to cross the great water. Perseverance furthers.

**Meaning**: When inner truth is strong enough to influence even the dullest, least tractable creatures, it can accomplish the most difficult undertakings — but this force must be grounded in what is genuinely right, not merely in shared interest or secret alliance, which fails the moment interests diverge.

## The Image
> Wind over lake: the image of INNER TRUTH. Thus the superior man discusses criminal cases In order to delay executions.

**Meaning**: Just as wind penetrates and stirs water, the superior man seeks to penetrate the minds of those he judges with real understanding — mildness born of clarity, not weakness, in order to judge rightly rather than hastily.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Being prepared brings good fortune. If there are secret designs, it is disquieting." — Inner stability and readiness bring good fortune; but cultivating secret, exclusive alliances undermines the independence that inner truth requires.
- **Nine in the second place**: "A crane calling in the shade. Its young answers it. I have a good goblet. I will share it with you." — Genuine feeling, truly expressed, evokes a spontaneous response from kindred spirits even at a distance — the effect follows naturally from sincerity, never from deliberate calculation.
- **Six in the third place**: "He finds a comrade. Now he beats the drum, now he stops. Now he sobs, now he sings." — When your center of gravity depends on another person rather than on yourself, you are tossed unpredictably between joy and sorrow by that dependency.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "The moon nearly at the full. The team horse goes astray. No blame." — Retain enough humility to keep learning from those above you, and enough independence to break from factional loyalty to your own kind — both are needed to keep moving forward freely.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "He possesses truth, which links together. No blame." — A leader whose character is ample enough to genuinely influence others creates real, lasting unity; without that central force, any unity is mere appearance that collapses under pressure.
- **Nine at the top**: "Cockcrow penetrating to heaven. Perseverance brings misfortune." — Loud words alone, without substance behind them, may work briefly but bring misfortune if relied upon persistently — the cock crows but cannot actually fly to heaven.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan reads the hexagram's structure directly as its meaning: yielding lines within create an "empty," humble heart open to what is good, while strong central lines in each trigram provide the trustworthiness that makes the emptiness safe rather than hollow.
- Confucius's extended gloss on the second line stresses that words and deeds are the "hinge and bowspring" of the superior man — spoken truthfully, they move people even a thousand miles away; spoken falsely, they provoke contradiction just as far — hence the emphasis on caution in what one says.
- Book III notes the hexagram's harmonious, symmetrical structure (yielding lines within, firm lines without, with well-matched trigram attributes of joyousness and gentleness) is itself the reason for its uniformly favorable judgment.
- The image of "pigs and fishes" is explained as deliberately chosen for being the least intelligent of creatures — that even they can be moved is offered as proof of how powerful genuine inner truth really is.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fifth place: "He possesses truth, which links together. No blame." Book III attributes this line's linking power to the imagery of rope (from Sun) and hand (from the nuclear trigram Kên) — and notes plainly that its influence comes from "the correct, central, and honored position it occupies." Applied practically: this is the line for someone in real authority who wants their organization or community to hold together under pressure — the lesson is that only a leader whose own character is substantial enough to be genuinely trusted can create unity that survives a crisis; imposed or engineered cohesion (a "secret bond," as the Judgment warns) collapses exactly when it's tested.

## Key Takeaways
- Ground your influence on others in genuine sincerity, not calculated persuasion — real feeling expressed plainly reaches further than any deliberate strategy.
- Watch your words carefully: they travel far and shape how you're received by people you'll never meet in person.
- Notice when your emotional stability depends entirely on another person; that dependency will swing you unpredictably between extremes.
- Stay humble toward those who can still teach you, while keeping independence from group loyalty that would compromise your judgment.
- Do not substitute loud, confident words for the substance that should back them — persistent bluster eventually produces misfortune, however well it works short-term.
