# Hexagram 29: K'an / The Abysmal (Water)

## Core Idea
Doubled danger — a yang line plunged between two yin lines like water closed in by a ravine — names an objective situation to be mastered by sincerity and consistent right conduct, not a subjective foolhardiness or guile; like water, one escapes danger by remaining true to one's own nature.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'an, water (danger)
- Lower: K'an, water (danger)

## The Judgment
> The Abysmal repeated. If you are sincere, you have success in your heart, and whatever you do succeeds.

**Meaning**: Water flows on, filling every place without losing its essential nature; likewise, sincerity in the face of repeated danger lets the heart penetrate the situation's meaning, and thorough, forward action — not tarrying — carries one through.

## The Image
> Water flows on uninterruptedly and reaches its goal: the image of the Abysmal repeated. Thus the superior man walks in lasting virtue and carries on the business of teaching.

**Meaning**: As water fills every depression before flowing on, the superior man makes goodness a settled attribute of character rather than an isolated occurrence, and teaches others through the same patient consistency and repetition.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "Repetition of the Abysmal. In the abyss one falls into a pit. Misfortune." — Growing accustomed to danger can mean growing accustomed to evil itself, losing the right way and bringing misfortune as a natural result.
- **Nine in the second place**: "The abyss is dangerous. One should strive to attain small things only." — In danger, don't try to escape immediately regardless of circumstance; be content with small gains while calmly weighing the conditions of the time.
- **Six in the third place**: "Forward and backward, abyss on abyss. In danger like this, pause at first and wait, otherwise you will fall into a pit in the abyss. Do not act in this way." — When every direction leads into danger and escape seems impossible, resist being misled into action that only deepens the trouble; wait until a real way out appears.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "A jug of wine, a bowl of rice with it; earthen vessels simply handed in through the window. There is certainly no blame in this." — In times of danger, ceremonious forms drop away; sincerity and honest mutual help matter far more than elaborate introduction or gift.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "The abyss is not filled to overflowing, it is filled only to the rim. No blame." — Danger comes from overreaching ambition; proceeding along the line of least resistance, without attempting great labors, is enough to reach the goal.
- **Six at the top**: "Bound with cords and ropes, shut in between thorn-hedged prison walls: for three years one does not find the way. Misfortune." — Someone who has lost the right way in extreme danger and become entangled in serious faults has no prospect of easy escape.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram's rulers are the two strong lines in the second and fifth places, with the fifth being ruler in a more marked degree; it represents water that flows on once it has filled a given place — the Commentary's phrase "water flows on and nowhere piles up" belongs to this line specifically.
- As one of the eight hexagrams formed by doubling a single trigram, K'an is explained in two complementary ways: first, as water showing how to overcome danger without losing its own dependable nature; second, as the human heart, where the innate disposition to good (the firm line forming the middle of each trigram) is at risk of being engulfed by desire and passion.
- The Commentary broadens the hexagram's meaning beyond personal peril: danger also serves a protective function — heaven's height cannot be climbed, earth's mountains and rivers separate and protect countries, and rulers likewise use danger to guard their realms — concluding "the effects of the time of danger are truly great."
- The Note on the six lines observes an important asymmetry: the two strong lines (second and fifth) fare notably better and have real prospects of escaping danger, while the weak lines at the beginning, third, and especially the top (three years without finding the way) are progressively more endangered — the hexagram's overall theme of light lines enclosed and threatened by dark lines plays out unevenly across the individual lines.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fifth place: "The abyss is not filled to overflowing, it is filled only to the rim. No blame." The commentary explains that although this line, strong and centrally placed, might easily feel powerful enough to attempt something great, its central position itself prevents overreach — "the central line is not yet great." This deepens the Judgment's core teaching about danger: success under threat doesn't come from ambition or force, but from doing only what's proportionate to the moment, extricating oneself from danger rather than attempting a grand escape or conquest.

## Key Takeaways
- Face real danger with sincerity and consistent action rather than either recklessness or evasive guile.
- Be wary of growing so accustomed to a dangerous or compromising situation that it reshapes your sense of what's acceptable.
- In deep trouble, resist acting for action's sake — sometimes the right move is to pause and wait until a genuine way out appears.
- When circumstances are dire, let sincerity replace elaborate formality; honest simplicity is enough to remove blame.
- Match your ambitions to what the dangerous moment actually allows — reaching the rim, not overflowing it, is the mark of real mastery.
