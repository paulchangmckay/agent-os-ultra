# Hexagram 2: K'un / The Receptive

## Core Idea
Pure yielding devotion — six broken lines representing the dark, receptive power of yin, whose nature is not to lead but to follow, completing and bringing to birth what the Creative begets.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'un (Earth)
- Lower: K'un (Earth)

## The Judgment
> THE RECEPTIVE brings about sublime success, Furthering through the perseverance of a mare. If the superior man undertakes something and tries to lead, He goes astray; But if he follows, he finds guidance. It is favorable to find friends in the west and south, To forego friends in the east and north. Quiet perseverance brings good fortune.

**Meaning**: Success comes not from taking the lead but from following — like a mare, whose strength is joined to devotion; gather companions when the work calls for effort, but go it alone when the work calls for quiet, unswayed judgment.

## The Image
> The earth's condition is receptive devotion. Thus the superior man who has breadth of character carries the outer world.

**Meaning**: Because the earth bears all things without exception, the superior man cultivates a breadth of character wide enough to support and tolerate people and things rather than rejecting what doesn't suit him.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "When there is hoarfrost underfoot, solid ice is not far off." — Small, early signs of decay predict much larger consequences if left unchecked; act on the first warning.
- **Six in the second place**: "Straight, square, great. Without purpose, yet nothing remains unfurthered." — Acting in unforced accord with what is right, without special agenda, naturally accomplishes everything that needs doing.
- **Six in the third place**: "Hidden lines. One is able to remain persevering. If by chance you are in the service of a king, Seek not works, but bring to completion." — Conceal your ability until the right moment; when serving another, let the credit go to them and focus on completion, not recognition.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "A tied-up sack. No blame, no praise." — In a dangerous, exposed position, strict reticence protects you; visibility here brings only risk.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "A yellow lower garment brings supreme good fortune." — True quality expresses itself indirectly and modestly rather than announcing itself; restraint in a prominent but non-independent position brings the best result.
- **Six at the top**: "Dragons fight in the meadow. Their blood is black and yellow." — When the yielding force refuses to yield and instead contends with the light-giving force for supremacy, both sides are wounded.
- **All lines sixes (special case)**: "Lasting perseverance furthers." — When every line is at its most yin, the hexagram turns into Ch'ien, THE CREATIVE; holding fast to what is right yields enduring strength, with neither advance nor retreat.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The six in the second place is the hexagram's ruler, embodying the fourfold character of the Receptive — yielding, devoted, central, and correct — just as the nine in the fifth place ruled Ch'ien.
- The T'uan Chuan frames the Receptive as the necessary complement to the Creative, not its opposite: the Creative begets, the Receptive brings to birth and gives form; the Receptive only goes astray when it tries to lead rather than follow.
- The Hsiang Chuan reads the doubled K'un trigram as massiveness — the solidity by which earth can carry all things without losing its own nature, and the model for the superior man's breadth of character.
- The single Wên Yen comment on this hexagram (in contrast to the many on Ch'ien) notes that the lines of K'un, unlike those of Ch'ien, stand side by side as separate situations rather than a developmental sequence — reflecting that the Receptive represents space and juxtaposition, where the Creative represents time and sequence.
- The commentary draws a parallel between the top line's "dragons fight in the meadow" and the historical moment when a subordinate refuses subordination: the dark principle is only ever named as something to be opposed, since the text is written for superior men, and dark ambition is always cast as the external threat.

## Worked Example
Take the six at the beginning: "When there is hoarfrost underfoot, solid ice is not far off." The commentary connects this directly to human catastrophe — a servant murdering a master, a son murdering a father — noting such events never arise in a single day; they build from small, ignored signs of decay over a long time. The line's counsel is not to fear the frost itself but to treat it as the trigger for corrective action, before the pattern it signals has time to harden into ice.

## Key Takeaways
- Notice small early warning signs and act on them immediately — waiting until the problem is undeniable means it has already become much harder to stop.
- When working under someone else's authority, do the work fully but let them keep the credit; don't seek personal recognition for what you complete.
- In an exposed, high-risk position, deliberate reticence is protective, not passive — silence here is a strategy, not a failure to act.
- Let real quality show itself indirectly, through consistent effect, rather than through display or self-promotion.
- Choose your companions by the season of the task: gather allies when the work requires collective effort, but keep your own counsel when the work requires undivided, private judgment.
- When you must yield, yield fully — contesting a position you shouldn't hold produces mutual injury, not victory.
