# Hexagram 44: Kou / Coming to Meet

## Core Idea
A single dark, inferior element has quietly re-emerged from below and, however small and harmless it seems, must be recognized and checked promptly — the danger lies precisely in underestimating something that looks too weak to matter.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Ch'ien (Heaven, the Creative)
- Lower: Sun (Wind, Gentleness, Penetration)

## The Judgment
> COMING TO MEET. The maiden is powerful. One should not marry such a maiden.

**Meaning**: The rise of an inferior element is pictured as a bold, easily-surrendering maiden who seizes power precisely because the strong element has carelessly met her halfway — the inferior man gains influence only because the superior man fails to see him as dangerous.

## The Image
> Under heaven, wind: the image of COMING TO MEET. Thus does the prince act when disseminating his commands and proclaiming them to the four quarters of heaven.

**Meaning**: As wind travels everywhere beneath heaven, the ruler's commands and decrees reach and move the people even though the ruler himself remains distant from them.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "It must be checked with a brake of bronze. Perseverance brings good fortune. If one lets it take its course, one experiences misfortune. Even a lean pig has it in him to rage around." — An inferior element that has crept in must be energetically checked at once, because its current insignificance is no guide to what it will become once strengthened.
- **Nine in the second place**: "There is a fish in the tank. No blame. Does not further guests." — The inferior element is best managed through gentle, contained control rather than violence, but it must not be allowed contact with anything beyond that containment.
- **Nine in the third place**: "There is no skin on his thighs, and walking comes hard. If one is mindful of the danger, no great mistake is made." — Tempted toward an evil that circumstance fortunately prevents, one experiences painful indecision, but clear insight into the danger avoids the more serious mistake.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "No fish in the tank. This leads to misfortune." — Becoming alienated from those one should keep favorably disposed, by failing to meet them halfway, causes them to become unavailable exactly when needed — and this failure is one's own fault.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "A melon covered with willow leaves. Hidden lines. Then it drops down to one from heaven." — A strong, well-poised person tolerates and protects those beneath him without imposing outward show, trusting the transforming power of an upright character to bring things to ripeness on their own.
- **Nine at the top**: "He comes to meet with his horns. Humiliation. No blame." — One who holds himself proudly aloof from all that is low, rebuffing it brusquely, may be reproached as distant, but since no active duty binds him to engage, he can bear this dislike with composure.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The Commentary on the Decision draws a direct parallel between this hexagram and Hexagram 24 (Return): when the strong element first appears among yin lines it is called Return, and the superior man "always stays where he belongs"; when the weak element first appears among yang lines it is this hexagram, and "the inferior man always has to depend on a lucky chance" — a deliberate structural and moral contrast between the two.
- The text explicitly invokes the image of gradual, dangerous growth familiar from Hexagram 2 (K'un, The Receptive): "When there is hoarfrost underfoot, solid ice is not far off" — the way of inferior people expands only because superior men fail to check it early, and the danger can be averted only if addressed the moment it first appears.
- Despite the generally cautionary tone, the Commentary on the Decision also affirms a positive reading of "meeting": "When heaven and earth meet, all creatures settle into firm lines... Great indeed is the meaning of the time of COMING TO MEET" — a needed meeting between complementary forces (like a wise prince and a capable official) is treated as auspicious, distinct from the cautionary maiden imagery of the Judgment.
- The hexagram is noted as the structural inverse of Hexagram 43 (Break-through) — the fourth line here is explicitly contrasted with the corresponding third line of Kuai: both describe isolation, but in Kuai the inner attitude is correct, while here it is not.

## Worked Example
Consider the six at the beginning: "It must be checked with a brake of bronze... Even a lean pig has it in him to rage around." Someone noticing a small, seemingly harmless bad habit reappearing — in themselves, in a team's culture, in a relationship — after having thought it eliminated faces the temptation to dismiss it as too minor to address. This line insists on the opposite response: check it immediately, precisely because its current weakness says nothing about its eventual strength once left unchecked. It sharpens the Judgment's warning that the inferior gains power only because the superior underestimates it — this line shows the concrete, early moment where that underestimation either happens or is avoided.

## Key Takeaways
- Address a small, resurfacing problem the moment you notice it — its present harmlessness is not evidence it will stay harmless.
- When gentle, contained management is enough to control an inferior influence, use it rather than force, but keep that influence strictly isolated from anything it could spread to.
- If you feel tempted toward something you know is compromising, let clear-eyed recognition of the danger substitute for the willpower you may lack in the moment.
- Do not let disinterest or distance drift into alienation from people who are counting on you to meet them halfway — that neglect becomes your own fault when their support is unavailable later.
- Lead by the quiet, patient influence of your own character rather than by constant outward correction or show — real change ripens on its own when trust is given room to work.
