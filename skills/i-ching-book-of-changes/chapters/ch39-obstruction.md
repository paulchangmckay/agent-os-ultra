# Hexagram 39: Chien / Obstruction

## Core Idea
A dangerous abyss lies ahead and an impassable mountain behind — obstruction is real, but temporary, and the way through it is to pause, turn inward, gather trustworthy allies, and follow a capable leader rather than push blindly forward.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'an (Water, the Abysmal, Danger)
- Lower: Kên (Mountain, Keeping Still)

## The Judgment
> OBSTRUCTION. The southwest furthers. The northeast does not further. It furthers one to see the great man. Perseverance brings good fortune.

**Meaning**: Facing obstacles that cannot be overcome directly, it is wise to pause, retreat toward help and fellowship (the southwest) rather than push toward the impassable way (the northeast), join with a capable leader, and persevere with unswerving inner purpose — an obstruction that lasts only for a time becomes an occasion for self-development.

## The Image
> Water on the mountain: the image of OBSTRUCTION. Thus the superior man turns his attention to himself and molds his character.

**Meaning**: Difficulties throw a person back on themselves; while the inferior man blames others, the superior man seeks the fault within, turning the external obstacle into inner enrichment.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "Going leads to obstructions, coming meets with praise." — Facing danger, blindly pressing ahead only complicates things; retreating for the time being, to await the right moment, is correct.
- **Six in the second place**: "The king's servant is beset by obstruction upon obstruction, but it is not his own fault." — When duty to a higher cause requires meeting danger directly, doing so without compunction is right, and no blame attaches even amid piled-up difficulty.
- **Nine in the third place**: "Going leads to obstructions; hence he comes back." — Someone responsible for others (as head of a family or group) who withdraws rather than plunging recklessly ahead is welcomed back with joy by those depending on him.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Going leads to obstructions, coming leads to union." — A situation too large to manage alone calls for holding back and gathering trustworthy companions rather than forging ahead unprepared.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "In the midst of the greatest obstructions, friends come." — One truly called to help in an emergency attracts capable helpers whose organized cooperation overcomes the obstruction.
- **Six at the top**: "Going leads to obstructions, coming leads to great good fortune. It furthers one to see the great man." — Even someone who might prefer to withdraw from the world entirely is called back by duty; precisely because of accumulated experience and inner freedom, they can accomplish something great in alliance with the capable leader.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The Commentary on the Decision defines the hexagram's essential wisdom precisely: "to see the danger and to know how to stand still, that is wisdom" — explicitly contrasted with Hexagram 4 (Youthful Folly), where the positions of danger and standstill are structurally reversed.
- The southwest/northeast directional guidance is tied to trigram geography: southwest is K'un (the Receptive, the level earth, where friends are found), northeast is Kên (the mountain, where one is lonely and the way comes to an end) — the counsel to retreat is thus built directly into the compass symbolism.
- The Image's water-on-mountain picture is read as showing accumulation: water blocked from flowing down a mountain increases until it finally overflows the barrier — the path past obstruction lies in inward accumulation, not forced outward movement.
- The text notes the ruler of the hexagram (nine in the fifth place) sits at the center of the danger trigram K'an itself, yet is surrounded by corresponding lines that come to it "as friends" — obstruction and rescue are shown to originate from the very same structural position.

## Worked Example
Consider the nine in the fifth place: "In the midst of the greatest obstructions, friends come." A person at the center of a genuine crisis — leading a team through a failure, a family through hardship — may feel that being at the epicenter of danger is itself a liability. This line reframes that position: because such a person is truly called to the task, the strength of their purpose draws capable help toward them rather than leaving them isolated. It sharpens the Judgment's counsel to "see the great man" by showing that being the great man in this situation is not about escaping the danger's center, but about occupying it with enough integrity that others organize around you.

## Key Takeaways
- When you meet an obstacle you cannot overcome directly, pause and retreat rather than forcing your way through — this is preparation, not defeat.
- Use setbacks as an occasion to examine yourself rather than assign blame outward; this is where real growth under adversity happens.
- If duty requires you to walk directly into danger despite the difficulty piling up, do so without self-reproach — the obligation, not the danger, defines the right action.
- When a task is too large for you alone, gather trustworthy companions rather than pushing ahead unsupported.
- Even someone who would rather withdraw from the world may be called back by duty precisely when their accumulated experience can achieve something great — don't mistake the wish to retreat permanently for the actual right course.
