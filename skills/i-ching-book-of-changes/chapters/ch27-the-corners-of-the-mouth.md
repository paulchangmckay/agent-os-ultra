# Hexagram 27: I / The Corners of the Mouth

## Core Idea
Pictured as an open mouth, this hexagram addresses nourishment in its full range: caring for one's own body (the lower lines), and providing spiritual and material care for others (the upper lines) — with everything depending on providing nourishment for what is right.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Kên, the mountain (still)
- Lower: Chên, thunder (movement)

## The Judgment
> THE CORNERS OF THE MOUTH. Perseverance brings good fortune. Pay heed to the providing of nourishment and to what a man seeks to fill his own mouth with.

**Meaning**: To know anyone's character, observe whom they care for and what parts of their own nature they cultivate — the superior person nourishes what is superior in himself and in others, in order to benefit everyone through them.

## The Image
> At the foot of the mountain, thunder: the image of PROVIDING NOURISHMENT. Thus the superior man is careful of his words and temperate in eating and drinking.

**Meaning**: Speech moves outward from within, eating moves inward from without; both must be moderated by tranquility so that words and food alike stay within proper measure, cultivating character in the process.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "You let your magic tortoise go, and look at me with the corners of your mouth drooping. Misfortune." — Someone fit to live self-reliantly instead renounces that independence and envies others' circumstances, drawing only derision and contempt.
- **Six in the second place**: "Turning to the summit for nourishment, deviating from the path to seek nourishment from the hill. Continuing to do this brings misfortune." — Unable to provide for oneself and shirking the proper way of earning a living, accepting favor from those above as an ongoing habit leads to misfortune.
- **Six in the third place**: "Turning away from nourishment. Perseverance brings misfortune. Do not act thus for ten years. Nothing serves to further." — Pursuing gratification that never truly nourishes, reeling from desire to satisfaction and back, never reaches its goal and should never be followed.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Turning to the summit for provision of nourishment brings good fortune. Spying about with sharp eyes like a tiger with insatiable craving. No blame." — Someone in high position seeking capable helpers with a hungry tiger's intensity is blameless, because the effort serves the good of all, not personal gain.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Turning away from the path. To remain persevering brings good fortune. One should not cross the great water." — Conscious of one's own deficiency in providing for the people, seeking counsel from a spiritually superior but outwardly modest person brings good fortune, provided one stays humbly aware of this dependence and avoids overreaching.
- **Nine at the top**: "The source of nourishment. Awareness of danger brings good fortune. It furthers one to cross the great water." — A sage of the highest order, from whom all nourishing influence flows, carries heavy responsibility; remaining conscious of it allows even great, difficult undertakings to succeed for everyone's benefit.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram's rulers are the six in the fifth place and the nine at the top, referenced in the Commentary's "he provides nourishment for men of worth and thus reaches the whole people."
- The Commentary distinguishes this hexagram's social theme (the human role in providing nourishment) from hexagram 5 (Waiting), which treats nourishment as a natural process of dependence — a parallel contrast is drawn between hexagrams 48 (The Well, water for nourishment) and 50 (The Caldron, food for nourishment).
- The trigram structure itself is read as jaws and teeth: the upper trigram Kên is immobile like the upper jaw, while the lower trigram Chên moves like the lower jaw, giving the hexagram its very name.
- Line by line, the Commentary distinguishes "good" versus "bad" turning toward the summit for nourishment based on trigram membership: the six in the second place turning to the top brings misfortune because it deviates from its proper peer relationship, while the six in the fourth place turning the same direction brings good fortune because it belongs to the same trigram as the source it seeks.

## Worked Example
Take the nine at the top: "The source of nourishment. Awareness of danger brings good fortune. It furthers one to cross the great water." The commentary explains that the danger here comes directly from the weight of the position — receiving authority and honor from the yielding ruler below while being the ultimate source from which all nourishment flows to everyone else. This deepens the Judgment's overall concern with rightly directed nourishment: the highest responsibility (providing for all) is inseparable from the highest exposure to danger, and it is precisely conscious awareness of that danger, not confidence in one's elevated position, that allows great undertakings to succeed.

## Key Takeaways
- Judge character — your own and others' — by what is cultivated and cared for, not by surface appearances.
- Provide for yourself through your own proper means rather than habitually accepting favor you haven't earned.
- Recognize when the pursuit of gratification has become a cycle that never actually satisfies, and stop it decisively.
- When seeking capable help to serve a larger purpose, pursue it energetically — self-interest and service are judged differently.
- Carry responsibility for others' welfare with active awareness of its danger; that awareness, not confidence, is what makes great undertakings succeed.
