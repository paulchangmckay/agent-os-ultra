# Hexagram 4: Mêng / Youthful Folly

## Core Idea
The inexperience of youth — a spring gushing forth without yet knowing its course, or a young traveler hesitating at the brink of a dangerous abyss — is not itself a flaw, but the raw condition that right instruction and right attitude can turn into success.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Kên (Mountain, keeping still)
- Lower: K'an (the Abysmal, danger)

## The Judgment
> YOUTHFUL FOLLY has success. It is not I who seek the young fool; the young fool seeks me. At the first oracle I inform him. If he asks two or three times, it is importunity. If he importunes, I give him no information. Perseverance furthers.

**Meaning**: Folly in youth is no evil, provided the youth is conscious of his inexperience, seeks out a teacher, and receives a clear answer with respectful acceptance rather than repeated, doubting questions — genuine perseverance in absorbing what's taught brings real success.

## The Image
> A spring wells up at the foot of the mountain: the image of YOUTH. Thus the superior man fosters his character by thoroughness in all that he does.

**Meaning**: Just as a spring escapes stagnation by steadily filling every hollow in its path, the superior man builds character by skipping nothing, gradually and thoroughly working through every gap.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "To make a fool develop it furthers one to apply discipline. The fetters should be removed. To go on in this way brings humiliation." — Early discipline builds good habits, but discipline that hardens into mere drilling becomes humiliating and crippling.
- **Nine in the second place**: "To bear with fools in kindliness brings good fortune. To know how to take women brings good fortune. The son is capable of taking charge of the household." — Inner strength combined with kindly tolerance for others' shortcomings qualifies one to take on real responsibility.
- **Six in the third place**: "Take not a maiden who, when she sees a man of bronze, loses possession of herself. Nothing furthers." — Throwing oneself at a stronger personality out of weakness, rather than waiting to be properly sought, serves no one well.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Entangled folly brings humiliation." — Clinging to unreal fantasies while cut off from any solid, firm guidance leads inevitably to humiliation.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Childlike folly brings good fortune." — Seeking instruction with unassuming, childlike openness, free of arrogance, guarantees real help.
- **Nine at the top**: "In punishing folly it does not further one to commit transgressions. The only thing that furthers is to prevent transgressions." — Correction of incorrigible folly must aim only at restoring order and preventing further wrongdoing, never at venting anger or excess.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine in the second place and the six in the fifth place are the two rulers: the strong, central second line is the wise teacher in a lowly position; the weak fifth line is the youthful ruler who honors and is taught through him — the correspondence between them models the correct teacher-student relationship.
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams places Mêng directly after Chun: once things are newly born from difficulty, they are wrapped at birth in obtuseness — youthful folly is simply the next natural stage after a difficult beginning.
- The T'uan Chuan reads the trigram structure itself as the source of the folly-image: danger (K'an) at the foot of a standstill (Kên) is exactly what folly looks like — hesitating before a hazard one doesn't yet understand.
- The commentary notes a division of labor among the lines: the second line represents gentleness in correcting folly, while the strong top line represents severity — together the hexagram provides both a nurturing and a corrective mode of instruction, matched to different kinds of youthful fools represented by the remaining four lines.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the second place: "To bear with fools in kindliness brings good fortune... The son is capable of taking charge of the household." The commentary explains this through the line's relationship with the six in the fifth place: a firm, capable teacher-figure in a low position paired with a yielding, honoring ruler-figure above creates the same dynamic as a compliant household head letting a capable son take charge. The lesson generalizes beyond family life to any relationship between a wise subordinate and the authority who defers to their competence — inner strength plus outer tolerance, not external power, is what earns the responsibility.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat inexperience as a stage to work through, not a flaw to hide — success in youth depends on seeking instruction, not avoiding admission of ignorance.
- Accept an answer once given rather than asking again out of doubt; repeated, mistrustful questioning wears out the goodwill of whoever is teaching you.
- Build character the way a spring fills a streambed: thoroughly, skipping no gap, rather than rushing past what hasn't yet been mastered.
- Don't throw your judgment away by imitating or currying favor with someone simply because they seem stronger or more established — wait to be genuinely sought.
- When correcting someone's persistent error, aim only at preventing further harm, never at venting frustration — correction that oversteps this becomes its own transgression.
- Approach real learning with the openness of a beginner, free of arrogance; that stance alone is what makes good instruction effective.
