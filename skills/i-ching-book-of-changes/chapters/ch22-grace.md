# Hexagram 22: Pi / Grace

## Core Idea
Grace is fire breaking from the earth's depths to illuminate and beautify the mountain — the ornament that makes a union pleasing rather than chaotic, but only ever a secondary form laid over an essential, strong content.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Kên, the mountain
- Lower: Li, fire

## The Judgment
> GRACE has success. In small matters it is favorable to undertake something.

**Meaning**: Grace is form, not substance, so it must be used sparingly and only for lesser matters; the strong, essential content always remains decisive, with beauty as its pleasing but subordinate variation.

## The Image
> Fire at the foot of the mountain: the image of GRACE. Thus does the superior man proceed when clearing up current affairs. But he dare not decide controversial issues in this way.

**Meaning**: Just as firelight illuminates a mountain only nearby and not far, beautiful form suffices for minor matters but important, controversial questions require greater earnestness than mere grace can provide.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "He lends grace to his toes, leaves the carriage, and walks." — A self-contained person scorns a dubious shortcut to advancement, finding it more graceful to proceed honestly on foot than to ride under false pretenses.
- **Six in the second place**: "Lends grace to the beard on his chin." — Ornament that has no independent existence, moving only as an attribute of its substance, warns against caring for outward form for its own sake, apart from real content.
- **Nine in the third place**: "Graceful and moist. Constant perseverance brings good fortune." — A charming, mellow situation under the spell of grace can also swamp a person; good fortune depends on remaining constant rather than sinking into convivial indolence.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Grace or simplicity? A white horse comes as if on wings. He is not a robber, he will woo at the right time." — Doubt between outward brilliance and plain simplicity resolves itself when confirmation comes unexpectedly; peace of mind is found in a true relationship rather than in renounced comforts.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Grace in hills and gardens. The roll of silk is meager and small. Humiliation, but in the end good fortune." — Withdrawing from luxury-seekers into solitude to find a true friend, one may feel ashamed of meager gifts, but sincerity of feeling, not material offering, carries the day.
- **Nine at the top**: "Simple grace. No blame." — At the highest development, all ornament is discarded; perfect grace is the simple fitness of form to its content, not exterior decoration.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram's rulers are the six in the second place and the nine at the top; the Commentary describes a reciprocal process — "the yielding comes and gives form to the firm, the firm ascends and gives form to the yielding" — where yin (form) and yang (content) alternately shape each other throughout the hexagram's lines.
- The Commentary links the hexagram's structure to cosmic form: the lower trigram Li is the sun, the nuclear trigram K'an is the moon, the nuclear trigram Chên (movement) represents the Great Bear, and the upper trigram Kên (stillness) represents the fixed constellations — contemplating the sun, moon, and stars reveals the changes of time, just as contemplating human forms of conduct lets one shape the world.
- As the inverse of hexagram 21 (Biting Through, which paired brightness with movement for swift, practical justice), this hexagram pairs stillness (Kên) outside with clarity (Li) inside — a theoretical, contemplative cast of mind suited to everyday matters, not extraordinary or controversial decisions.
- The Note observes that every line in the hexagram relates to another through either correspondence or holding-together, and in every case it is the reciprocal relationship of a firm line and a yielding line that produces grace — with a consistent undercurrent counteracting any overemphasis of form over content.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "Graceful and moist. Constant perseverance brings good fortune." The commentary notes this line sits at the highest point of Li (clarity) yet also in the middle of the nuclear trigram K'an (the abyss) — meaning the very height of charm and grace carries a real danger of being submerged by it. This sharpens the Judgment's warning that grace is ornament, not substance: enjoying a graceful, pleasant situation is fine, but only constancy protects against letting that pleasantness become indolence that swallows the person enjoying it.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat outward polish and charm as secondary to substance — never let form replace or outweigh real content.
- Reserve grace and aesthetic concern for small matters; decide controversial or weighty questions on firmer grounds.
- Refuse an easy, ornamented shortcut when it compromises honest effort — walking is more graceful than riding under false pretenses.
- Watch for the point where enjoying a pleasant, graceful situation tips into indolence; perseverance is the safeguard.
- Recognize that the highest form of grace is simplicity — a fit between form and content, with ornament discarded once it's no longer needed.
