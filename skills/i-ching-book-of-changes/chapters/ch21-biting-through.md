# Hexagram 21: Shih Ho / Biting Through

## Core Idea
Biting Through pictures an open mouth with an obstacle between the teeth, preventing the lips from meeting; only energetic biting through — the forceful, clear application of law and penalty — removes what obstructs union, whether in the mouth, in nature, or in social life.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Li, clarity/lightning
- Lower: Chên, movement/thunder

## The Judgment
> BITING THROUGH has success. It is favorable to let justice be administered.

**Meaning**: When union is blocked by a talebearer or troublemaker, energetic and immediate action succeeds; the just measure comes from combining gentleness (Li) with hardness (Chên) rather than either extreme alone.

## The Image
> Thunder and lightning: the image of BITING THROUGH. Thus the kings of former times made firm the laws through clearly defined penalties.

**Meaning**: Clarity in distinguishing mild from severe penalties (lightning) combined with the force to enforce them (thunder) strengthens law by instilling respect — clarity and swiftness, not punishment for its own sake, are what actually work.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "His feet are fastened in the stocks, so that his toes disappear. No blame." — A mild penalty at the first sign of wrongdoing halts the offender early and is itself blameless.
- **Six in the second place**: "Bites through tender meat, so that his nose disappears. No blame." — Discriminating right from wrong is easy here, but indignation at a hardened offender can push the response slightly too far; the penalty is still just.
- **Six in the third place**: "Bites on old dried meat and strikes on something poisonous. Slight humiliation. No blame." — Someone lacking full authority takes up an old, difficult case and provokes resentment against himself, but since punishment was called for, he remains free of blame.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "Bites on dried gristly meat. Receives metal arrows. It furthers one to be mindful of difficulties and to be persevering. Good fortune." — Overcoming a powerful, entrenched obstacle requires being hard as metal and straight as an arrow; persevering awareness of the difficulty brings eventual success.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Bites on dried lean meat. Receives yellow gold. Perseveringly aware of danger. No blame." — A clear but not-easy case calls for impartiality as true as gold; staying conscious of the dangers of one's responsibility prevents mistakes.
- **Nine at the top**: "His neck is fastened in the wooden cangue, so that his ears disappear. Misfortune." — An incorrigible offender who is deaf to warnings meets real misfortune through his own obstinacy.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The six in the fifth place is the hexagram's ruler; the Commentary on the Decision notes "the yielding receives the place of honor and goes upward" — its inherent gentleness, compensated by the firmness of its position, prevents legal proceedings from tipping into cruelty without collapsing into weakness.
- The hexagram's name is explained by its very structure: the top and bottom lines form the jaws, and the single nine in the fourth place is the obstacle lodged between them that must be bitten through — force is structurally necessary here, not incidental.
- Despite a favorable outer form (movement and clarity uniting), the Commentary flags the inner structure as less favorable than it looks: the nuclear trigrams K'an (danger) and Kên (keeping still) sit between the outer trigrams, both arising from the single fateful fourth line — meaning real danger and difficulty run underneath the apparent success.
- The Commentary specifically preserves the phrase order "thunder and lightning" (not "lightning and thunder") because the movement of enforcement properly starts from below, with the law's decisiveness (thunder) and its clarity (lightning) both essential and correctly sequenced.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fourth place: "Bites on dried gristly meat. Receives metal arrows. It furthers one to be mindful of difficulties and to be persevering. Good fortune." The commentary notes this line "does not yet give light" despite standing at the beginning of Li (clarity), because it sits within the nuclear trigram K'an (danger) — real difficulty underlies apparent progress. This deepens the Judgment's warning that biting through a powerful obstacle takes real effort: even when the outcome is ultimately good fortune, awareness of ongoing difficulty and persistence are what get you there, not confidence in the case's apparent clarity alone.

## Key Takeaways
- Act on obstruction early and decisively — a mild response at the first sign of trouble prevents escalation and stays blameless.
- Combine firmness with clarity when confronting an obstacle; either extreme alone (pure force or pure gentleness) fails.
- Expect resentment when correcting an old, entrenched problem — accept the friction as the cost of necessary action.
- Stay aware of the real difficulty behind a seemingly clear-cut case; persistence, not confidence, sees it through.
- Recognize when someone is truly incorrigible — deafness to repeated warning brings its own consequence regardless of what you do.
