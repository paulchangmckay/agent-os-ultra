# Hexagram 10: Lü / Treading [Conduct]

## Core Idea
The weak, cheerful element follows directly behind the strong one — like treading on the tail of a tiger — and succeeds only because the approach is good-humored and without presumption, so the strong is not provoked.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Ch'ien (Heaven, strength)
- Lower: Tui (the Joyous, the lake)

## The Judgment
> TREADING. Treading upon the tail of the tiger. It does not bite the man. Success.

**Meaning**: Even in a genuinely dangerous situation — handling wild or intractable people — good manners and pleasant, decorous conduct can achieve one's purpose without provoking harm.

## The Image
> Heaven above, the lake below: the image of TREADING. Thus the superior man discriminates between high and low, and thereby fortifies the thinking of the people.

**Meaning**: Since even heaven and the lake naturally differ in elevation without envy between them, the superior man ensures social rank corresponds to genuine inner worth, so people accept the differences rather than resenting them.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Simple conduct. Progress without blame." — Free of social obligations at the start, one can progress without blame by staying content with simplicity rather than grasping to escape lowliness.
- **Nine in the second place**: "Treading a smooth, level course. The perseverance of a dark man brings good fortune." — A withdrawn, content person who seeks nothing and is not dazzled by enticing goals travels safely, unassailed, along an even path.
- **Six in the third place**: "A one-eyed man is able to see, a lame man is able to tread. He treads on the tail of the tiger. The tiger bites the man. Misfortune. Thus does a warrior act on behalf of his great prince." — Overestimating limited abilities and plunging into danger beyond one's strength invites disaster, excusable only when it is a warrior's reckless service to his prince.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "He treads on the tail of the tiger. Caution and circumspection lead ultimately to good fortune." — Facing real danger with inner strength matched by outward caution leads to eventual success.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "Resolute conduct. Perseverance with awareness of danger." — Resolute action combined with continuous awareness of the danger involved is what makes success possible.
- **Nine at the top**: "Look to your conduct and weigh the favorable signs. When everything is fulfilled, supreme good fortune comes." — At the completion of a course of action, looking back at one's conduct and its consequences is the only real way to judge what is to come.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The six in the third place is the constituting ruler (the sole yielding line among the firm ones, entering their midst "with fear and trembling"), while the nine in the fifth place is the governing ruler — strong, central, correct, treading into the ruler's place "without blame."
- The T'uan Chuan explains the tiger image structurally: the yielding lower trigram Tui follows directly behind the firm upper trigram Ch'ien, like the tail of a tiger being trodden on — joyousness (Tui's attribute) moving in the same direction as strength (Ch'ien's attribute) is precisely why the tiger does not bite.
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams places Lü directly after Hsiao Ch'u (Taming Power of the Small): once beings are subjected to restraint, the mores — codes of proper conduct — necessarily arise.
- A closing note flags an instructive paradox: although the hexagram as a whole promises the tiger will not bite, the specific line most associated with the tiger image (the six in the third place) is the one line that actually gets bitten — because the whole-hexagram judgment evaluates the lower trigram as a joyous unit, while the individual line judgment evaluates that line's own precarious position (weak in a strong place, at the very mouth of Tui).

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fourth place: "He treads on the tail of the tiger. Caution and circumspection lead ultimately to good fortune." The commentary explicitly contrasts this line with the six in the third place: there, inner weakness paired with outward aggressiveness leads straight into danger and injury; here, inner strength paired with outward caution leads to good fortune despite facing the same essential risk. The line demonstrates that treading on the tiger's tail is not inherently safe or unsafe — the outcome hinges entirely on whether genuine capability is matched with a correspondingly careful approach.

## Key Takeaways
- In a genuinely delicate or dangerous situation, good manners and a non-provoking approach can succeed where confrontation would fail.
- Stay content with simple, modest circumstances rather than pushing to escape them for its own sake — restless ambition to merely "get out" of a low position invites trouble once achieved.
- Don't overestimate limited ability and plunge into danger beyond your real strength; recognize your own "one eye" or "lame leg" honestly before acting.
- When you do have to take on real risk, pair your inner resolve with real outward caution — that combination, not raw courage alone, produces the good outcome.
- Treat resoluteness and awareness of danger as a pair, not alternatives — resolve without awareness of risk is what gets people hurt.
- At the end of any undertaking, judge your prospects by honestly reviewing your actual conduct and its consequences, not by self-assessment alone.
