# Hexagram 54: Kuei Mei / The Marrying Maiden

## Core Idea
A relationship or undertaking entered into out of its proper order or standing — one that can only succeed through tactful reserve, since it lacks the fixed structure of a normal, sanctioned bond.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Chên (Thunder, the Arousing)
- Lower: Tui (Lake, the Joyous)

## The Judgment
> THE MARRYING MAIDEN. Undertakings bring misfortune. Nothing that would further.

**Meaning**: A relationship based on personal inclination rather than properly regulated standing — like a secondary wife entering a household — succeeds only through tactful restraint; asserting oneself into a position not rightfully held leads to disorder.

## The Image
> Thunder over the lake: The image of THE MARRYING MAIDEN. Thus the superior man Understands the transitory In the light of the eternity of the end.

**Meaning**: Relationships drifting along without a fixed, enduring aim easily come apart; keeping the eventual, lasting outcome in mind is what protects against the reefs of misunderstanding along the way.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "The marrying maiden as a concubine. A lame man who is able to tread. Undertakings bring good fortune." — Accepting a modest, subordinate position gracefully still allows real accomplishment, even if outward status is limited.
- **Nine in the second place**: "A one-eyed man who is able to see. The perseverance of a solitary man furthers." — Even disappointed or left in loneliness by a partner's failure, holding to one's inner loyalty and duty is what sustains you.
- **Six in the third place**: "The marrying maiden as a slave. She marries as a concubine." — Throwing oneself at a desired outcome from an ill-fitting position is laid bare here without judgment, as a lesson for the reader to draw for themselves.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "The marrying maiden draws out the allotted time. A late marriage comes in due course." — Withholding action and waiting past the expected time, out of genuine integrity, is rewarded with the right outcome even if it comes late.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "The sovereign I gave his daughter in marriage. The embroidered garments of the princess Were not as gorgeous As those of the servingmaid. The moon that is nearly full Brings good fortune." — Real nobility shows in humility, not display; someone of high standing who steps gracefully into a modest role, without vanity, brings good fortune.
- **Six at the top**: "The woman holds the basket, but there are no fruits in it. The man stabs the sheep, but no blood flows. Nothing that acts to further." — Going through the motions of a commitment without genuine substance or reverence behind it dooms the relationship.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan elevates the hexagram beyond marriage customs to a cosmic statement: "if heaven and earth do not unite, all creatures fail to prosper" — this hexagram represents "the end and beginning of humanity" itself.
- The Judgment's warning ("undertakings bring misfortune") is explained structurally: none of the four middle lines occupy their proper place, and the hexagram's constituting rulers (six in the third, six at the top) are yielding lines resting on hard ones — a structurally poor fit.
- However, the six in the fifth place is identified as the hexagram's true governing ruler: positioned with honor and associating graciously with those below, it is what "changes what is not good into good and transforms misfortune into good fortune."
- Book III notes the hexagram carries an alternate reading (from commentator Liu Yüan) as an elder brother leading his younger sister to marriage, tracing to matriarchal-era stories — a reminder that even canonical readings admit interpretive variation.

## Worked Example
Take the six in the fifth place: "The sovereign I gave his daughter in marriage... Her embroidered garments were not as gorgeous as those of the servingmaid... Good fortune." Book III identifies this as the line that redeems the whole hexagram — central, honored, yet yielding, it "condescends" to the strong line below it the way a noble bride defers to a husband of lesser standing. The lack of outward display is the point: substance over appearance. Applied practically: in a relationship or arrangement that lacks conventional standing or structure, the way to make it work is not through asserting rank or display but through genuine humility — status quietly set aside is what earns durable good fortune here.

## Key Takeaways
- In a relationship without a fixed, sanctioned structure, tactful reserve — not self-assertion — is what keeps it workable.
- Accepting a modest position gracefully does not prevent real accomplishment; status and effectiveness are not the same thing.
- If a partner or connection disappoints you, personal loyalty to your own principles is what carries you through, not the other person's behavior.
- Waiting past the expected moment, when done out of genuine integrity rather than fear, is eventually rewarded.
- Never let ritual or commitment become empty performance — going through the motions without real substance behind them dooms the relationship, as the final line warns.

## Connects To
