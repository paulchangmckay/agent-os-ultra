# Hexagram 3: Chun / Difficulty at the Beginning

## Core Idea
The struggle of a first birth — like a blade of grass pushing against resistance as it sprouts from the earth — where profusion and chaos are actually the raw material of a coming order, not a sign that the venture is wrong.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'an (the Abysmal, danger; its image is rain)
- Lower: Chên (the Arousing; its image is thunder)

## The Judgment
> Difficulty at the Beginning works supreme success, Furthering through perseverance. Nothing should be undertaken. It furthers one to appoint helpers.

**Meaning**: Times of new growth are inherently difficult, but perseverance amid the danger promises real success — the right response is to hold back from premature action and bring in capable helpers rather than trying to push through alone.

## The Image
> Clouds and thunder: The image of Difficulty at the Beginning. Thus the superior man brings order out of confusion.

**Meaning**: Just as a storm's clouds and thunder already contain an implicit order, the superior man sorts the tangled profusion of a beginning the way one separates and re-binds knotted threads into skeins.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Hesitation and hindrance. It furthers one to remain persevering. It furthers one to appoint helpers." — Facing an early obstacle, don't force advance; persevere with the goal in sight and seek assistants through humility, not arrogance.
- **Six in the second place**: "Difficulties pile up. Horse and wagon part. He is not a robber; He wants to woo when the time comes. The maiden is chaste, She does not pledge herself. Ten years — then she pledges herself." — Unexpected help arrives from an unrelated source; decline it and wait out the full cycle of time until the proper connection can be made.
- **Six in the third place**: "Whoever hunts deer without the forester only loses his way in the forest. The superior man understands the signs of the time and prefers to desist. To go on brings humiliation." — Pursuing a goal without proper guidance ends in failure; recognizing this and desisting is wisdom, not weakness.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Horse and wagon part. Strive for union. To go brings good fortune. Everything acts to further." — When a genuine opportunity for connection appears, seize it without false pride or false reserve.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "Difficulties in blessing. A little perseverance brings good fortune. Great perseverance brings misfortune." — Good intentions can go unrecognized and distorted by circumstance; proceed cautiously step by step rather than forcing a decisive stroke.
- **Six at the top**: "Horse and wagon part. Bloody tears flow." — Some are overwhelmed by the difficulties of a beginning and collapse into resignation — the saddest possible outcome, and one not to be persisted in.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine at the beginning and the nine in the fifth place — the only two yang lines — are the hexagram's dual rulers: the lower is the capable helper who quiets the people, the upper is the one positioned to appoint him.
- The T'uan Chuan reads the trigram pairing directly: Chên (movement) beneath K'an (danger) means "movement in the midst of danger," which is precisely how supreme success through perseverance becomes possible — one moves *out* of the danger rather than avoiding it.
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams explains Chun's placement: once heaven and earth exist, individual beings arise to fill the space between them, and this filling-up is itself the chaotic difficulty the hexagram names.
- Line-level commentary shows why even a well-placed ruler struggles here: the nine in the fifth place is central and correct, but hemmed in by the gorge-image of K'an on both sides and only weakly supported by the six in the second place, so its good influence is real but constrained — hence the warning against forcing "great perseverance."
- At the top line, the commentary notes that because a six naturally changes into its opposite, the trigram of danger and gorge would give way to Sun (wind), which overcomes standstill — a structural hint that the moment calls for change, not continued endurance.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fifth place: "Difficulties in blessing. A little perseverance brings good fortune. Great perseverance brings misfortune." This is a ruler of the hexagram — centrally placed, well-intentioned — yet the commentary explains that its beneficial influence is boxed in on both sides by the danger-trigram K'an, and its natural ally below (the six in the second place) is too weak to fully support it. The line doesn't fail for lack of virtue; it fails if it tries to force a large-scale resolution before general confidence has caught up. The counsel is to keep working faithfully in small, unobtrusive steps rather than attempting one decisive stroke.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat the confusion of a new beginning as raw material to be sorted, not proof the venture is doomed — order is implicit in the chaos, waiting to be drawn out.
- Don't go it alone in a difficult start; actively seek capable helpers, and approach them with humility rather than authority.
- Be suspicious of unexpected help that arrives from an unrelated source — accepting it can cost you your freedom of decision later.
- If you lack proper guidance or preparation for a goal, desisting is the mature move; pressing on without it invites humiliation, not victory.
- When your good intentions are being blocked or misread by circumstance, don't escalate to a forceful, one-time push — small consistent perseverance succeeds where a grand gesture fails.
- If a situation has become truly stuck and isolated, stop enduring it passively — that is the moment to change course, not double down.
