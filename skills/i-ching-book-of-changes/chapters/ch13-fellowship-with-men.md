# Hexagram 13: T'ung Jên / Fellowship with Men

## Core Idea
Fire flames naturally up toward heaven — the image of true fellowship, which succeeds only when grounded in universal, shared concern rather than private or factional interest.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Ch'ien (Heaven, strength)
- Lower: Li (Fire, clarity)

## The Judgment
> FELLOWSHIP WITH MEN in the open. Success. It furthers one to cross the great water. The perseverance of the superior man furthers.

**Meaning**: True fellowship must rest on goals shared broadly among people, not private interest; when this kind of unity prevails, even great and dangerous undertakings can succeed — but it requires a persevering, clear-sighted leader to bring it about.

## The Image
> Heaven together with fire: the image of FELLOWSHIP WITH MEN. Thus the superior man organizes the clans and makes distinctions between things.

**Meaning**: Because a genuine fellowship must be organized rather than a mere undifferentiated mingling, the superior man arranges people and things according to what actually belongs together, giving fellowship real order instead of chaos.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Fellowship with men at the gate. No blame." — Union formed openly, right at the outset before any divergent aims or secret agreements have arisen, is blameless.
- **Six in the second place**: "Fellowship with men in the clan. Humiliation." — Fellowship narrowed to a private faction based on personal or exclusive interest inevitably leads to humiliation.
- **Nine in the third place**: "He hides weapons in the thicket; he climbs the high hill in front of it. For three years he does not rise up." — Mutual mistrust and secret maneuvering against a rival only drives both parties further from true fellowship the longer it continues.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "He climbs up on his wall; he cannot attack. Good fortune." — Recognizing that a contested position is untenable and standing down from open conflict, rather than persisting in a fight one cannot win, is itself the good fortune.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "Men bound in fellowship first weep and lament, but afterward they laugh. After great struggles they succeed in meeting." — Two people kept apart by circumstance but united at heart will, after real struggle, overcome the obstacles between them and turn their sorrow into joy.
- **Nine at the top**: "Fellowship with men in the meadow. No remorse." — A fellowship limited to those simply nearby, not yet reaching the full ideal of universal union, still carries no cause for regret.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The six in the second place and the nine in the fifth place are the hexagram's rulers: the sole yielding line finds the correct, central place, and the Creative (the strong lines) corresponds with it — this correspondence is literally how the T'uan Chuan defines "fellowship with men."
- The T'uan Chuan stresses that this fellowship is brought about "by the official, not by the ruler" — by virtue of character rather than positional authority — and that "first knowledge, then strength" (the qualities of Li and Ch'ien respectively) is the road to real culture.
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams places T'ung Jên directly after P'i (Standstill): "things cannot be at a standstill forever," so fellowship is the natural next movement once stagnation breaks.
- The commentary notes a structural echo with Hexagram 44 (Kou, Coming to Meet): the two nuclear trigrams here, Ch'ien and Sun, are the very trigrams that together form that hexagram, reinforcing the fellowship theme.
- A closing note frames the whole hexagram as an unattained ideal: none of the six individual lines reaches the "great success" the hexagram as a whole envisions — each seeks fellowship on some narrower basis (a gate, a clan, a wall, a meadow), and the demand for true universal brotherhood points beyond every situation the lines actually depict.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fifth place: "Men bound in fellowship first weep and lament, but afterward they laugh. After great struggles they succeed in meeting." The commentary explains that this line and the six in the second place stand in direct, correct correspondence, but the third and fourth lines initially obstruct their union, causing real grief. Because the bond is central and correct, however, the two eventually do meet — and Confucius's appended words capture the deeper point: when two people are truly united in their innermost hearts, that bond "shatters even the strength of iron or bronze." The line shows that genuine fellowship is not exempt from real obstacles; it is proven by surviving them.

## Key Takeaways
- Build fellowship on goals broad enough to include others generally, not on private advantage — narrow, self-interested unions curdle into humiliation.
- Form alliances and agreements openly, at the very start, rather than through secret arrangements that only a few are party to.
- Recognize when mutual suspicion has replaced real cooperation — prolonged mistrust and hidden maneuvering pushes people further apart the longer it's allowed to continue.
- When you find yourself in an unwinnable contest for someone's loyalty or position, standing down is the fortunate move, not a defeat.
- Expect real struggle before a deeply aligned partnership or reunion comes together — the difficulty doesn't disprove the bond, it precedes its fulfillment.
- A fellowship limited to convenient or nearby people is still worthwhile even if it falls short of a larger ideal — don't let an unattained ultimate goal erase the value of the more modest union you actually have.
