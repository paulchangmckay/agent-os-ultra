# The Structure of the Hexagrams

## Core Idea
A hexagram's meaning isn't read off its name alone — it comes from a fixed structural grammar: which two trigrams compose it, what "time" (situation-type) it represents, which places its lines occupy, whether firm/yielding lines sit correctly, and how lines relate to each other through correspondence and holding-together. Learn this grammar once and you can work out why any of the sixty-four hexagrams says what it says, instead of treating each one as a one-off riddle.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Correspondence**: lines in analogous places in the lower and upper trigram — first/fourth, second/fifth, third/top — have an especially close relationship when they differ in kind (one firm, one yielding). The second and fifth (the two central lines) are the most important case: they stand as official to ruler, son to father, wife to husband. A strong second line corresponding with a yielding fifth-place ruler is favorable in most cases (source lists hexagrams 4, 7, 11, 14, 18, 19, 32, 34, 38, 40, 41, 46, 50 as wholly favorable this way); a yielding second corresponding with a strong fifth-place ruler is markedly less reliable, unfavorable in several cases (12, 13, 17, 20, 31) and merely difficult-but-explainable in others (3, 33, 39, 63). Correspondence between first and fourth follows the same firm/yielding-must-differ rule; correspondence between third and top "hardly ever occurs" as a genuine, favorable relationship.
- **Holding Together**: a relationship between two *adjacent* lines of different character, described as "receiving" from the lower line's side and "resting upon" from the upper's. The fourth-and-fifth pair (minister and ruler) matters most: a yielding minister holding together with a strong ruler is favorable (very favorable in hexagrams 8, 9, 20, 29, 37, 42, 48, 53, 57, 59, 60, 61) because proximity plus reverence works; a strong, "incorrect" fourth line holding together with a yielding ruler is generally unfavorable (30, 32, 35, 50, 51) since it reads as a minister overshadowing his ruler.
- **Ruling Line(s) of a hexagram**: two distinct kinds of "ruler." The *constituting* ruler is whichever line gives the hexagram its defining meaning, regardless of whether that line is admirable (example given: the weak top line of hexagram 43, Break-Through, which the whole hexagram is *about* casting out). The *governing* ruler is the line — usually but not always in the fifth place — that is genuinely good in character and rules by virtue of both position and the demands of the time. When the same line is both, the hexagram has one clean ruler; when they differ, it has two, and that split is itself diagnostic (it signals the line's character and its place don't match the time's demands).
- **The Places (line positions 1–6)**: odd positions (1, 3, 5) are light/superior places; even positions (2, 4, 6) are dark/inferior places. A line is "correct" when firm (undivided) lines sit in odd places or yielding (divided) lines sit in even places — but correctness is not automatically favorable; it depends on what the hexagram's time demands. Position meanings: 1st = beginning, not yet entered the situation; 2nd = official/son, "central" by default, usually favorably judged; 3rd = transitional, unstable — sits at the boundary between the lower and upper trigram, usually the riskiest of the six; 4th = minister, close to the ruler, usually judged cautiously ("too close to the prince, must be doubly cautious"); 5th = the ruler's place, central and strong by default; 6th (top) = has withdrawn from the affairs of the time, or (in the light trigrams) the sage beyond worldly position.
- **Nuclear trigrams (hu kua)**: formed from a hexagram's four *middle* lines (positions 2–5), overlapping so the upper nuclear trigram is lines 3-4-5 and the lower nuclear trigram is lines 2-3-4. These add a second, hidden layer of trigram meaning beneath the two primary (outer) trigrams. Consequence: the bottom and top lines belong to only one trigram each (their primary trigram only) and so "tend to drop out of connection"; the second and fifth lines belong to two trigrams each (primary + one nuclear) and land in relative equilibrium, "usually favorable"; the third and fourth lines belong to *three* trigrams each (both primary trigrams they sit between, plus both nuclear trigrams), which is why they carry the most structural tension.
- **Time**: the situation-type the whole hexagram represents. The source text gives four distinct senses this can take: a movement's decrease/growth or emptiness/fullness (e.g., Peace/11, Standstill/12, Splitting Apart/23, Return/24); a characteristic action or process (Conflict/6, The Army/7, Biting Through/21, Providing Nourishment/27); a law or principle (Treading/10, Modesty/15, Influence/31, Duration/32); or a symbolic situation as such (The Well/48, The Caldron/50). Time is determinative: the same line value (say, a six in the third place) reads as favorable or unfavorable depending entirely on which time-sense governs the hexagram it's in.

## Key Concepts
- **Firm / yielding** — undivided (—) vs. divided (- -) lines; the raw material every other structural feature (place, correspondence, ruler) is evaluated against.
- **Central** — the second and fifth line positions, central within their respective trigram regardless of firm/yielding character; central position is favorable in the great majority of cases even without "correctness."
- **Correct** — a firm line in an odd place, or a yielding line in an even place; necessary-but-not-sufficient for a favorable reading, since the time can override it.
- **Constituting ruler vs. governing ruler** — see Frameworks above; marked in the traditional commentary by a square (constituting) versus a circle (governing), collapsing to one circle when identical.
- **Going / coming** — lines emphasized in the upper trigram are described as "going"; lines emphasized in the lower trigram as "coming."
- **Primary trigram vs. nuclear trigram** — the two outer (defining) trigrams versus the two overlapping inner trigrams built from lines 2-3-4 and 3-4-5.
- **Inner / outer** — the lower trigram is "within, below, behind"; the upper trigram is "above, without, in front."

## Worked Example
The source text itself works through Li, THE CLINGING, FIRE (hexagram 30), to demonstrate nuclear trigrams: its four middle lines (positions 2–5) split into an upper nuclear trigram of Tui (the Joyous) and a lower nuclear trigram of Sun (the Gentle) — a second, hidden pair of trigrams layered underneath the hexagram's primary Li-over-Li structure. The text also works a second example, Chung Fu, INNER TRUTH (hexagram 61), whose four middle lines yield Kên (Keeping Still) above and Chên (the Arousing) below as nuclear trigrams. Applying the correspondence/ruler framework on top of this same layered structure: for any hexagram, first identify its two primary trigrams (top and bottom), then its two nuclear trigrams (from the middle four lines), then check which line is the constituting ruler (what the hexagram is fundamentally about) versus the governing ruler (usually the strong, central fifth line, if it is genuinely good) — the source notes this ruler can always be confirmed against the Commentary on the Decision (the T'uan Chuan) for that hexagram.

## Key Takeaways
- Start every hexagram reading by identifying the two primary trigrams (top/bottom) and, if you need a deeper reading, the two nuclear trigrams hidden in lines 2–5 — this gives you four trigram-layers of meaning before you even look at individual line texts.
- Identify the hexagram's "time" first (which of the four senses: movement, process, law, or symbolic situation) — the same line value means different things depending on which time-type governs the hexagram.
- Check every line for three things in order: is it firm or yielding, is it correctly placed for its position (odd/firm or even/yielding), and is it central (2nd or 5th place) — central position usually outweighs mere correctness.
- Check the second and fifth lines for correspondence (do they differ in kind, and how does official relate to ruler) — this is usually the single most informative relationship in the hexagram.
- Check the fourth and fifth lines for holding together (adjacent-line relationship between minister and ruler) — a yielding minister next to a strong ruler is usually favorable; the reverse usually is not.
- Locate the constituting ruler (what the hexagram is essentially about) and the governing ruler (the genuinely good line ruling by position) separately — when they're the same line, the hexagram is clean; when they differ, that gap is itself part of the message.
- Treat the first and top lines as structurally looser (each belongs to only one trigram) — read them as "not yet in the situation" and "already withdrawn from it" respectively, unless the specific time explicitly makes them active (the source flags Difficulty at the Beginning/3, Possession in Great Measure/14, Contemplation/20, Taming Power of the Great/26, and Increase/42 as such exceptions).
