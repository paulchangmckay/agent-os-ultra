# Hexagram 36: Ming I / Darkening of the Light

## Core Idea
The sun has sunk beneath the earth and its light is wounded — a person of true worth must survive under a dark, hostile authority by veiling inner clarity while never abandoning it.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'un (Earth, the Receptive)
- Lower: Li (the Clinging, Light, Fire)

## The Judgment
> DARKENING OF THE LIGHT. In adversity it furthers one to be persevering.

**Meaning**: One must not be swept along by unfavorable circumstances nor let steadfastness be shaken; by keeping the inner light while remaining outwardly yielding, even the greatest adversity can be overcome.

## The Image
> The light has sunk into the earth: the image of DARKENING OF THE LIGHT. Thus does the superior man live with the great mass: he veils his light, yet still shines.

**Meaning**: In dark times, caution and reserve are essential — avoid needlessly provoking hostility, avoid censoriously exposing others' faults, and let many things pass without being personally compromised.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Darkening of the light during flight. He lowers his wings. The superior man does not eat for three days on his wanderings. But he has somewhere to go. The host has occasion to gossip about him." — Fleeing a hostile fate without rest or fixed abode, refusing to compromise principle even at the cost of privation, while still holding a fixed goal others fail to understand.
- **Six in the second place**: "Darkening of the light injures him in the left thigh. He gives aid with the strength of a horse. Good fortune." — Wounded but not fatally, he thinks only of saving others still in danger, and this dutiful effort brings good fortune.
- **Nine in the third place**: "Darkening of the light during the hunt in the south. Their great leader is captured. One must not expect perseverance too soon." — The ringleader of disorder is seized as if by chance while striving in good faith; abuses long entrenched must not be corrected too hastily.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "He penetrates the left side of the belly. One gets at the very heart of the darkening of the light, and leaves gate and courtyard." — Standing close to the source of darkness, one discovers there is no hope of improvement and so leaves the scene before disaster breaks.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Darkening of the light as with Prince Chi. Perseverance furthers." — Unable to leave his post, he conceals his true sentiments (feigning insanity) while never abandoning his convictions inwardly.
- **Six at the top**: "Not light but darkness. First he climbed up to heaven, then he plunged into the depths of the earth." — The dark power, having risen high enough to wound all who serve the light, ultimately perishes of its own darkness once it has wholly overcome the good.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram carries an explicit historical layer: the six at the top is the tyrant Chou Hsin; the nine at the beginning is Po I (who withdrew into hiding); the six in the second is King Wên (long imprisoned by the tyrant); the nine in the third is King Wu (who overthrew him); the six in the fourth is Prince Wei Tzu (who escaped by timely flight); the six in the fifth is Prince Chi (who survived by feigning madness).
- The Commentary on the Decision pairs the hexagram's two trigram attributes directly with these figures: Li's "beautiful and clear within" with King Wên's inner nature, K'un's "gentle and devoted without" with his outward conduct under adversity — the hexagram is presented as literally built around his historical situation.
- The text is noted as the structural inverse of Hexagram 35 (Progress), reinforcing that these two hexagrams are a deliberate paired opposite: rising clear light versus light sunk and wounded.
- The fifth line's endurance is explained through the nuclear trigram Chên (arousal, pressing forward) sitting above it — showing structurally that the veiled light cannot be permanently suppressed and will press forward again once conditions allow.

## Worked Example
Consider the six in the fifth place: "Darkening of the light as with Prince Chi. Perseverance furthers." Someone trapped in a position they cannot honorably abandon — bound by duty, family, or circumstance to a hostile or compromised authority — faces the same problem Prince Chi faced: open resistance would be fatal, but silent complicity would betray the self. This line shows the path is neither: conceal the outward expression of one's convictions while never surrendering them inwardly. It sharpens the Judgment's abstract counsel — "veiling one's light" — into a concrete, survivable strategy for someone who has no way out and must simply endure with integrity intact.

## Key Takeaways
- When you cannot escape a hostile environment, protect your convictions by concealing them, not by abandoning them.
- If you can see clearly that a situation offers no hope of improvement, leave before the crisis breaks rather than staying to witness it.
- When correcting long-entrenched abuses, do not expect immediate stability — go carefully rather than demanding instant order.
- In danger, look past your own wound to whoever else is still exposed — helping others in your own moment of injury can still bring good fortune.
- Trust that a dark power which has become total contains the seed of its own collapse — it exhausts the very energy that let it rise.
