# Hexagram 6: Sung / Conflict

## Core Idea
Strength above and danger-driven cunning below pull apart from each other rather than together, producing genuine conflict — the hexagram's counsel throughout is not how to win a conflict but how to avoid letting it run to the bitter end.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Ch'ien (Heaven, strength)
- Lower: K'an (the Abysmal, danger)

## The Judgment
> CONFLICT. You are sincere and are being obstructed. A cautious halt halfway brings good fortune. Going through to the end brings misfortune. It furthers one to see the great man. It does not further one to cross the great water.

**Meaning**: Even when you are in the right, carrying a conflict all the way through perpetuates enmity; the wiser course is to meet the opponent halfway, seek an impartial arbiter, and avoid launching new risky ventures while the underlying conflict saps unified strength.

## The Image
> Heaven and water go their opposite ways: the image of CONFLICT. Thus in all his transactions the superior man carefully considers the beginning.

**Meaning**: Because conflict is latent in opposing tendencies from the start, the superior man heads it off in advance by clearly defining rights and duties at the outset, before positions have hardened.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "If one does not perpetuate the affair, there is a little gossip. In the end, good fortune comes." — Dropping a dispute in its early stage, rather than pushing it to a decision, leads to a good outcome despite minor friction.
- **Nine in the second place**: "One cannot engage in conflict; one returns home, gives way. The people of his town, three hundred households, remain free of guilt." — Timely withdrawal from an unequal contest with a stronger opponent is no disgrace, and it protects the wider community from being drawn in.
- **Six in the third place**: "To nourish oneself on ancient virtue induces perseverance. Danger. In the end, good fortune comes. If by chance you are in the service of a king, seek not works." — What is honestly and honorably acquired cannot be taken away; avoid conflict in service to a superior by not seeking credit for yourself.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "One cannot engage in conflict. One turns back and submits to fate, changes one's attitude, and finds peace in perseverance. Good fortune." — Recognizing that right is not on your side, turning back from a fight you could win but shouldn't pursue brings lasting peace.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "To contend before him brings supreme good fortune." — Bringing a dispute before a powerful, impartial arbiter who can render a just decision leads to the best possible outcome.
- **Nine at the top**: "Even if by chance a leather belt is bestowed on one, by the end of a morning it will have been snatched away three times." — Carrying a conflict all the way through to victory brings only a distinction that cannot last; what is won by force is taken away by force.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine in the fifth place is the ruler of the hexagram — the impartial judge who "overhears the quarrel" — while every other line represents a party actually caught up in the contention.
- The T'uan Chuan derives the hexagram's meaning directly from its trigrams: strength (Ch'ien) above and danger/cunning (K'an) below produce conflict almost automatically, and structurally this hexagram is described as the exact inverse of Hexagram 5 (Waiting) — conflict here, forbearance there.
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams explains Sung's placement: "over meat and drink, there is certain to be conflict" — dispute follows naturally from the shared nourishment and waiting of the previous hexagram.
- A closing structural note maps the six lines onto roles in a dispute: the judge (fifth line) settles it; the weak first and third lines hold back from contention; the strong second and fourth lines are inclined to contend but withdraw once they recognize the judge's authority or the futility of the fight; only the strong top line pushes the conflict all the way through — and the commentary explicitly likens it to the "arrogant dragon" at the top of Ch'ien, warning that its victory will be regretted.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fourth place: "One cannot engage in conflict. One turns back and submits to fate, changes one's attitude, and finds peace in perseverance." The commentary notes this line is neither central nor correct and was originally inclined to quarrel — unlike the second line, it faces a weaker opponent and might actually win. But because right is not genuinely on its side, pursuing the fight would violate its own conscience. The line's good fortune comes specifically from the internal reversal — choosing peace over a winnable fight — which shows that the hexagram values the rightness of the cause over the likelihood of victory.

## Key Takeaways
- When a dispute is just beginning, consider dropping it rather than pushing for a decision — early withdrawal often costs less than winning outright.
- Retreating from an unequal conflict is not a loss of honor; it protects you and everyone connected to you from unnecessary harm.
- Let credit for honorably completed work go to whoever holds authority over you — seeking recognition for yourself invites conflict you don't need.
- If you find you could win a fight but your position isn't actually right, turning back brings more lasting peace than the win would.
- When a conflict can't be resolved between the parties themselves, bring it to an impartial, authoritative third party rather than letting it drag on.
- Winning a conflict all the way to the end is not the same as resolving it — victories taken by force tend to be taken back by force.
