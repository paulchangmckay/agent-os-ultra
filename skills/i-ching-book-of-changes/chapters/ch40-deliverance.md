# Hexagram 40: Hsieh / Deliverance

## Core Idea
Movement has carried us out of the sphere of danger — tension is beginning to ease — but deliverance has only just begun, and the task now is to return quickly to normal order without overreaching or lingering unnecessarily.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Chên (Thunder, the Arousing, Movement)
- Lower: K'an (Water, the Abysmal, Danger)

## The Judgment
> DELIVERANCE. The southwest furthers. If there is no longer anything where one has to go, return brings good fortune. If there is still something where one has to go, hastening brings good fortune.

**Meaning**: When tensions begin to ease, return to ordinary conditions as quickly as possible; if any residual matters remain, attend to them swiftly and thoroughly rather than letting them linger — the point is not to overdo the triumph or push further than necessary.

## The Image
> Thunder and rain set in: the image of DELIVERANCE. Thus the superior man pardons mistakes and forgives misdeeds.

**Meaning**: A thunderstorm clears the air; likewise the superior man deals with others' failings by letting unintentional mistakes pass, as thunder dies away, and by forgiving deliberate wrongs, as rain washes everything clean.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "Without blame." — With the hindrance now past, quietly recuperating and keeping still is exactly right; little needs to be said or done.
- **Nine in the second place**: "One kills three foxes in the field and receives a yellow arrow. Perseverance brings good fortune." — Removing the cunning, flattering influences that obstruct deliverance requires proceeding with measured means (yellow) and a straight course (the arrow); inner rectitude becomes a weapon against what is false.
- **Six in the third place**: "If a man carries a burden on his back and nonetheless rides in a carriage, he thereby encourages robbers to draw near. Perseverance leads to humiliation." — Someone acting above their real station, comfortable in circumstances that don't suit their nature, invites exploitation and disgrace by their own carelessness.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "Deliver yourself from your great toe. Then the companion comes, and him you can trust." — Freeing oneself from unworthy but habitual attachments (as indispensable as a big toe feels to walking) allows genuinely trustworthy allies to approach.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "If only the superior man can deliver himself, it brings good fortune. Thus he proves to inferior men that he is in earnest." — Inferior influences cannot be driven off by external prohibition alone; only a genuine inner break with them, visibly in earnest, causes them to withdraw on their own.
- **Six at the top**: "The prince shoots at a hawk on a high wall. He kills it. Everything serves to further." — A powerful, hardened inferior obstructing deliverance from a high position must be forcibly removed with properly prepared means; acting only once truly ready ensures success.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The Commentary on the Decision frames the hexagram's core mechanism plainly: "Danger produces movement. Through movement one escapes danger: this is deliverance" — the trigram structure (K'an's danger below, Chên's movement above) is the literal source of the hexagram's meaning.
- The text explicitly connects this hexagram to Hexagram 3 (Chun, Difficulty at the Beginning) as a further development of the same danger-and-movement relationship, and notes it is the structural inverse of the preceding hexagram (Obstruction) — obstruction resolved becomes deliverance.
- The natural analogy is extended precisely: "When heaven and earth deliver themselves, thunder and rain set in... the seed pods of all fruits, plants, and trees break open" — deliverance is framed as a cosmic pattern, not merely a personal or social one.
- Confucius's added remarks on the third line (carrying a burden while riding in a carriage) and the top line (the hunter who "contains the means in his own person" and "bides his time") both extend the bare line text into fuller counsel on timing and self-invited misfortune — model instances of commentary adding reasoning beyond the terse original.

## Worked Example
Consider the nine in the fourth place: "Deliver yourself from your great toe. Then the companion comes, and him you can trust." Someone emerging from a difficult period often finds they've grown attached to relationships or habits formed purely out of necessity during the hard time — associations that felt indispensable but were never truly aligned with their values. This line counsels a deliberate break from such attachments as the very first act of deliverance, because trustworthy allies who share real purpose will not come close while those lesser attachments remain. It deepens the Judgment's instruction to "return" quickly: the return is not just geographic or circumstantial but relational — cutting loose what no longer belongs before you can attract what does.

## Key Takeaways
- Once real danger has passed, resist the urge to keep fighting; recuperate and return to normal order rather than pushing further than necessary.
- Deal with any lingering obstacles briskly and with measured, principled means, not haphazardly or with excess force.
- Watch for the trap of enjoying comfort or status you haven't actually earned — it invites exploitation.
- Deliberately release habitual but unworthy attachments formed during hard times before expecting genuinely trustworthy allies to draw close.
- When an entrenched, powerful obstruction remains after everything else has cleared, prepare your means fully and act decisively rather than half-heartedly.
