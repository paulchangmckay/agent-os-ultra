# Hexagram 43: Kuai / Break-through (Resoluteness)

## Core Idea
After long accumulated tension, resolute action finally breaks through — inferior influence, though still clinging to a position of power, must be confronted openly, truthfully, and without resort to force or hatred.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Tui (the Lake, the Joyous)
- Lower: Ch'ien (Heaven, the Creative, Strength)

## The Judgment
> BREAK-THROUGH. One must resolutely make the matter known at the court of the king. It must be announced truthfully. Danger. It is necessary to notify one's own city. It does not further to resort to arms. It furthers one to undertake something.

**Meaning**: Even one inferior man in power can oppress the good, just as one lingering passion can obscure reason — resolute, truthful confrontation is necessary, but it must never be carried out through direct force, since branding evil and fighting it blow for blow only entangles the fighter in hatred and passion.

## The Image
> The lake has risen up to heaven: the image of BREAK-THROUGH. Thus the superior man dispenses riches downward and refrains from resting on his virtue.

**Meaning**: Water gathered dangerously high, like a lake risen to heaven, warns of an imminent violent collapse — the superior man forestalls this by continuously distributing while accumulating, and by remaining receptive to self-examination rather than becoming self-satisfied.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Mighty in the forward-striding toes. When one goes and is not equal to the task, one makes a mistake." — At the very start of a resolute advance, resistance is still strong; pressing ahead beyond one's actual strength invites an early, disastrous setback.
- **Nine in the second place**: "A cry of alarm. Arms at evening and at night. Fear nothing." — Constant readiness and caution, being on guard before danger is even visible, is what allows one to face real danger without fear.
- **Nine in the third place**: "To be powerful in the cheekbones brings misfortune. The superior man is firmly resolved. He walks alone and is caught in the rain. He is bespattered, and people murmur against him. No blame." — Maintaining an ambiguous but necessary association with an inferior person, while staying inwardly resolved, invites misjudgment and grumbling from others, yet remaining true to oneself is not a mistake.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "There is no skin on his thighs, and walking comes hard. If a man were to let himself be led like a sheep, remorse would disappear. But if these words are heard they will not be believed." — Obstinate insistence on forcing one's own way creates painful, self-inflicted difficulty; good advice to yield is ignored precisely because of that same obstinacy.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "In dealing with weeds, firm resolution is necessary. Walking in the middle remains free of blame." — Struggling against deep-rooted inferior influence in a high position, which regrows easily like weeds, requires unwavering resolve and a balanced, undeflected course.
- **Six at the top**: "No cry. In the end misfortune comes." — Even when victory looks nearly complete, the remaining trace of evil is most dangerous precisely because it looks easy to finish; carelessness here lets new misfortune sprout from what was overlooked.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The Commentary on the Decision frames the hexagram's ethical stance precisely: "Strong and joyous — this means resolute and harmonious," pairing Ch'ien's strength with Tui's joyousness to insist that resoluteness against evil must remain serene and calm, not hateful.
- The hexagram is explicitly identified as the next developmental stage after Hexagram 34 (Ta Chuang, The Power of the Great) — the first line's text is noted as identical in both hexagrams, only "moderated somewhat, because the situation has developed further."
- The text stresses that "it does not further to resort to arms" is central to the whole hexagram's meaning: since the outcome (removing the single inferior line) is not genuinely in doubt, force only risks entangling the resolute party in the very passions being opposed.
- The top line's warning is explained structurally: it is the sole remaining dark line, and its silence ("no cry") reflects its capacity to suppress warning voices — the commentary treats vigilance against a nearly-defeated evil as more important than vigilance against an active one.

## Worked Example
Consider the nine in the third place: "To be powerful in the cheekbones brings misfortune. The superior man is firmly resolved. He walks alone and is caught in the rain. He is bespattered, and people murmur against him. No blame." Someone working to correct a systemic problem — corruption in an organization, a harmful pattern in a family — may find themselves forced to maintain some association with the very person or practice they oppose, perhaps to avoid tipping their hand too early. This line shows that such a position invites public misjudgment: onlookers assume complicity where there is actually calculated restraint. It deepens the Judgment's warning against direct force by showing the personal cost of the alternative — patient, misunderstood resolve — and insists that cost does not constitute a mistake, so long as inner resolution stays intact.

## Key Takeaways
- Gauge your actual strength before pressing an advance — early overreach against resistance still strong causes the most damage.
- Stay constantly alert and prepared, even before danger is visible; readiness itself removes fear when danger does arrive.
- Expect to be misjudged when your path toward resolving a problem requires temporary, calculated proximity to what you oppose — endure the misunderstanding rather than abandoning the strategy.
- Never fight entrenched wrongdoing with direct force or hatred; branding it and attacking blow for blow only entangles you in the same passions.
- Treat the final, seemingly easy stage of eliminating a problem with the most caution, not the least — a nearly-finished evil is exactly what slips past a relaxed guard.
