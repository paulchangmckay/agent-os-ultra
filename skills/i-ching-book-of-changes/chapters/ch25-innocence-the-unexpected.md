# Hexagram 25: Wu Wang / Innocence (The Unexpected)

## Core Idea
Innocence describes movement that follows the law of heaven without ulterior purpose or reflection — the natural, unsullied state of mind that acts rightly by instinct, and, when disrupted, also names the misfortune that comes unexpectedly and from outside oneself.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Ch'ien, heaven
- Lower: Chên, movement/thunder

## The Judgment
> INNOCENCE. Supreme success. Perseverance furthers. If someone is not as he should be, he has misfortune, and it does not further him to undertake anything.

**Meaning**: Devotion to the innately good nature received from heaven brings instinctive rightness and supreme success, but only when that instinct is genuinely in accord with heaven's will — instinctive action without that rightness brings only misfortune.

## The Image
> Under heaven thunder rolls: all things attain the natural state of innocence. Thus the kings of old, rich in virtue, and in harmony with the time, fostered and nourished all beings.

**Meaning**: Just as thunder in spring stirs all things into their natural, childlike original state, good rulers draw on their own spiritual resources to nurture all forms of life and culture, and to do so at the proper time.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Innocent behavior brings good fortune." — The heart's original impulses are good, so one may follow them confidently, assured of good fortune.
- **Six in the second place**: "If one does not count on the harvest while plowing, nor on the use of the ground while clearing it, it furthers one to undertake something." — Do every task for its own sake as the time demands, without an eye to the result, and it succeeds.
- **Six in the third place**: "Undeserved misfortune. The cow that was tethered by someone is the wanderer's gain, the citizen's loss." — Undeserved misfortune can befall even the innocent through another's action; one must accommodate the demands of the time or be overtaken by the unexpected.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "He who can be persevering remains without blame." — What truly belongs to you cannot be lost even if thrown away; the only real concern is staying true to your own nature rather than listening to others.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "Use no medicine in an illness incurred through no fault of your own. It will pass of itself." — An evil arising accidentally from outside, with no foothold in one's own nature, should be left to pass on its own rather than treated with external remedies.
- **Nine at the top**: "Innocent action brings misfortune. Nothing furthers." — When the time is not ripe for further progress, waiting quietly is best; pushing ahead against fate, even without ill intent, fails to succeed.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram's rulers are the nine at the beginning and the nine in the fifth place: the first is "the firm [that] comes from without and becomes the ruler within," the primal divine spirit received as guide; the fifth, central and correspondent, embodies "the essence of the Creative" and "the tirelessness of the supremely sincere."
- The Commentary notes this hexagram differs from hexagram 12 (P'i, Standstill) only in having a firm line at the beginning — if that firmness were lost, the whole situation would change, underscoring how much the entire structure depends on that one line.
- The Discussion of the Trigrams gloss ("God comes forth in the sign of the Arousing") ties the Image's thunder directly to "the beginning of all life," reinforcing that innocence here is life's very first, uncorrupted stirring.
- The Note on the six lines observes that everything depends on timing: the first and second lines' good fortune comes because the time has come for them to act, while the third, fifth, and top lines' misfortune or caution (illness, undeserved loss, thoughtless action) reflect a time for remaining quiet rather than any failure of innocence itself.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fifth place: "Use no medicine in an illness incurred through no fault of your own. It will pass of itself." The commentary explains this line represents someone by nature free of illness, whose apparent sickness comes from vicariously taking on the ills of others because of his central, correct, ruling position. This deepens the Judgment's core distinction between true innocence and misfortune: an evil that has no real foothold in one's own nature is fundamentally external, and treating it with external remedies misunderstands its source — the right response is patience, letting nature run its course, not intervention.

## Key Takeaways
- Trust your original, unreflective impulses when they are genuinely free of ulterior motive — they carry their own instinctive rightness.
- Do the task itself well without fixating on the payoff; results follow naturally from right action, not from calculating them in advance.
- Recognize that some misfortune is truly external and undeserved — don't over-interpret it as a personal failing, but do adapt to the demands of the moment.
- When an unexpected trouble has no real root in your own nature, let it resolve on its own rather than forcing an intervention.
- Know when the time for action has passed — persisting or pushing ahead once the moment is gone brings misfortune regardless of good intent.
