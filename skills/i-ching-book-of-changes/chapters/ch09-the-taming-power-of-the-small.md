# Hexagram 9: Hsiao Ch'u / The Taming Power of the Small

## Core Idea
A single weak line restrains five strong lines, like wind driving clouds together across the sky without yet being strong enough to bring rain — real but limited influence, achieved only through gentleness and small, patient means rather than force.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Sun (Wind, gentleness)
- Lower: Ch'ien (Heaven, strength)

## The Judgment
> THE TAMING POWER OF THE SMALL has success. Dense clouds, no rain from our western region.

**Meaning**: The moment for decisive, large-scale action has not yet arrived; only small means — friendly persuasion, restraint, patience — can exert influence now, and success will come gradually rather than all at once.

## The Image
> The wind drives across heaven: the image of THE TAMING POWER OF THE SMALL. Thus the superior man refines the outward aspect of his nature.

**Meaning**: Because wind, lacking solid substance, can gather clouds but not yet produce rain, a person unable to make a great impact on the outer world should focus instead on refining themselves in small ways.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Return to the way. How could there be blame in this? Good fortune." — Encountering an obstruction, a strong man wisely returns to a path suited to his situation rather than forcing his way forward.
- **Nine in the second place**: "He allows himself to be drawn into returning. Good fortune." — Seeing that the way forward is blocked, retreating alongside others of like mind avoids needless personal risk.
- **Nine in the third place**: "The spokes burst out of the wagon wheels. Man and wife roll their eyes." — Forcing an advance against a restraint that only seems weak leads to breakdown and conflict, compromising one's own dignity.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "If you are sincere, blood vanishes and fear gives way. No blame." — In the dangerous position of restraining someone more powerful, the force of disinterested sincerity is strong enough to dissolve the danger entirely.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "If you are sincere and loyally attached, you are rich in your neighbor." — Mutual loyalty between complementary partners creates a shared wealth that is greater for being shared rather than hoarded.
- **Nine at the top**: "The rain comes, there is rest. This is due to the lasting effect of character. Perseverance brings the woman into danger. The moon is nearly full. If the superior man persists, misfortune comes." — Success achieved gradually through accumulated character must not be pressed further once attained; presuming on it or persisting past this point invites misfortune.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The six in the fourth place is the hexagram's constituting ruler (the only yin line, holding the decisive place) while the nine in the fifth place is its governing ruler, whose attitude accords with and perfects the fourth line's restraint — together they explain how "the strong is central and its will is done."
- The T'uan Chuan explains the "dense clouds, no rain" image structurally: the upper trigram Sun (wind) has enough strength to condense the rising vapor of the lower trigram Ch'ien into clouds, but not enough to bring actual rain — success is real but partial.
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams places Hsiao Ch'u directly after Pi (Holding Together): once people hold together, some form of restraint naturally follows.
- The Miscellaneous Notes explicitly contrast this hexagram with Hexagram 14 (Ta Yu, Possession in Great Measure): here "the small" occupies the place of the official (fourth line), while there the small, yielding element occupies the ruler's place (fifth line) — a structural pairing worth noting.
- Line-level commentary on the top line explains that because it is a moving nine, the trigram Sun (wind) turns into K'an (rain and moon) — the rain's arrival is literally the transformation this hexagram is building toward, and the warning against persistence reflects the danger of an accumulated small success being pushed past its natural limit.

## Worked Example
Take the six in the fourth place: "If you are sincere, blood vanishes and fear gives way. No blame." The commentary describes this line as sitting in the genuinely dangerous position of a minister trying to restrain a powerful ruler with only weak means at hand — a task that could easily provoke violence. Yet because the line is "empty within," meaning purely sincere rather than self-interested, the danger dissolves: the nine in the fifth place recognizes this sincerity and supports it rather than resisting it. The line shows that in an inherently precarious position of influence over someone stronger, disinterested honesty — not cleverness or leverage — is what actually neutralizes the risk.

## Key Takeaways
- When you meet real obstruction, don't force your way through — return to a path that fits your actual situation and wait for a better moment.
- If you see that others in your position are also being blocked, retreat alongside them rather than isolating yourself in a losing push.
- Don't mistake a restraint that looks weak for one that actually is weak — pressing against it anyway can break things you can't easily repair, including your own standing.
- When you must restrain or advise someone more powerful than you, rely on transparent sincerity rather than pressure or cleverness — genuine disinterestedness is what disarms the danger.
- Build wealth, influence, or trust as something shared with a loyal partner or ally, not something hoarded — mutual reinforcement makes both parties richer.
- Once a gradual, hard-won success is achieved, resist the urge to push further or presume upon it — declare the accumulated gain sufficient and hold there, rather than risk it by insisting on more.
