# Hexagram 15: Ch'ien / Modesty

## Core Idea
A mountain hidden within the earth — height concealed beneath lowliness — shows how modesty functions in both the great and the humble: it lifts up the lowly and lets the strong carry their work through without display.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'un (Earth)
- Lower: Kên (Mountain, keeping still)

## The Judgment
> MODESTY creates success. The superior man carries things through.

**Meaning**: It is the law of both heaven and earth to empty out fullness and to fill up modesty — the sun declines from its zenith, mountains are worn down while valleys fill — and this same law governs human fate, so a person's own modest or arrogant conduct determines whether benevolent or destructive forces shape their outcome.

## The Image
> Within the earth, a mountain: the image of MODESTY. Thus the superior man reduces that which is too much, and augments that which is too little. He weighs things and makes them equal.

**Meaning**: Just as a mountain's height and the earth's depth offset each other into a level plain, the superior man creates just conditions by actively reducing excess and building up deficiency, rather than leaving imbalance to correct itself.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "A superior man modest about his modesty may cross the great water. Good fortune." — An unassuming attitude that makes no claims or demands of others makes even difficult undertakings easy, since nothing is put forward to provoke resistance.
- **Six in the second place**: "Modesty that comes to expression. Perseverance brings good fortune." — When genuine inner modesty naturally shows itself in outward behavior, it creates lasting, uncontested influence.
- **Nine in the third place**: "A superior man of modesty and merit carries things to conclusion. Good fortune." — Real achievement paired with modesty, rather than being dazzled by one's own fame, wins the support needed to see the work through to completion.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Nothing that would not further modesty in movement." — Genuine modesty in a position of real responsibility means active interest and diligence in one's work, not hiding behind rules to avoid responsibility while still drawing pay.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "No boasting of wealth before one's neighbor. It is favorable to attack with force. Nothing that would not further." — Modesty is not passive good nature; a person in a responsible position must sometimes take decisive, purely objective action without personal boastfulness or offense.
- **Six at the top**: "Modesty that comes to expression. It is favorable to set armies marching to chastise one's own city and one's country." — Genuine modesty must be proven through real, energetic action — beginning by disciplining one's own conduct and immediate circle rather than blaming others.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine in the third place — the hexagram's sole yang line — is its ruler, and unusually for a third line (which the commentary elsewhere often treats as unfavorable), this one is described as fully favorable: it carries the same judgment as the whole hexagram.
- The T'uan Chuan sets out the law of modesty in four parallel domains: heaven (the sun declines from its zenith, the moon wanes from fullness), earth (mountains erode into valleys, valleys fill into hills), fate (powerful families draw ruin upon themselves while modest ones prosper), and human sentiment (arrogance breeds dislike, modesty wins love) — concluding that "the ultimate cause is never the outside world... but rather man himself."
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams places Ch'ien directly after Ta Yu (Possession in Great Measure): "he who possesses something great must not make it too full" — modesty is the necessary check on great possession.
- The commentary notes that the upper trigram K'un and the lower nuclear trigram K'an together form Hexagram 7 (Shih, The Army) — a structural link invoked specifically to explain the top line's image of marshaling armies, here redirected inward as self-discipline rather than external conquest.
- Confucius's words on the third line are preserved directly: "When a man does not boast of his efforts and does not count his merits a virtue, he is a man of great parts... Noble of nature, reverent in his conduct, the modest man is full of merit, and therefore he is able to maintain his position."

## Worked Example
Take the six at the top: "Modesty that comes to expression. It is favorable to set armies marching to chastise one's own city and one's country." The commentary is explicit that the martial imagery here is not about conquering others — it points inward. A person whose modesty is genuine must actually demonstrate it through real, disciplined effort, and when conflict or blame arises, the temptation is always to point at someone else. True modesty instead marshals its "armies" against one's own ego and immediate circle first. The line shows that modesty proven only in words, without the courage to discipline oneself first, isn't yet the real thing.

## Key Takeaways
- Approach difficult undertakings without demands or preconditions — an unassuming approach removes the friction that provokes resistance.
- Let genuine modesty show naturally in behavior rather than performing it; real inner attitude, not display, is what earns lasting trust.
- When you achieve something significant, resist being dazzled by your own success — staying modest about real merit is what lets you actually finish the work.
- In a position of real responsibility, don't mistake modesty for passivity or rule-hiding — active diligence and ownership of your work is what modesty looks like there.
- Modesty doesn't rule out decisive, even forceful action when the situation calls for it — just keep such action objective and free of personal boastfulness.
- When conflict arises, discipline yourself and your own circle first before looking to blame or correct others — that is where genuine modesty proves itself.
