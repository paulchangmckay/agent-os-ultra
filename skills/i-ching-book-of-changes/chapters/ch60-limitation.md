# Hexagram 60: Chieh / Limitation

## Core Idea
Setting effective, moderate limits — on expenditure, on action, on oneself and others — because unlimited possibility dissolves a life into the boundless, while galling, excessive limitation eventually provokes rebellion.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'an (Water, the Abysmal)
- Lower: Tui (Lake, the Joyous)

## The Judgment
> LIMITATION. Success. Galling limitation must not be persevered in.

**Meaning**: Limits are troublesome but effective — like the fixed boundary between summer and winter — yet limitation must itself have limits; imposed too harshly on oneself or others, it becomes injurious and provokes resistance.

## The Image
> Water over lake: the image of LIMITATION. Thus the superior man Creates number and measure, And examines the nature of virtue and correct conduct.

**Meaning**: Just as a lake can hold only a finite amount of the world's inexhaustible water, a person gains significance not through unlimited possibility but by consciously defining discriminations and duties — the very backbone of morality.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Not going out of the door and the courtyard Is without blame." — When circumscribed by real limitation, know where to stop; discretion at the outset accumulates the energy for decisive action later.
- **Nine in the second place**: "Not going out of the gate and the courtyard Brings misfortune." — Once the moment for action has arrived and obstacles are cleared, continued hesitation is a mistake that squanders the opportunity.
- **Six in the third place**: "He who knows no limitation Will have cause to lament. No blame." — Losing all sense of necessary limits in pursuit of pleasure brings regret you must own as self-caused, not blame on others.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Contented limitation. Success." — A limitation that follows the natural course of things, rather than fighting it, costs little energy and leads readily to success.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "Sweet limitation brings good fortune. Going brings esteem." — A leader who applies limitation first to himself, asking little of others while achieving much with modest means, earns good fortune and emulation.
- **Six at the top**: "Galling limitation. Perseverance brings misfortune. Remorse disappears." — Severity persisted in too long provokes rebellion, yet in certain moments ruthlessness toward oneself is the only way to guard against real wrongdoing — the key is not sustaining it past its usefulness.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan grounds the hexagram's success in its symmetric structure — three yang and three yin lines, with strong lines centrally placed in the second and fifth positions — giving the ruler an "all-pervading influence" despite being flanked by yielding lines.
- Book III states the cosmic scale of the principle directly: "heaven and earth have their limitations, and the four seasons of the year arise" — limitation is what makes structured time, and structured life, possible at all.
- The hexagram is explicitly the structural inverse of Huan, Dispersion (59): both share the same nuclear-trigram relationships, but here water is held together by the lake, while there it is dispersed by wind.
- Book III connects the Image's "number and measure" to ancient China's calendar of twenty-four seasonal divisions (chieh ch'i), used to align agricultural activity with the rhythm of the seasons — limitation as the very mechanism of workable order.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fifth place: "Sweet limitation brings good fortune. Going brings esteem." Book III explains this line's good fortune comes from "remaining central in one's own place" — the ruler's strong, correct, central position makes even self-restraint feel natural rather than forced, and by that example makes limitation "sweet" for everyone else too. Applied practically: if you're in a position of leading others and need to impose limits, the only version that will actually hold is the one you apply to yourself first and most rigorously — demanding restraint from others while excusing yourself from it is precisely the "galling" limitation the top line warns will eventually collapse.

## Key Takeaways
- Know when to hold back and when to act — discretion before the moment is right builds capacity; hesitation once the moment has arrived squanders it.
- Own the consequences of ignoring necessary limits yourself, rather than blaming others for a situation of your own making.
- Prefer limitations that align with how things naturally work over ones that require constant force to maintain — natural limits sustain themselves.
- If you're setting limits for others, apply them to yourself first and most strictly; that is what makes limitation acceptable rather than resented.
- Recognize that severity has a shelf life — useful briefly to prevent real wrongdoing, but harmful and unsustainable if maintained indefinitely.
