# Hexagram 53: Chien / Development (Gradual Progress)

## Core Idea
Slow, step-by-step growth — like a tree taking root on a mountain, or the formalities of a proper marriage — where perseverance, not speed, is what makes progress durable.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Sun (Wood, Penetration)
- Lower: Kên (Mountain, Keeping Still)

## The Judgment
> DEVELOPMENT. The maiden Is given in marriage. Good fortune. Perseverance furthers.

**Meaning**: Real development, like the gradual formalities leading to a marriage, cannot be rushed — correct relationships and lasting influence both require allowing the proper course of events to unfold, sustained by perseverance.

## The Image
> On the mountain, a tree: The image of DEVELOPMENT. Thus the superior man abides in dignity and virtue, In order to improve the mores.

**Meaning**: Like a tree that grows visibly and gradually, shaping the landscape around it, a person improves their surroundings not through sudden influence but through steady, consistent cultivation of their own character.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "The wild goose gradually draws near the shore. The young son is in danger. There is talk. No blame." — A hesitant, unaided first step draws criticism, but caution here keeps you blameless and prevents rash danger.
- **Six in the second place**: "The wild goose gradually draws near the cliff. Eating and drinking in peace and concord. Good fortune." — Having found a secure footing, share your good fortune with others rather than keeping it to yourself.
- **Nine in the third place**: "The wild goose gradually draws near the plateau. The man goes forth and does not return. The woman carries a child but does not bring it forth. Misfortune. It furthers one to fight off robbers." — Overreaching beyond the natural pace of development brings misfortune; hold your position and defend against real threats, but don't force the pace.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "The wild goose gradually draws near the tree. Perhaps it will find a flat branch. No blame." — In an awkward, unsuitable situation, adaptability and yielding can still find you a safe, if temporary, footing.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "The wild goose gradually draws near the summit. For three years the woman has no child. In the end nothing can hinder her. Good fortune." — Isolation and misunderstanding at a high position are temporary; a bond grounded in real affinity is eventually restored, whatever the delay.
- **Nine at the top**: "The wild goose gradually draws near the cloud heights. Its feathers can be used for the sacred dance. Good fortune." — A completed life's work becomes an example that uplifts others, like a bird's feathers repurposed for sacred ceremony.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan ties the hexagram's good fortune to its two ruling lines (six in the second, nine in the fifth) each occupying their correct and central place — attaining the right position is what makes the "inexhaustible" progress possible.
- Inner calm (Kên) combined with outer adaptability (Sun) is named as the specific mechanism that keeps gradual development from petering out — stillness within, penetration without.
- Book III explicitly contrasts this hexagram with Chin, Progress (35), and Shêng, Pushing Upward (46): those depict fast or forceful growth (sunrise, a tree breaking through earth), while Chien depicts the slow growth of a tree already rooted on a mountainside.
- The whole line sequence is read as one continuous image of a wild goose's flight from shore to cliff to plateau to tree to summit to the clouds — each stage mapping a stage of a life or relationship's gradual maturation.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "The wild goose gradually draws near the plateau. The man goes forth and does not return... Misfortune. It furthers one to fight off robbers." Book III notes this line is strong, in a strong place, at the top of the lower trigram — a position prone to pushing forward without turning back, in violation of the goose's proper gradual path (a plateau is dry, unsuitable terrain for it). The lesson: when someone abandons the graduated pace that development requires and forces their way past a natural stopping point, they invite real harm to themselves and those depending on them. The corrective isn't retreat but restraint — defend your position against genuine incursion, without initiating the overreach yourself.

## Key Takeaways
- Let relationships and undertakings develop in their natural sequence; skipping stages to move faster invites danger, not progress.
- Early hesitation and criticism from others are not failures — they are the normal cost of a cautious, well-paced start.
- Once you've found secure footing, extend the benefit to others rather than hoarding it.
- When placed in an awkward or unsuitable situation, look for the adaptable, "flat branch" solution rather than forcing a fit.
- Influence that lasts comes from steady, visible personal cultivation over time, not from sudden or forceful persuasion.

## Connects To
- Kuei Mei (54), The Marrying Maiden — the next hexagram in the Sequence, explicitly linked: "through progress one is sure to reach the place where one belongs," and Book III notes the two hexagrams are related as different models of arranging marriage (formal, gradual courtship here versus a young girl's own initiative there).
- Hsien (31), Influence — Book III notes this hexagram is closely related to Influence, contrasting the quick mutual attraction of the youngest son and daughter there with the slower, mores-bound development of an elder daughter and youngest son here.
