# Hexagram 14: Ta Yu / Possession in Great Measure

## Core Idea
Fire in heaven shines far, and all things stand out manifest in the light — a single yielding line in the place of honor holds all the strong lines in accord with it, showing that great possession is secured not by force but by unselfish modesty.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Li (Fire, clarity)
- Lower: Ch'ien (Heaven, strength)

## The Judgment
> POSSESSION IN GREAT MEASURE. Supreme success.

**Meaning**: Strength within paired with clarity and culture without produces supreme success and wealth — the weak line's power to hold the strong lines rests entirely on its unselfish modesty, not on control.

## The Image
> Fire in heaven above: the image of POSSESSION IN GREAT MEASURE. Thus the superior man curbs evil and furthers good, and thereby obeys the benevolent will of heaven.

**Meaning**: As the sun brings both good and evil equally into the light, the superior man's task with great possession is to actively combat what is evil and promote what is good, fulfilling heaven's benevolent intent rather than merely holding wealth passively.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "No relationship with what is harmful; there is no blame in this. If one remains conscious of difficulty, one remains without blame." — Possession still in its early stages, untested, brings no blame, provided one stays aware of the real difficulties ahead rather than growing complacent.
- **Nine in the second place**: "A big wagon for loading. One may undertake something. No blame." — Great possession's real value lies in its mobility and usefulness — having capable people who can carry real responsibility allows one to actually undertake important work.
- **Nine in the third place**: "A prince offers it to the Son of Heaven. A petty man cannot do this." — A magnanimous person places his possessions at the service of the ruler or the people rather than treating them as exclusive private property; a petty man, unable to do this, is harmed by his own wealth.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "He makes a difference between himself and his neighbor. No blame." — In a position surrounded by rich and powerful neighbors, avoiding envy and rivalry by clearly distinguishing what is one's own from what belongs to others keeps one free of error.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "He whose truth is accessible, yet dignified, has good fortune." — Winning people through unaffected sincerity rather than coercion succeeds, but that sincerity must be balanced with dignity to prevent insolence from spreading.
- **Nine at the top**: "He is blessed by heaven. Good fortune. Nothing that does not further." — At the height of possession and power, remaining modest and honoring those of real wisdom outside worldly affairs places one under heaven's beneficent influence, and everything goes well.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The six in the fifth place is the ruler of the hexagram: empty, central, occupying the honored position, and capable of holding all five strong lines in accord — described as receiving "the honored place in the great middle," a stronger and more direct possession than the six in the second place of the preceding hexagram, Fellowship with Men.
- The T'uan Chuan reads the hexagram's success from its combined attributes: firm and strong within (Ch'ien), ordered and clear without (Li) — "his character is firm and strong, ordered and clear; it finds correspondence in heaven and moves with the time."
- The Sequence of the Hexagrams places Ta Yu directly after T'ung Jên (Fellowship with Men): "through fellowship with men things are sure to fall to one's lot" — genuine union with others naturally produces abundance.
- The Miscellaneous Notes explicitly identify this hexagram as the inverse of the preceding one, and note it is more favorable precisely because its ruling line occupies the actual place of authority (the fifth place, that of the prince), where Fellowship with Men's ruling line occupied only the second place, that of an official.
- A closing structural note observes that this hexagram is organized so favorably that its movement, once set going at the beginning, does not decay into standstill or reverse into its opposite by the end — it closes harmoniously, in deliberate contrast to hexagrams whose arcs turn back on themselves.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "A prince offers it to the Son of Heaven. A petty man cannot do this." The commentary explains that this line, strong and correctly placed, belongs to trigrams associated with readiness to sacrifice — it represents someone positioned like a prince who recognizes that what he holds should ultimately serve the larger whole, not himself alone. A small-minded person, by contrast, gives only from a desire for personal gain, which the commentary says harms him directly. The line demonstrates that great possession is only sustainable when its holder treats it as something held in trust rather than owned outright.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat early, untested possession or success as a stage that still requires caution, not a settled state to relax into.
- Value resources and people for what they let you actually undertake and accomplish, not for their quantity alone — capability and mobility matter more than accumulation.
- When you hold significant resources or standing, be willing to place them at the service of a larger cause rather than guarding them purely as private property.
- Surrounded by powerful peers, actively avoid envy and rivalry by keeping a clear, deliberate line between what is yours and what is theirs.
- Win people's loyalty through real, accessible sincerity, but pair it with enough dignity that familiarity doesn't curdle into presumption or disrespect.
- At the peak of success or power, stay modest and continue to honor wisdom and counsel from outside your own position — that posture is what keeps the good fortune from reversing.
