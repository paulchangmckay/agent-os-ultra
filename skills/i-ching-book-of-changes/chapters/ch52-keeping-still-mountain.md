# Hexagram 52: Kên / Keeping Still, Mountain

## Core Idea
True stillness of heart — not suppression, but resting within the bounds of one's own situation so that movement, when it comes, arrives at the right time.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Kên (Mountain, Keeping Still)
- Lower: Kên (Mountain, Keeping Still)

## The Judgment
> KEEPING STILL. Keeping his back still So that he no longer feels his body. He goes into his courtyard And does not see his people. No blame.

**Meaning**: Genuine quiet means stopping when it is time to stop and moving when it is time to move — resting so completely that self-concern falls away, which brings clarity rather than isolation.

## The Image
> Mountains standing close together: The image of KEEPING STILL. Thus the superior man Does not permit his thoughts To go beyond his situation.

**Meaning**: The heart cannot stop thinking, but the superior man restricts his thoughts to what is actually within his current situation — thinking beyond that only causes needless distress.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "Keeping his toes still. No blame. Continued perseverance furthers." — Halt before you've even begun to move; at this early stage restraint is easy and right, but it takes persistence to hold it.
- **Six in the second place**: "Keeping his calves still. He cannot rescue him whom he follows. His heart is not glad." — A subordinate who halts cannot stop a superior already in powerful motion — good intentions alone cannot save someone swept up ahead of you.
- **Nine in the third place**: "Keeping his hips still. Making his sacrum stiff. Dangerous. The heart suffocates." — Forcing stillness through rigid suppression is dangerous; calm must arise naturally, not be imposed by force.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Keeping his trunk still. No blame." — Restraining one's impulses at the right moment is a genuine achievement, even if full egolessness hasn't yet been reached.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Keeping his jaws still. The words have order. Remorse disappears." — Reserved, disciplined speech prevents the regret that comes from loose talk or presumptuous jokes.
- **Nine at the top**: "Noblehearted keeping still. Good fortune." — The consummation of stillness: a general resignation toward life as a whole, bringing good fortune in every particular matter.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The T'uan Chuan frames stopping and advancing as two faces of the same rightness: "when it is time to stop, then stop; when it is time to advance, then advance" — so their course becomes bright and clear.
- The image of the back is explained as the part of the body invisible to oneself — stilling it means stilling self-awareness itself, so one moves through the world (the "courtyard") without the friction of ego.
- Structurally, the hexagram is unique among doubled trigrams in that its upper and lower halves have no relationship of correspondence at all between corresponding lines — they "turn their backs on each other," which is precisely why the Image says one does not see one's own people.
- Line by line, danger tracks position: the third line's forced, rigid stillness (a lone light line boxed between dark ones) risks suffocating the heart, while the top line's stillness is ample and unforced, hence purely good.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "Keeping his hips still. Making his sacrum stiff. Dangerous. The heart suffocates." Book III locates this line in the middle of the nuclear trigram K'an (associated with the heart) and notes it is the one light line trapped between dark lines — a position of confinement. The lesson is that stillness forced at the body's central hinge (the hips, the boundary between upper and lower movement) doesn't produce calm, it produces suffocation. In practice: if you try to manufacture inner peace through willed, rigid suppression rather than letting it arise from actual composure, the result is worse than the restlessness you were trying to cure — the fix is to loosen the grip, not tighten it.

## Key Takeaways
- Stop early, before momentum builds — restraint is far easier at the very first impulse than once movement is underway.
- Recognize when you cannot rescue someone already swept into motion ahead of you; your own stillness cannot substitute for theirs.
- Never force calm through rigid suppression — genuine composure grows naturally or not at all, and forcing it backfires.
- Guard your speech especially when situations feel precarious; disciplined words prevent the regret loose talk creates.
- Aim for a stillness broad enough to cover your whole outlook on life, not just isolated moments — that is what makes it durable good fortune.
