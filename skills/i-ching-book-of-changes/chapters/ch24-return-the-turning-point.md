# Hexagram 24: Fu / Return (The Turning Point)

## Core Idea
Return marks the turning point where, after a time of complete decay, a single light line re-enters from below — the winter solstice's promise that light comes back not through force but through the natural, cyclic course of nature itself.

## Trigrams
- Upper: K'un, earth (devotion)
- Lower: Chên, thunder (movement)

## The Judgment
> RETURN. Success. Going out and coming in without error. Friends come without blame. To and fro goes the way. On the seventh day comes return. It furthers one to have somewhere to go.

**Meaning**: After decay, the powerful light naturally returns without being forced; because this transformation arises spontaneously and in harmony with the time, no harm results from discarding the old and welcoming the new.

## The Image
> Thunder within the earth: the image of THE TURNING POINT. Thus the kings of antiquity closed the passes at the time of solstice. Merchants and strangers did not go about, and the ruler did not travel through the provinces.

**Meaning**: When renewing energy is still weak and just beginning, it must be reinforced by rest rather than dissipated through premature use — a principle that applies equally to recovering health or restoring an estranged understanding.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Return from a short distance. No need for remorse. Great good fortune." — Turning back from a slight digression before it goes too far, especially in character, leaves no cause for remorse.
- **Six in the second place**: "Quiet return. Good fortune." — Return is an act of self-mastery made easier by good company; setting aside pride to follow good men brings good fortune.
- **Six in the third place**: "Repeated return. Danger. No blame." — Someone unstable may repeatedly desert and return to the good; though risky, this doesn't become permanent habituation to evil, so a general capacity for improvement remains open.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Walking in the midst of others, one returns alone." — Surrounded by inferior company but spiritually tied to a strong, good friend, one turns back alone; this resolve to choose the good is its own reward.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Noblehearted return. No remorse." — At the time for return, one should look within, examine oneself honestly, and make a noblehearted confession of fault rather than hiding behind trivial excuses.
- **Six at the top**: "Missing the return. Misfortune. Misfortune from within and without. If armies are set marching in this way, one will in the end suffer a great defeat, disastrous for the ruler of the country. For ten years it will not be possible to attack again." — Missing the right time for return, rooted in a wrong attitude, brings misfortune from both within and without — the picture of blind obstinacy and the judgment it invites.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The nine at the beginning is the hexagram's ruler, referenced in the Commentary's "the firm returns" — Confucius himself is quoted praising this line through the figure of Yen Hui, who "never fails to recognize" a fault and "never commits the error a second time."
- The Commentary frames Return as revealing "the mind of heaven and earth": the light force is an eternal cyclic movement that comes forth again just when it appears completely vanquished — the yang line's earlier departure (in the previous hexagram, Po/Splitting Apart) left an effect behind, like a fruit that falls but leaves its seed.
- As the inverse of Splitting Apart, Return shows movement (Chên) acting through devotion (K'un), which is why "going out and coming in" happen "without error" — the seventh-day return is explained numerically: the light force diminishes in hexagram 44 (Kou, Coming to Meet) and returns after seven changes.
- The Note contrasts the six lines in pairs: "missing the return" (top) is the opposite of "return from a short distance" (beginning); "quiet return" (second) and "solitary return" (fourth) resemble each other through their tie to the ruling first line; "repeated return" (third) and "noblehearted return" (fifth) are opposites — one shows back-and-forth instability, the other calm consistency.

## Worked Example
Take the six in the third place: "Repeated return. Danger. No blame." The commentary clarifies that this line sits "at the peak of movement," meaning it turns back and forth repeatedly — first from good to bad, then from bad to good again. Despite the real danger this instability carries, the commentary insists the line's essential meaning is "deliverance from blame," because the return, however repeated, still counts as movement toward the good rather than settled habituation to evil. This deepens the Judgment's core promise: return is available even to someone who struggles and backslides, as long as the turning back keeps happening.

## Key Takeaways
- Catch a mistake or bad thought early and correct it immediately — the shorter the digression, the more completely it resolves.
- Let good company and humility, not stubborn pride, ease your return to the right path.
- Don't judge yourself too harshly for repeated backsliding — what matters is that the turning back keeps recurring, not that the path was perfectly straight.
- When you must return alone, spiritual connection to one genuinely good influence is enough, even surrounded by inferior company.
- Recognize the danger of stubbornly refusing to turn back once it's clearly time — obstinate persistence in a wrong direction invites lasting, compounding misfortune.
