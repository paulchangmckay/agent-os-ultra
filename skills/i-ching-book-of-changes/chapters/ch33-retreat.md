# Hexagram 33: Tun / Retreat

## Core Idea
The power of the dark (the inferior, the small) is ascending from below; the superior man's correct response is not to fight but to withdraw in good order, preserving his strength for a better time.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Ch'ien (Heaven, strong movement)
- Lower: Kên (Mountain, Keeping Still)

## The Judgment
> RETREAT. Success. In what is small, perseverance furthers.

**Meaning**: When hostile forces favored by the time are advancing, retreat carried out at the right moment, in the right manner, is itself the path to success — it is strength recognizing timing, not weakness in flight.

## The Image
> Mountain under heaven: the image of RETREAT. Thus the superior man keeps the inferior man at a distance, not angrily but with reserve.

**Meaning**: Heaven retreats upward before the mountain that rises toward it, remaining forever out of reach — so the superior man distances himself from an encroaching inferior through dignified reserve, never through hatred or open conflict.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "At the tail in retreat. This is dangerous. One must not wish to undertake anything." — Positioned at the rear of a retreat, in contact with the pursuer; keeping still is the only safe course.
- **Six in the second place**: "He holds him fast with yellow oxhide. No one can tear him loose." — An inferior man clings so firmly and rightly to the retreating superior men that he cannot be shaken off; firmness of purpose reaches its goal.
- **Nine in the third place**: "A halted retreat is nerve-wracking and dangerous. To retain people as men- and maidservants brings good fortune." — Held back against one's will is dangerous; the only expedient is to keep those who cling to you in a subordinate service, though nothing great can be accomplished this way.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "Voluntary retreat brings good fortune to the superior man and downfall to the inferior man." — Departing willingly and without inner conflict benefits the one who leaves; it is the inferior man left behind, deprived of guidance, who suffers.
- **Nine in the fifth place**: "Friendly retreat. Perseverance brings good fortune." — Recognizing the right moment allows retreat to proceed within the forms of friendliness, though firmness of decision must still not waver.
- **Nine at the top**: "Cheerful retreat. Everything serves to further." — When the path ahead is entirely clear of doubt, departure becomes a cheerful, unhesitating act that leads only to good.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The ruler of the hexagram is the strong nine in the fifth place, corresponding with the weak six in the second — this pairing shows the retreat is a voluntary, strong withdrawal, not a forced flight of the weak.
- The lower trigram Kên (Keeping Still) hampers the retreat of the three lower lines, while the upper trigram Ch'ien (strong movement) lets the three upper lines retreat freely and unhindered — structure itself dictates who can leave easily and who cannot.
- The commentary stresses that "the meaning of the time of RETREAT is great" — hitting the right moment for withdrawal is treated as a matter of the highest importance, not a minor tactical choice.
- An alternate reading of the fifth line suggests the "friendly retreat" may be only inward: outwardly remaining at one's post while inwardly preparing a countermove.

## Worked Example
Consider the nine in the fourth place: "Voluntary retreat brings good fortune to the superior man and downfall to the inferior man." A person recognizing that the tide has turned against their position — a project losing sponsorship, a role losing influence — faces a choice between clinging on or stepping back cleanly. This line shows that a retreat made willingly, before being forced, costs the departing party nothing and does not even require ill will toward whoever remains; the damage falls only on those who stay attached to a declining position without the judgment to leave. This adds to the Judgment's picture by showing that the "success" of retreat is not uniform — its rewards depend on which line, which position, one occupies when the movement begins.

## Key Takeaways
- Withdraw while you still hold full power and position — waiting until you are desperate turns retreat into flight.
- Distinguish retreat from flight: retreat is a strategic use of strength, flight is panic; only the former succeeds.
- When you cannot cleanly disengage from people who cling to you, manage them in a limited, subordinate way rather than pretending you can accomplish great things with their help.
- Keep opposition to an inferior force reserved and dignified, never openly hostile — anger binds you to the very thing you are trying to leave behind.
- If you must remain in place for now, treat your withdrawal as inward: keep your convictions intact while outwardly biding your time for a countermove.
