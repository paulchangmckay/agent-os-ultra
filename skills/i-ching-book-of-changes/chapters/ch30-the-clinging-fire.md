# Hexagram 30: Li / The Clinging, Fire

## Core Idea
A dark line clinging to a light line above and below — like fire, which has no form of its own and must cling to a burning object to give off light — pictures the truth that all brightness, whether physical or spiritual, depends on something to which it clings and rests in order to keep shining.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Li, fire
- Lower: Li, fire

## The Judgment
> THE CLINGING. Perseverance furthers. It brings success. Care of the cow brings good fortune.

**Meaning**: Everything that gives light depends on something to cling to in order to keep shining, so recognizing life's inherent dependence and cultivating docile compliance toward the harmonious forces of the cosmos, symbolized by the cow, brings clarity without sharpness and true success.

## The Image
> That which is bright rises twice: the image of FIRE. Thus the great man, by perpetuating this brightness, illumines the four quarters of the world.

**Meaning**: The doubled trigram represents the sun's repeated daily rising; the great man continues nature's work by letting his own clarity spread ever further and penetrate human nature ever more deeply.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "The footprints run crisscross. If one is seriously intent, no blame." — At the restless start of activity, preserving inner composure and serious concentration is what secures clarity amid the flood of incoming impressions.
- **Six in the second place**: "Yellow light. Supreme good fortune." — Yellow, the color of the mean, symbolizes the highest culture and art, whose consummate harmony consists in holding to the middle way.
- **Nine in the third place**: "In the light of the setting sun, men either beat the pot and sing or loudly bewail the approach of old age. Misfortune." — Both uninhibited revelry and melancholy lament are wrong responses to life's transience; the superior man instead cultivates himself and awaits his allotted time regardless of when death comes.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "Its coming is sudden; it flames up, dies down, is thrown away." — Like a meteor or straw fire, an excitable, restless rise to prominence that spends itself too rapidly produces no lasting effect and ends badly.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Tears in floods, sighing and lamenting. Good fortune." — At life's zenith, understanding the vanity of all things and setting aside both hope and fear brings good fortune through genuine change of heart, not a passing mood.
- **Nine at the top**: "The king uses him to march forth and chastise. Then it is best to kill the leaders and take captive the followers. No blame." — Chastisement should cure evil at its root without excess: eradicate the ringleaders, spare the followers — asceticism or punishment carried too far defeats its own purpose.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The hexagram's rulers are the two yin lines in the second and fifth places, with the second being ruler in a more marked degree, "for fire is brightest when it first flames up."
- The Commentary generalizes the clinging principle across all of nature: sun and moon cling to heaven for their brightness, plants cling to the soil for their life, and human psychic nature must likewise cling to the forces of spiritual life in order to become "transfigured and attain influence on earth."
- The Miscellaneous Notes and Appended Judgments frame this hexagram as the deliberate opposite of the preceding one (K'an, The Abysmal) in both structure and entire meaning, and trace hunting nets and baskets (Fu Hsi's knotted cords) to its image of something "divided within and closed without."
- On the top line, the commentary clarifies that the ruler (six in the fifth place, the king) uses the strong top line to lead armed forces, and because that line is correctly placed and strong, it does not push the business of war too far — showing "the light at its height" restrained by correctness rather than excess.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the fourth place: "Its coming is sudden; it flames up, dies down, is thrown away." The commentary notes this line is restless, sitting at the intersection of two nuclear trigrams, "oppressed from below and rejected from above" — it has, in itself, "nothing that would cause it to be accepted." This deepens the Judgment's core teaching about clinging and dependence: a rise to prominence that lacks a genuine, sustaining foundation to cling to burns brightly but briefly, like a meteor, and produces no lasting good — brightness without something reliable to rest on consumes itself.

## Key Takeaways
- Recognize that clarity and brightness of mind are never self-sustaining — they depend on something worthy to cling to and rest on.
- Bring serious composure to the start of any undertaking; the beginning holds the seed of everything that follows.
- Hold to the middle way rather than extremes — neither uninhibited pursuit of pleasure nor melancholy over what's passing serves you well.
- Watch for rises to prominence that are all flash and no foundation — they burn out and leave nothing lasting.
- When correcting wrongdoing, root out the true source and stop there; punishment or discipline pushed past what's needed defeats its own purpose.
