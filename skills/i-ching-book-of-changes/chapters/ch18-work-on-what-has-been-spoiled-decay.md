# Hexagram 18: Ku / Work on What Has Been Spoiled [Decay]

## Core Idea
Decay names a state of corruption caused by human neglect or abuse of freedom, not blind fate — and because it was caused by man, it can be undone by man's deliberate, energetic work.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Kên, mountain (heavy, standing still)
- Lower: Sun, wind/wood (gentle, listless)

## The Judgment
> WORK ON WHAT HAS BEEN SPOILED has supreme success. It furthers one to cross the great water. Before the starting point, three days. After the starting point, three days.

**Meaning**: Corruption caused by inertia and indifference can be repaired through decisive work, but success requires careful deliberation both before beginning reform and after, so the new way takes hold and no relapse follows.

## The Image
> The wind blows low on the mountain: the image of DECAY. Thus the superior man stirs up the people and strengthens their spirit.

**Meaning**: Just as wind thrown back off a mountain spoils vegetation, debasing fashions corrupt society; the superior man regenerates it in sequence — first stirring public opinion, then giving it stability and nourishment.

## The Lines
- **Six at the beginning**: "Setting right what has been spoiled by the father. If there is a son, no blame rests upon the departed father. Danger. In the end good fortune." — Early decay is still easily remedied, like a son quietly correcting his father's oversight, provided the danger of reform is taken seriously.
- **Nine in the second place**: "Setting right what has been spoiled by the mother. One must not be too persevering." — Correcting faults rooted in weakness calls for gentle consideration, not drastic proceeding that would wound.
- **Nine in the third place**: "Setting right what has been spoiled by the father. There will be a little remorse. No great blame." — Proceeding a bit too energetically in reform may cause minor friction, but excess energy is safer than too little, so no serious blame results.
- **Six in the fourth place**: "Tolerating what has been spoiled by the father. In continuing one sees humiliation." — Being too weak to act on decay and simply letting it run its course leads to humiliation if continued.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Setting right what has been spoiled by the father. One meets with praise." — Lacking the power to reform corruption alone, one still earns praise by achieving a thorough reform with the help of able colleagues.
- **Nine at the top**: "He does not serve kings and princes, sets himself higher goals." — Some who have developed beyond ordinary concerns may rightly withdraw from public affairs, not out of idleness but to embody higher aims for the future.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The six in the fifth place is the hexagram's ruler: every other line carries a warning, but only the fifth line's outcome is simple praise, because it alone completes the work of reform.
- The Commentary reads the trigram structure as inverted from hexagram 17 (Following) — the strong, upward-striving force sits above a weak, sinking force below, so the movements diverge and relationships are lacking, producing spoilage; yet the nuclear trigrams (Chên emerging from Tui) show an inner trend toward energetic, joyous improvement.
- "Before the starting point, three days; after the starting point, three days" is explained through the cyclic calendar sign Chia: winter (ending) precedes spring (new beginning) just as the commentary states "a new beginning follows every ending, is the course of heaven" — tying the injunction to careful timing to the natural cycle itself.
- A second gloss ties the hexagram directly to Ch'ien (father) and K'un (mother) via the Inner-World trigram arrangement, reinforcing why every line speaks of "spoiled by the father" or "by the mother."

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "Setting right what has been spoiled by the father. There will be a little remorse. No great blame." This line shows someone correcting an inherited problem with real vigor — enough that minor friction and regret are the natural cost. The Judgment already tells us success in reforming decay requires energetic action rather than passive drift; this line confirms that even excessive energy, applied with good intention, is preferable to under-correction, and stays free of serious blame. It's a useful counterweight to the fourth line's caution about tolerating decay too long.

## Key Takeaways
- Treat decay as a call to work, not a verdict on fate — corruption caused by neglect can be undone by deliberate effort.
- Plan both before and after a reform: understand root causes first, then guard against relapse once changed.
- Match your correction's intensity to the fault's origin — gentleness for weakness-born problems, energy for entrenched neglect.
- Don't let excessive caution become passive tolerance of a problem; continued drift invites humiliation.
- Recognize when withdrawing from a fight is legitimate — only if it serves higher, longer-term aims rather than convenient idleness.
