# Hexagram 34: Ta Chuang / The Power of the Great

## Core Idea
Great inner strength has risen to power and is moving forward, but true greatness is only safe and sustainable when it stays bound to what is right — power without justice degenerates into mere force.

## Trigrams
- Upper: Chên (Thunder, the Arousing, movement)
- Lower: Ch'ien (Heaven, the Creative, strength)

## The Judgment
> THE POWER OF THE GREAT. Perseverance furthers.

**Meaning**: Inner worth has mounted with great force and passed beyond the midpoint, creating real danger of relying on power alone and forgetting what is right — true greatness stays indissolubly joined to justice, never becoming mere force.

## The Image
> Thunder in heaven above: the image of THE POWER OF THE GREAT. Thus the superior man does not tread upon paths that do not accord with established order.

**Meaning**: Thunder's upward movement is in harmony with heaven's own, showing that real power moves in accord with what is established as right — so the superior man in times of great strength avoids any action that departs from that order.

## The Lines
- **Nine at the beginning**: "Power in the toes. Continuing brings misfortune. This is certainly true." — Great power in a lowly position is tempted to force advance; pressed further, this leads to misfortune.
- **Nine in the second place**: "Perseverance brings good fortune." — As the gates to success open, exuberant self-confidence is the danger; inner equilibrium rather than excessive force brings good fortune.
- **Nine in the third place**: "The inferior man works through power. The superior man does not act thus. To continue is dangerous. A goat butts against a hedge and gets its horns entangled." — Boasting of power entangles one just as a goat's horns catch in a hedge; the superior man renounces empty displays of force in good time.
- **Nine in the fourth place**: "Perseverance brings good fortune. Remorse disappears. The hedge opens; there is no entanglement. Power depends upon the axle of a big cart." — Quiet, persevering removal of resistance succeeds in the end; real power, like a cart's axle, moves great loads without showing itself outwardly.
- **Six in the fifth place**: "Loses the goat with ease. No remorse." — Everything has become easy; a formerly stubborn, belligerent stance can be given up without regret.
- **Six at the top**: "A goat butts against a hedge. It cannot go backward, it cannot go forward. Nothing serves to further. If one notes the difficulty, this brings good fortune." — Overextension leads to deadlock, unable to advance or retreat; recognizing this and choosing not to continue lets things right themselves.

## Confucian Commentary Highlights
- The ruler of the hexagram is the nine in the fourth place, the topmost of the four advancing strong lines, and the hexagram is explicitly framed as the structural inverse of Hexagram 33 (Retreat) — the same four-line configuration read in reverse.
- The Commentary on the Decision insists greatness and rightness are not two separate things: "what is great must be right" — without rightness there is no greatness at all, and this is offered as the pattern seen in the relations of heaven and earth themselves.
- The text contrasts this hexagram with T'ai (Peace, 11), where light lines are advancing but not yet a majority, and Kuai (Break-through, 43), where light lines dominate but downfall is already near — only Ta Chuang's four-line balance is said to denote true power.
- The goat imagery recurs across the lines because the hexagram's nuclear trigram is read as Tui (associated with the sheep/goat), giving the butting-and-entangled-horns image a structural rather than arbitrary origin.

## Worked Example
Take the nine in the third place: "The inferior man works through power. The superior man does not act thus... A goat butts against a hedge and gets its horns entangled." Someone newly given real authority — a promotion, a resourced initiative — often feels the pull to prove that authority by forcing outcomes. This line warns that visible displays of power create their own obstruction, the same way a goat's aggressive charge traps it by the horns. It sharpens the Judgment's core caution: strength that has "passed beyond the median line" is exactly the strength most tempted to overreach, and the line shows concretely what overreach looks like and what it costs.

## Key Takeaways
- Use restraint, not display, when you hold real power — the show of force is what creates entanglement, not the strength itself.
- When resistance is giving way easily, resist the pull of overconfidence; persevere in equilibrium rather than pushing harder than needed.
- If you feel yourself deadlocked, unable to advance or retreat, stop and acknowledge the difficulty rather than forcing a way through — recognition itself starts the correction.
- Let your power operate quietly, like a cart's axle: the less it is flaunted outwardly, the more it can actually move.
- Judge your position on the arc between rising and excess — power still building (as in early lines) needs patience; power already peaked (as in the top line) needs the humility to stand down.
