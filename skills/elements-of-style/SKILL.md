## Core Rules & Mental Models

**Essentials-first teaching** (Ch 1): four rules (three comma, one semicolon) cover the punctuation needed by 19 of every 20 sentences. Master a small set of rules until they're automatic, rather than memorizing an exhaustive rulebook.

**Elementary Rules of Usage** (Ch 2, Rules 1–7):
- Form the possessive singular with 's (Charles's), except pronominal possessives (its, theirs) and a few archaic exceptions.
- Serial comma: comma after every item in a series of 3+, except the last.
- Enclose parenthetic expressions in commas on both sides — never one side only. Test: remove the material; if what's left is a complete statement, it's parenthetic.
- Comma before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses; if the second clause opens with an adverb instead, use a semicolon.
- Never join independent clauses with a bare comma (comma splice) — use a semicolon, period, or conjunction.
- Don't break a sentence in two with a period unless the fragment is a deliberate, emphatic effect.
- An opening participial phrase must modify the sentence's actual grammatical subject, or it dangles.

**Elementary Principles of Composition** (Ch 3, Rules 8–18) — the book's core:
- One paragraph, one topic; open with a topic sentence, close by reinforcing it.
- Use the active voice by default — ask what the sentence is really about, and make that the subject. Passive is correct only when the paragraph's true focus is the thing acted upon.
- State things positively; use "not" only for denial or antithesis, never to dodge a claim.
- Prefer specific, concrete language — it costs the reader less effort to picture than abstraction.
- Omit needless words: cut anything that doesn't earn its place, in sentences and paragraphs alike.
- Avoid a run of same-shaped loose sentences (X, and Y. X, but Y.) — vary the pattern.
- Express co-ordinate ideas in matching grammatical form (parallel construction).
- Keep related words together — subject near its verb, relative pronoun right after its antecedent, modifiers next to what they modify.
- Keep one tense throughout a summary.
- Put the word you want remembered at the end of the sentence — the position of emphasis.

**Words Commonly Misused** (Ch 5) — the recast-don't-patch rule: when you catch a misused word, don't swap in a synonym; rewrite the sentence from its actual subject. Watch especially for filler nouns (case, character, factor, feature, nature, system) that pad a sentence without adding meaning.

**Spelling** (Ch 6) — an unfamiliar or over-simplified spelling costs the reader attention even when "more efficient" to write; stick to currently accepted forms.
