# Patterns — The Elements of Style

## Recast, Don't Patch
**When to use**: you catch a misused word (case, character, factor, "the fact that," due to, etc.) or a comma splice, dangling modifier, or mismatched parallel construction.
**How**: don't swap in a synonym or bolt on a fix — rewrite the whole sentence from its actual subject. A wrong word or broken structure is usually a symptom of an unclear underlying thought; patching the surface leaves the thought unclear.
**Trade-offs**: costs more time per fix than a word-swap, but produces a genuinely clearer sentence instead of a grammatically-legal but still-muddy one.

## Compression by Combination
**When to use**: a single idea is spread across several short, choppy sentences that each restate connective tissue ("this led him," "this wish of his").
**How**: identify the one real relationship between the sentences (cause, consequence, sequence) and combine them into one sentence that expresses it directly, usually with a participial opener or subordinate clause.
**Trade-offs**: the Macbeth example compresses 51 words to 26 this way — roughly half. Overuse risks producing sentences so dense the reader loses the thread; alternate with simpler sentences per Rule 14.

## The Active-Voice Default
**When to use**: drafting or revising any declarative sentence.
**How**: ask what the sentence is actually about — its true subject — and make that the grammatical subject with an active verb. Switch to passive only when the paragraph's real focus is the thing being acted upon, not the actor.
**Trade-offs**: passive is sometimes genuinely correct (see Ch 3 Rule 10's "dramatists of the Restoration" example) — the pattern is a default, not an absolute ban.

## Topic-Sentence Framing
**When to use**: opening any paragraph in expository or argumentative writing.
**How**: state the paragraph's main point in the first one or two sentences; develop it in the body; close by reinforcing that same point or stating its most important consequence — never by drifting into an unrelated detail.
**Trade-offs**: narrative/descriptive paragraphs (novels, stories) often skip an explicit topic sentence entirely — the pattern is strongest for exposition and argument, not narration.

## The End-Position Emphasis
**When to use**: you want a specific word or phrase to land with force.
**How**: restructure the sentence so that word/phrase is the last thing the reader encounters — the "logical predicate" position. Secondary emphasis goes to the sentence's opening position.
**Trade-offs**: overusing periodic (end-loaded) sentences across an entire piece creates its own monotony — Strunk pairs this with Rule 14's call for sentence-pattern variety.

## Removal Test for Parenthetic Material
**When to use**: deciding whether a clause or phrase needs commas around it.
**How**: mentally delete the material. If what remains reads as a complete, independent statement, the material is parenthetic (or non-restrictive) — enclose it in commas on both sides. If removing it changes or breaks the sentence's core meaning, it's restrictive — no commas.
**Trade-offs**: none — this is presented as a reliable, mechanical test, not a judgment call.
