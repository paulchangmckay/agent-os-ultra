# Chapter III: Elementary Principles of Composition

## Core Idea
This is the heart of the book: eleven principles (Rules 8–18) governing how paragraphs and sentences should be built so a reader can follow them with the least effort. The recurring theme is that every structural choice — paragraph breaks, active voice, word order, parallel form — exists to aid the reader, not to decorate the prose.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rule 8 — Make the paragraph the unit of composition**: one paragraph per topic; a paragraph break signals "a new step in the development of the subject."
  - How: for longer work, outline your topics first (Strunk shows a 7-part outline for a poem review: composition facts, form, subject, treatment, distinction, characteristic traits, relation to other works), then give each topic its own paragraph.
- **Rule 9 — Begin each paragraph with a topic sentence; end in conformity with the beginning**: topic sentence near the top, body develops it, final sentence emphasizes the topic sentence's thought or states an important consequence.
  - Why it works: it lets the reader grasp the paragraph's purpose immediately and hold onto it through to the end. Ending on a digression is "particularly to be avoided."
- **Rule 10 — Use the active voice**: "I shall always remember my first visit to Boston" beats "My first visit to Boston will always be remembered by me" — more direct, bolder, more concise.
  - When to use passive anyway: when the sentence's real subject is the thing being acted on, not the actor — "The dramatists of the Restoration are little esteemed today" is correct if the paragraph is about the dramatists.
  - Failure mode: stacking one passive on another ("He has been proved to have been seen entering the building") buries the real subject; and using an abstract noun as the subject of a passive verb ("A survey of this region was made in 1900") drains the sentence of force compared to "This region was surveyed in 1900."
- **Rule 11 — Put statements in positive form**: make definite assertions; use "not" only for denial or antithesis, never to dodge a claim ("He was not very often on time" → "He usually came late").
- **Rule 12 — Use definite, specific, concrete language**: prefer the specific to the general — "It rained every day for a week" over "A period of unfavorable weather set in."
  - Why it works: abstract words force the reader to translate them into a mental image before the sentence lands; specific words supply the image directly, at less cost to the reader (Strunk cites Herbert Spencer's Philosophy of Style on this point).
- **Rule 13 — Omit needless words**: "A sentence should contain no unnecessary words, a paragraph no unnecessary sentences, for the same reason that a drawing should have no unnecessary lines." This does not mean short sentences only — it means every word earns its place.
  - Targets by name: "the fact that," "the question as to whether," "he is a man who," "who is / which was" when the material can be compressed.
- **Rule 14 — Avoid a succession of loose sentences**: a single loose sentence (two co-ordinate clauses joined by and/but/so) is fine; a paragraph full of them becomes "monotonous and tedious."
  - How: recast some as simple sentences, semicolon-joined pairs, periodic sentences, or three-clause sentences — vary the pattern to match the actual relations of the thought.
- **Rule 15 — Express co-ordinate ideas in similar form**: parallel construction — "Formerly, science was taught by the textbook method; now it is taught by the laboratory method," not a mismatched "while" construction.
  - How: an article or preposition governing a whole series must appear either only before the first term or before every term; correlatives (both...and, either...or, not only...but also) must be followed by matching grammatical constructions.
- **Rule 16 — Keep related words together**: subject and verb shouldn't be separated by a movable phrase; relative pronouns should sit right after their antecedent; modifiers belong next to what they modify ("He only found two mistakes" → "He found only two mistakes").
- **Rule 17 — In summaries, keep to one tense**: dramas are summarized in present tense; novels/poems preferably present (past is acceptable); antecedent action goes in the perfect (if present-tense summary) or past perfect (if past-tense summary) — and stick with the chosen tense throughout.
- **Rule 18 — Place the emphatic words of a sentence at the end**: the end (and secondarily the beginning) is the position of prominence; put the new information — the logical predicate — there, not buried mid-sentence.
  - How: a periodic sentence (one that withholds its main point until the end) uses this deliberately for effect.

## Key Concepts
- **Topic sentence**: the sentence, usually near the paragraph's start, that states its main point.
- **Loose sentence**: a sentence that reaches grammatical completeness before its end, typically two clauses joined by and/but — fine occasionally, tedious in a row.
- **Periodic sentence**: a sentence that withholds its main clause or point until the end, creating suspense and giving that final position emphasis.
- **Parallel construction**: expressing similar ideas in matching grammatical form so their similarity is visible on the page.
- **Logical predicate**: the "new" information in a sentence — the part that should usually land at the end for emphasis.

## Mental Models
- Use "does this word tell?" as the test for every word in a sentence — if removing it loses nothing, cut it (Rule 13).
- Read a paragraph and ask "what does the topic sentence promise, and does the ending deliver on or extend that promise?" — if the ending wanders, cut or move it (Rule 9).
- When choosing active vs. passive, ask "what is this sentence actually about?" — the true subject of the paragraph, not just grammatical convenience, decides the voice.

## Worked Example
Rule 13 shows the same content compressed from 51 words to 26: "Macbeth was very ambitious. This led him to wish to become king of Scotland. The witches told him that this wish of his would come true. The king of Scotland at this time was Duncan. Encouraged by his wife, Macbeth murdered Duncan. He was thus enabled to succeed Duncan as king." becomes "Encouraged by his wife, Macbeth achieved his ambition and realized the prediction of the witches by murdering Duncan and becoming king of Scotland in his place." Same information, half the words, because the sentence-by-sentence version restates connective tissue ("this led him," "this wish of his") that a single compound sentence handles implicitly.

## Anti-patterns
- **Passive with an abstract-noun subject**: "Confirmation of these reports cannot be obtained" instead of "These reports cannot be confirmed" — the noun does no work the verb couldn't do more directly.
- **Negative evasion**: using "not" to avoid committing to a real claim, leaving the reader to guess what you actually mean.
- **Redundant relative clauses**: "His brother, who is a member of the same firm" when "His brother, a member of the same firm" says the same thing in fewer words.
- **Mismatched parallel series**: "It was both a long ceremony and very tedious" instead of "The ceremony was both long and tedious" — breaks the grammatical symmetry the reader expects from "both...and."
- **Burying the emphatic word mid-sentence**: placing the sentence's real point in the middle robs it of the prominence the end position would give it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Every paragraph gets one topic, a topic sentence near the top, and an ending that reinforces or extends that topic — never a digression.
2. Default to active voice; use passive deliberately, only when the true subject of the paragraph is the thing being acted upon.
3. State things positively; let "not" do denial or antithesis, never evasion.
4. Prefer specific, concrete words — they cost the reader less effort to picture than abstractions.
5. Cut every word that doesn't earn its place; a paragraph is compressed the same way a sentence is.
6. Vary sentence pattern across a paragraph — a run of same-shaped loose sentences reads as monotonous regardless of content.
7. Put the word you want remembered at the end of the sentence.
