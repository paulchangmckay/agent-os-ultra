# Chapter VI: Spelling

## Core Idea
English spelling isn't fixed by any authority — it's fixed by general agreement, and that agreement drifts slowly. The chapter's real point isn't the word list; it's that unaccepted or over-simplified spellings cost the reader attention, because a reader who hits an unfamiliar form has to consciously reconstruct the intended word.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Reader-cost test for unconventional spelling**: an accepted spelling is read "automatically, without thought of its needless complexity." An unaccepted simplification (Strunk's example: "tho" for "though") forces the reader to mentally supply the missing letters — a small tax on attention that the writer pays for with the reader's patience.
  - When to use: whenever tempted by a trendy or simplified spelling, weigh the minor effort saved in writing against the larger effort imposed on every reader.

## Key Concepts
- **Commonly misspelled words**: a reference list including accidentally, believe, benefit, criticize, deceive, definite, develop, disappoint, dissipate, embarrass, existence, fiery, hypocrisy, immediately, incidentally, occurred, parallel, playwright, preceding, prejudice, principal/principle, privilege, pursue, receive-family words, rhythm, separate, similar, and undoubtedly — words whose correct spelling is worth memorizing outright rather than re-deriving each time.
- **Doubling rule**: a single consonant (other than v) preceded by a stressed short vowel doubles before -ed and -ing — planned, letting, beginning. ("Coming" is a named exception.)
- **Hyphenated time words**: to-day, to-night, to-morrow take hyphens; "together" does not follow this pattern.
- **Two-word compounds**: any one, every one, some one, some time are written as two words (except "sometime" in the sense of "formerly").

## Mental Models
- Spelling drift follows a lifecycle: a word may have more than one accepted form at a given moment; over time one form wins general preference and the other comes to look obsolete and gets discarded. New simplified forms introduced by innovators either win acceptance or die of neglect — there's no shortcut to forcing a spelling into general use.

## Anti-patterns
- **Premature spelling simplification**: adopting a not-yet-accepted simplified form (like "thru" or "tho") in ordinary writing — it reads as an affectation or a distraction rather than an efficiency, because the reader still has to do the decoding work.

## Key Takeaways
1. Correct spelling is a matter of current general agreement, not fixed law — but that doesn't make it optional in your own writing today.
2. An unfamiliar or simplified spelling costs the reader attention even when it's theoretically "more efficient" to write.
3. Memorize the specific commonly-misspelled words rather than relying on rules — English spelling has too many exceptions for rules alone.
4. The consonant-doubling rule (stressed short vowel + single final consonant → double before -ed/-ing) is one of the few genuinely reliable spelling rules in English.
