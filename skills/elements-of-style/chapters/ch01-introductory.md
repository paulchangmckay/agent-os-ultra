# Chapter I: Introductory

## Core Idea
Strunk deliberately narrows the field: instead of a score of punctuation rules, he gives three comma rules and one semicolon rule, because those four cover the internal punctuation needed by nineteen sentences out of twenty. The book trades completeness for coverage of what actually goes wrong.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Essentials-first teaching**: concentrate instruction on the small set of rules and principles most commonly violated, rather than an exhaustive rulebook.
  - When to use: any time you're designing a style guide, checklist, or onboarding doc — cover the 20% of mistakes that cause 80% of the damage, not every edge case.
  - How: identify the handful of violations you see repeatedly; state a rule for each; let judgment (not more rules) handle the rest.

## Key Concepts
- **Plain English style**: writing that is direct, concise, and free of ornamental or vague language — the book's implicit standard throughout.
- **Rules of usage**: the mechanical, largely non-negotiable conventions (Chapter II) — punctuation, grammar.
- **Principles of composition**: the higher-level, judgment-based guidance (Chapter III) — paragraph and sentence construction.

## Mental Models
- Treat rules as training wheels: master them until "their application becomes second nature," then use judgment. Strunk says the best writers sometimes break the rules — but only once they're certain of doing better by breaking them.
- Think of "brief space" as a design constraint, not a limitation: a smaller rulebook that's actually followed beats a comprehensive one that isn't.

## Anti-patterns
- **Rule-breaking without mastery**: disregarding the rules of rhetoric before you've earned the judgment to know when the violation pays for itself. Strunk's test: "unless he is certain of doing as well, he will probably do best to follow the rules."

## Key Takeaways
1. Four punctuation rules (three for commas, one for the semicolon) handle nearly all internal punctuation needs.
2. The book covers only a small portion of English style on purpose — depth over breadth for the essentials.
3. Once the essentials are second nature, study the masters of literature for the finer points of style.
