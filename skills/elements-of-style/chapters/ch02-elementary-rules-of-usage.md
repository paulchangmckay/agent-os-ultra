# Chapter II: Elementary Rules of Usage

## Core Idea
Seven mechanical rules — mostly punctuation — that should become automatic. Get these wrong and a reader notices the mistake before the meaning; get them right and punctuation disappears into the sentence.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Rule 1 — Possessive singular with 's**: form the possessive of singular nouns by adding 's, regardless of the final consonant (Charles's friend, Burns's poems).
  - Exceptions: ancient proper names in -es/-is, "Jesus'", "for conscience' sake"; pronominal possessives (hers, its, theirs, yours, oneself) take no apostrophe.
- **Rule 2 — Serial comma**: in a series of three or more terms with one conjunction, put a comma after every term except the last (red, white, and blue).
  - Exception: firm names omit the last comma (Brown, Shipley & Co.).
- **Rule 3 — Enclose parenthetic expressions in commas**: if the interruption is parenthetic, it gets a comma on both sides — never one and not the other.
  - How: test whether the sentence still makes a complete, independent statement with the expression removed. Non-restrictive relative clauses (ones that don't identify their antecedent) are always parenthetic; restrictive ones never are.
- **Rule 4 — Comma before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses**: "The situation is perilous, but there is still one chance of escape."
  - When to use: clauses joined by and, but, for, or, nor, while (meaning "and at the same time"). If the second clause starts with an adverb instead of a conjunction, use a semicolon (Rule 5), not a comma.
- **Rule 5 — Never join independent clauses with just a comma**: use a semicolon, or split into two sentences, or add a conjunction (then use Rule 4's comma).
  - Why it works: "Stevenson's romances are entertaining; they are full of exciting adventures" signals the close relationship between the two statements more forcefully than a period would, and more correctly than a bare comma (a comma splice).
  - Exception: very short, similarly-formed clauses can take a comma ("Man proposes, God disposes").
- **Rule 6 — Don't break sentences in two**: don't use a period where a comma belongs, turning a phrase into a fake sentence.
  - Exception: an emphatic word or phrase may stand alone as a sentence — deliberately, not by accident.
- **Rule 7 — A participial phrase at the start of a sentence must modify the grammatical subject**: "Walking slowly down the road, he saw a woman..." — walking must refer to he, not the woman.
  - Failure mode: dangling modifiers become unintentionally ludicrous ("Being in a dilapidated condition, I was able to buy the house very cheap" — the house, not "I," is dilapidated).

## Key Concepts
- **Parenthetic expression**: a word, phrase, or clause that interrupts the main flow without being essential to the sentence's core meaning.
- **Restrictive vs. non-restrictive clause**: restrictive clauses identify which thing is meant (no commas); non-restrictive clauses add supplementary information about an already-identified thing (always set off by commas).
- **Comma splice**: joining two independent clauses with only a comma — the fault Rule 5 forbids.
- **Loose sentence**: two co-ordinate clauses joined by a conjunction, where the sentence "makes complete sense when the comma is reached" — acceptable in moderation (Rule 4), monotonous in excess (see Ch 3, Rule 14).

## Mental Models
- Use the "independent statement" test for parenthetic material: if you can lift it out and the sentence still stands complete on both sides, it's parenthetic and needs full comma enclosure.
- Use a semicolon, not "and," when you want to show a relationship (cause, consequence) between two complete statements without spelling out what that relationship is — it's a compact tool, not just a weak period.

## Worked Example
Strunk walks through the "audience" sentence to teach restrictive vs. non-restrictive: "The audience, which had at first been indifferent, became more and more interested." Because "which audience" is already known, the clause is supplementary — parenthetic, set off by commas. Contrast: "The candidate who best meets these requirements will obtain the place" — here the clause is essential to identifying which candidate, so no commas. The same logic explains why "Nether Stowey, where Coleridge wrote The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, is a few miles from Bridgewater" takes commas (Nether Stowey is already fully identified by name) while "The day will come when you will admit your mistake" does not (the day is identified only by the clause itself, making it restrictive).

## Anti-patterns
- **Inconsistent parenthetic punctuation**: inserting one comma and omitting the other around a parenthetic phrase — Strunk calls this "indefensible."
- **The comma splice**: "It is nearly half past five, we cannot reach town before dark" — joining two independent clauses with a bare comma instead of a semicolon, period, or conjunction.
- **The fake sentence**: "He was an interesting talker. A man who had traveled all over the world..." — punctuating a fragment as if it were a full sentence, unless the emphasis is deliberate.
- **Dangling participle**: opening a sentence with a participial phrase that doesn't logically attach to the grammatical subject.

## Key Takeaways
1. Four rules (2, 3, 4, 5) cover virtually all internal punctuation of ordinary sentences — master them until they're automatic.
2. Test parenthetic material by removing it: if what remains is a complete, independent statement, enclose the material in commas — both sides, always.
3. A semicolon (not a comma) joins two independent clauses without a conjunction; this is often the strongest option because it implies a relationship without stating it.
4. Never mix one comma and one period/nothing around a parenthetic expression — punctuate both sides the same way.
5. The subject of an opening participial phrase must be the sentence's actual grammatical subject, or the sentence becomes unintentionally comic.
