# Chapter V: Words and Expressions Commonly Misused

## Core Idea
An alphabetical glossary of specific words that go wrong in specific, recurring ways — some are outright bad English, some are merely overused fillers, and some are fine words used in the wrong sense. Strunk's underlying claim: a writer who commits from the start to expressing their own precise thought, rather than reaching for a "ready-made formula," rarely needs this list.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **The recast-don't-patch rule**: when you catch yourself having used one of these words wrongly, don't swap in a synonym — recast the whole sentence. A wrong word choice is usually a symptom of a vague thought, and patching the word leaves the vagueness intact.

## Key Concepts (selected — see glossary.md for the full list)
- **All right**: idiomatic only as a detached phrase ("Agreed"); always two words, never "alright."
- **But / Doubt**: "no doubt but that" is redundant — just "no doubt that."
- **Case / Character / Nature / Factor / Feature / System**: near-interchangeable filler nouns that pad a sentence without adding meaning — "acts of a hostile character" should just be "hostile acts."
- **Claim (verb)**: means "lay claim to"; not a substitute for declare, maintain, or charge.
- **Compare to / Compare with**: "compare to" points out resemblance between essentially different things (life compared to a pilgrimage); "compare with" points out differences between essentially similar things (Congress compared with Parliament).
- **Data**: a plural, like phenomena and strata — "these data were tabulated," not "this data was."
- **Different than**: not permissible; use different from, other than, or unlike.
- **Due to**: correct only as a predicate/modifier of a specific noun ("losses due to fire"), not as a loose adverbial substitute for "because of."
- **Fact**: reserve for matters capable of direct verification (a date, a melting point) — not matters of judgment ("Napoleon was the greatest general" is not a fact).
- **He is a man who...**: a redundant construction; compress to the adjective directly ("He is very ambitious").
- **However**: as "nevertheless," never place it first in its sentence or clause; when it does come first, it means "in whatever way."
- **Kind of / Sort of**: not substitutes for "rather" (before adjectives/verbs) or "something like" (before nouns) outside familiar speech.
- **Less / Fewer**: less refers to quantity, fewer to number — "fewer men," "less courage."
- **Like / As**: like governs nouns and pronouns only; before phrases and clauses, use as ("He thought as I did," not "like I did").
- **Literal(ly)**: reserve for actual fact, not as an intensifier for metaphor — "a literal flood of abuse" is usually wrong.
- **Most**: not a substitute for "almost" ("most everybody" → "almost everybody").
- **One of the most...**: a threadbare, forcible-feeble way to open an essay or paragraph; avoid the formula entirely.
- **Party/Split infinitive**: interposing an adverb between "to" and its infinitive ("to diligently inquire") is in disfavor among careful writers — prefer "to inquire diligently."
- **Shall / Will, Should / Would**: shall/should for first person (belief about future action), will/would for second and third, or to express determination in first person.
- **State**: not a substitute for "say" or "remark"; reserve for expressing something fully and clearly.
- **They (with singular antecedent)**: don't use a plural pronoun for a distributive singular antecedent ("each one," "everybody") — use "he," unless the antecedent must be feminine.
- **Very**: use sparingly; where real emphasis is needed, choose a word that is strong on its own.
- **While**: don't use as a loose substitute for "and," "but," or "although" — reserve it for its literal sense, "during the time that."
- **Whom**: don't substitute for "who" before "he said" or similar insertions when "who" is really the subject of the following verb.

## Mental Models
- For any word on this list, ask: "am I using this in its literal, specific sense, or as vague filler / a borrowed intensifier?" If the latter, cut or replace it.
- Treat overused nouns (case, character, factor, feature, nature, system) as a smell: if a sentence needs one to make sense, the sentence is probably hiding its real subject behind an abstraction — find the concrete noun or verb underneath.

## Worked Example
The "But" entry shows the recast-don't-patch principle in miniature. Original: "America had vast resources, but she seemed almost wholly unprepared for war. But within a year she had created an army of four million men." Two consecutive "but" clauses create a contrast-to-a-contrast, which reads as confused. Corrected by rearrangement: "America seemed almost wholly unprepared for war, but she had vast resources. Within a year she had created an army of four million men." The fix isn't swapping "but" for a synonym — it's reordering the ideas so only one contrast is needed.

## Anti-patterns
- **Filler-noun padding**: reaching for case, character, factor, feature, nature, or system to sound more formal, when a direct noun or verb already says it.
- **Borrowed intensifiers**: "literally," "very," "certainly" used reflexively to intensify any statement, regardless of whether real emphasis is warranted.
- **Formula openings**: starting a paragraph or essay with "one of the most..." or "it is interesting to note that..." — announcing significance instead of demonstrating it.

## Key Takeaways
1. Most of these words aren't wrong in themselves — they're wrong in the specific sense or position the writer reaches for out of habit.
2. When you catch a misuse, recast the sentence; don't just swap in a better word and leave the underlying vagueness.
3. Filler nouns (case, character, factor, feature, nature, system) are a reliable signal that the sentence's real subject hasn't been found yet.
4. Reserve intensifiers (very, literally, certainly) for when the emphasis is actually earned — overuse cancels their force.
